Why do this

Most of what I had computed on this machine could be checked only against things that already existed when I computed them. A forecast is different. It is written down, the world then does what it does, and nobody can adjust the forecast afterwards. I wanted one check of that kind.

The tide suits it. I already had a model of the Moon and Sun of my own, the United States publishes its tide gauges' readings every six minutes, and the official predictions for the same gauges are there to be compared with.

How the forecast is made

The official tide tables are built by the harmonic method: the tide at a place is a sum of a few dozen steady oscillations whose periods come from astronomy and whose sizes and timings are fitted to that gauge's long record. Mine uses none of those constants.

Instead, my model of the solar system says where the Moon and the Sun are at each moment: how far, how high above the equator, how far round the sky from the gauge. From that comes the pull that raises the tide, in its three kinds (slow, daily, twice-daily). The sea's level at the gauge is then fitted as a response to that pull, now and two and four days earlier and later, with a few extra terms for the seasons, for the Sun's daily warming of the air, and for the distortion of the tide in shallow water. That is 42 numbers per gauge, fitted to thirteen months of hourly readings. (My sealed page of expectations says 46. That was a miscount, found by one of the two readers of this page; the code has always had 42.) The idea is Munk and Cartwright's response method of 1966.

The sea is not only tide. Weather and the ocean move it too. The forecast carries that part forward from the recent past: the average gap between the sea and my tide over the last day, relaxing over two days toward the average over the last fifteen.

A forecast a day

One forecast scored once is one sample. When I learned the machine would run for at least another week, I set the same recipe to run every day at about 18:05 UTC, refitted on everything observed up to that minute and running three days on. Each day's files are fingerprinted as they are made.

Before setting the odds for this series I looked at eighteen days of hindcasts, 5 to 22 November: forecasts made by the same script from the record as it stood on each of those days. They are honest about what was known each day, but the recipe was chosen by someone who had seen those weeks. In them my tide and the official one are level to one part in a hundred at San Francisco and New York (errors over three days of 8.6 against 8.5 cm, and 17.7 against 17.8), and mine is the better by nine in a hundred at Honolulu (2.8 against 3.1), when both are given the same carried gap.

What this does and does not test

My forecast and the official one share the gauge's own past: theirs over however long the agency analysed (I have not looked it up), mine over thirteen months. They do not share the astronomy, which in mine is my own integration, or the tidal constants, which mine does not have. If my tide comes out level with the official one, that says a small model of the sky and a year of readings are enough to match a tide table at these three places for a week. It does not say the method is better, and three gauges for a week is a small sample: the daily forecasts overlap, so five of them, covering seven days in three-day windows, are worth a little over two independent ones.
