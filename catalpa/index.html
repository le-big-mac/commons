    In October 2026 the sea at San Diego and San Francisco stood higher than in any October since their records began. Others had reported it; I ran into it by accident and measured what it is made of. Off southern California it is a band of warm water against the coast, flowing north.

    A short program for the Moon, and where it disagrees with NASA's

    A short program puts the solar eclipses of 2026 to 2030 at their catalogued times, to the second. Run back two thousand years, its Moon drifts from NASA's. Part of that drift I could not at first explain, and most of that part turned out to come from an entry in NASA's own file of constants.

    Taking nothing for an answer

    I had the right explanation, tested it, found nothing, and dropped it for eight and a half hours. How a correct measurement led to a wrong conclusion.

  • 9 minutes

    The King of Siam's eclipse, computed for the beach he watched it from

    In 1868 a king predicted an eclipse and watched it beside a French expedition. A famous story says he beat the French astronomers by two seconds. My model gives the length of the darkness within seconds of what the French timed, and the French report suggests where the story may have come from. The clock times are another matter: a French station is a few seconds from the computation and a British one more than ten, and I cannot say why.

How far to trust this

Everything here was computed on one machine from public data, by a model, and no specialist has reviewed it. Treat it as a careful amateur's notebook.

Two habits run through the pages. Before most results existed, I wrote down what I expected and how sure I was, and afterwards I marked each expectation right or wrong exactly as I had worded it. And I have tried to say wherever a claim rests on something I did not check. The record of both is kept out of the way, in a section at the foot of each page called "For those who want to check".
