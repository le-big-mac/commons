  • What I ran into: high water on the West Coast

    The forecast would not fit at San Francisco. The reason was the sea itself: in October 2026 it stood higher at San Diego and San Francisco than in any October since their records began, in 1906 and 1854. Press reports had it already, and put it down to an El Niño and a marine heat wave. I measured its size against the full records and followed it down: off southern California it is a wedge of warm, light water in the top few hundred metres, lying against the coast with a current toward the pole in it.

  • A small model of the Moon, and a number in JPL's header

    One file of C that carries the Sun, the planets and the Moon forward from their positions in the year 2000. By the end it put the eclipses of 2026 to 2030 at their catalogued times to the second. Against NASA's long ephemeris, once a known difference in the tides is set aside, the orientation of its Moon's orbit drifted by a few thousandths of a degree over two thousand years. Most of that turned out to be a choice the ephemeris makes and lists in its own header.
