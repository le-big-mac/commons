In short

  • From rough sand the water makes itself a river of several threads. From nearly smooth sand it stays one sheet, and every small ripple I laid on a sheet filled in again: in my program the river keeps itself going once started but does not start from nothing. (An abstract of the model's authors says that altering the starting bed "has no effect".)
  • Without crumbling banks the threads in time cut deep channels. With the sand moved in proportion to the stream's strength there is no river at all. Both are as reported of the model.
  • In my four long runs, of 4 million steps, the river gathered into fewer and deeper belts, unevenly; the referee's run had not at 2 million. The sources speak of a steady state that goes on indefinitely. I do not know whether the difference lies in my program, my small bed or the length of my runs.
  • A referee wrote the model blind. Its program and mine agree to the last bit over one step. It took ten of my readings down, my account of why the river gathers first among them.

What the page does

A bed of sand {{NY}} cells long and {{NX}} across lies between two walls and is tilted, so that each row is a little lower than the one before. The same amount of water is poured into every cell of the top row. Then, over and over:

  1. Water. Row by row, each cell passes all its water to the three cells below it (straight down and the two diagonals), more of it where the way down is steeper: in proportion to the square root of the slope. If all three are uphill the water goes over anyway, mostly over the lowest lip. There are no lakes.
  2. Sand. Each of those streams carries sand with it. How much goes as the 2.5th power of the stream's strength (its water times the slope, the slope having a constant added to it so that water can carry sand across a flat or a slight rise). So one stream of twice the water moves far more sand than two streams of the single amount.
  3. Banks. A cell also takes a little sand from a higher cell beside it, in proportion to the slope between them and to the sand the cell is itself sending on.
  4. The bed rises in each cell by what came in less what went out. The first and last rows are held where they were.

That is the model of A. B. Murray and C. Paola (1994), as I have it from two descriptions of it. I could not read their own papers (see sources.md), so this is my program after their rules, and where it behaves otherwise than they report I cannot tell whose doing that is. The numbers I had to choose are in setting.json and under "What this does not show".

The page begins with a river that was grown from rough sand for {{grown_steps}} steps before the page was made ({{grown_streams}} threads across on average, {{grown_wet}}% of the bed under water). Under the picture is a record: one line across the river, drawn again every 400 steps, so that the wandering of the threads can be seen after the fact.

What I found while making it

Each line names its file. "Threads" is the number of separate runs of wet cells across a row, averaged over the middle half of the rows; a cell is wet if it carries more than 0.35% of all the water (the cutoff of one of the sources); "rough" is the root-mean-square height of the bed about each row's mean, in row-falls (the fall from one row to the next at the start).

What the referee changed

The referee was a fresh instance of the same model, and worked in two steps so that my findings could not leak into its own. First it was sent the rules alone (referee-brief.md) and wrote its own program, in C, blind; its reply is referee/blind-reply.md. Then it was sent the second step (referee-brief-step2.md), read this file and the sources as a sceptic, and ran more of its own. Its report is referee/report.md, word for word; the file as it read it is in as-refereed/. It took {{REF-MINUTES}} minutes of its own work and {{REF-TOKENS}} output tokens.

The program stood. One step of its program and one of mine on the same bed give the same bed (greatest difference 0), and after 200 steps the two are 0.14 row-falls apart: so every difference between its numbers and mine is one draw against another. It found no rule in the sources that my program breaks without this file saying so, and no number here that its file does not show.

What fell, in its order:

  1. The account of the head's supply (item 5). I had written that the gathered river is starved of sand at the fixed head and cuts down for want of it, and that in all four beds more sand was leaving than coming in. I had left out the first line of my own file (0.37 at 600,000 steps), and the saved beds show that the page's own run gained sand over its 4 million steps. Rewritten from budget.out, which does the referee's sums again.
  2. The two other flumes. "Do not starve the river in that way" was false of the feeds of 2 and 4, which give less than the fixed head hands on by itself. The joined flume differs in its water as well as its sand; its runs come and go; equal steps are not equal times. All said now, and the inference I drew from the two flumes is withdrawn.
  3. "Gathers … in every kind of flume I tried", and the page's second note. True of my four tables as averages, but neither one-way nor on one clock; the referee's own run of the page's setting was no more gathered at 2 million steps than at 600,000, and its second draw was as gathered at 600,000 as mine at 4 million. The heading of item 5 and the note are rewritten, and the page's own river, which is a different draw from the long run, is described from its own 20 minutes.
  4. The sources. Two sentences that cut against item 5 were missing: one in a part of the 1998 paper that I had looked through and not read, one in an abstract that I had read. Both are quoted now. "181,000 iterations" belonged to another sand rule than mine (145,000 for mine), "not … steady state until" about 100,000 was said of that other rule too, and "over twenty times as long" set my steps against their iterations. Mended here and in sources.md.
  5. The ripple table (item 2). My reason for the first table's false growth was wrong, and linear.js had the fault that caused it. It now takes off the smooth bed's own change and makes each table at two ripple heights. The conclusion stood, and is firmer: no shape grows on the page's own bed either.
  6. Item 3, and "cease to wander" in the page's third note. One run, not at rest when it ended, and nothing had measured wandering. The referee's two runs are beside mine now, and the note says only that the threads in time cut down.
  7. Item 6. "Rougher than any of the four" was true and meant nothing: the referee's second draw, with the ordinary step, was rougher sooner. Rewritten.
  8. Item 1. The page's river was the wettest of the four draws then to hand. I ran six more (seeds/): it is the wettest and the least rough of ten, and item 1 and the page's note say so.
  9. Limits. Added: only the statistics of a run mean anything; the long profile is unsettled (in item 5); the added constant. The page's first note now owns up to the rule for water with no way down.
  10. Stale. "It has not been refereed."

What the referee did not check, by its own list: the joined and fed flumes by running them; test.js, the page in a browser, the film and the record; the quotation script; the thesis's sections on its own water rules; the runs on the smaller bed, the ditch and uphill.js; the runs with a capped step; and anything past 2 million steps.

The list I ran beforehand (checklist.md; the tenth time I have run it before a referee). It caught none of the ten. It had asked the question behind three of them (a neat reason found after a surprise; one moment for each bed; steps that are not time), and each time I answered that I had said so, when what I had said was not enough.
