What I read about the model, and what each says

Fetched on 23 November 2026 into ~/braid-data/ (one folder each; not published here) and read as plain text. The quotations are checked against the saved texts by quotes.py (quotes.out). The model is A. B. Murray and C. Paola's. I have not read either of their two papers on it: both are behind publishers' paywalls, which I left alone. What is below is two descriptions of the model by others (one of them with its authors among the writers) and the two papers' abstracts.

  1. J. R. Cudden, An investigation of braided river dynamics using a new numerical modelling approach, PhD thesis, University of Glasgow, 2002 (theses.gla.ac.uk/5421). Read: section 2.5.6.1 with Table 2.4 and the caption of Figure 2.4 (her account of Murray and Paola's model), sections 3.8.1 and 3.8.2 (the water rules of her own model), and the part of section 5.4.4 on sideways transport. The rest of the thesis is about her own model and I did not read it. From 2.5.6.1: "Most runs start with uniform slope and white-noise elevation perturbations are placed on the grid to give a random initial topography." (She does not say how large the perturbations are.) Water goes from each cell to "one or more of the three downstream neighbouring cells"; "The model does not allow the formation of lakes and all water entering a cell leaves it during that iteration." Table 2.4 gives six sand rules; the one I use is her rule 3, the sand sent to a downstream neighbour being K (Q (S + Cs))^m, with K "a constant adjusted so that the elevation changes by at most a few percent of the mean elevation difference between rows in each time step", Cs "the order of the average slope" and m "usually 2.5". Sideways, a cell takes sand from a higher neighbour in its row in proportion to the slope between them and to the sand the cell is itself sending on, with a constant such that this is a few percent of the latter ("the percentage is not explicitly quoted"). "In most runs the elevation of the first and last rows of cells are fixed". And two findings she reports from the model's authors: "Experiments with the model show that if the sediment flux is linear, braiding does not develop."; and, of the sideways rule, "Without this rule the model eventually digs itself a canyon that confines the flow, thus reaching a static steady state." - The rule for water with no way down. Her section on Murray and Paola's model gives the rule only for cells with a downhill neighbour (shares as slope to the power n = 0.5). For a cell whose three downstream neighbours are all higher I had the rule from memory: shares as |slope| to the power minus n. Her own model has that same rule (her "Q rule 2", section 3.8.2), and she writes of her two rules together that, following Murray and Paola, "Q rule 1 (and Q rule 2 below) can be derived from the equation of motion for uniform flow in a wide channel". That is support for my memory, not proof that the original used it.
  2. V. B. Sapozhnikov, A. B. Murray, C. Paola and E. Foufoula-Georgiou, "Validation of braided-stream models: spatial state-space plots, self-affine scaling, and island shapes", Water Resources Research 34 (1998), 2353–2364; an open copy on one of the authors' university pages. Read: sections 1 and 2 (the summary of the model) and section 3.3 with the caption of Figure 5; the rest (the comparisons with real rivers) I looked through but did not study. It gives the same sand rule and says the exponent is 2.5 and that the results "do not depend sensitively" on it. Of the runs: the beds are 46 by 1000 and 100 by 1500 cells, snapshots are taken after 20,000 to 181,000 iterations, and in one set "discharge is introduced in each cell in the first row, rather than in one fourth of the cells as in all the other model runs we discuss." Some patterns "appear not to reach steady state until" about 100,000 iterations. "Widths in the model are the number of cells in a cross section with discharge greater than a cutoff, which is 0.35% of the total discharge in these runs." (I took that cutoff for what counts as water on the page.) - Two sentences that bear on what I found. The model "is aimed not at predicting the conditions of formation of initial instabilities that might lead to braiding, but at the properties of the fully developed braided state." And, after describing how gathering water scours and spreading water drops its sand: "The slope dependence of the stream power tends to oppose these instabilities."
  3. The abstracts of the two original papers, as given on a university's publications pages: A. B. Murray and C. Paola, "A cellular model of braided rivers", Nature 371 (1994), 54–57; and "Properties of a cellular braided-stream model", Earth Surface Processes and Landforms 22 (1997), 1001–1025. Abstracts only. 1994: "A broad sheet of water flowing over non-cohesive sediment typically breaks up into a network of interconnected channels called a braided stream"; "The patterns arise from local scour and deposition caused by a nonlinear dependence of bedload sediment flux on water discharge." 1997: "The most crucial parameter choice is the use of a non-linear exponent (>1) to describe the relation between sediment flux and local stream power."; sideways transport "is not crucial to produce braiding, but is needed to provide reasonable lateral channel shifting, and to maintain a continuing dynamic behaviour." - A sentence that cuts against something I found. 1997: "As long as lateral sediment transport is included, altering the initial topography for a run has no effect, other than a transient period of regrading." In my program a nearly smooth bed under water from wall to wall stays one sheet for as long as I ran it (see the README). I do not know which starting beds the authors tried, nor whether my program differs from theirs in some way I cannot see from two descriptions.

Things I could not reach: the two papers themselves; a third description (by a search tool's account, a 2005 paper testing the model against a laboratory river), whose repository page no longer exists; and C. Paola's "Modelling stream braiding over a range of scales" (2001), which the thesis gives as its source for the steady state and the canyon, and which sits in a university repository that refused my request. I left it at that.
