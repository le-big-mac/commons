The isolated state

A map of J. H. von Thünen's rings of farming round a town, drawn from the formulas in his own book. Not a study: a page to play with, tests of my program against the book's printed figures, and notes. Nothing was sealed. A fresh instance read the book blind and then read this file as a sceptic; what it took down is under "What the referee changed".

In short

  • Thünen asked what would be grown where round one great town on a level plain with nothing but carts, and answered with numbers from his own estate's accounts: rings of gardens, forest, careful grain farming, cheap grain farming and cattle. The two grain rings end, by his reckoning, at 24.7 and 31.5 miles; the gardens, the forest and the cattle reach as far as he supposes the town to need them, 4, 7 and 50 miles. In section 28 he says a real country could be mapped the same way, each place coloured by the ring it belongs to, and that such a map "would give a most interesting and instructive survey". The page draws it: rivers and soils can be changed, and the rings follow.
  • The program is three formulas from the book. Of 76 figures the book prints, in fourteen tables and worked cases, the program gives 43 exactly as printed and all but two of the rest within the margins listed below (figures.out, test.out). The two it does not give are a radius of 2.2 miles on the poorest soil and a limit of 23.5 miles in section 24.
  • Where the page goes beyond the book it says so. The largest such thing is the third ring, for which the book has no rule: the page takes its width from the book's plates.
  • With a river through the town the page shows what the book says of its second plate: the third ring runs along the river to the border, and the stock ring is gone beside it. Three things on that plate it does not show.

How a place gets its ring

  1. Miles of carting. With no river, the crow's distance to the town. With rivers, the cost of the cheapest way, over land, or over land to the water and along it, reckoned in miles of land carriage, a mile by water counting as a tenth of one by land (section 28; the page has a slider for the tenth).
  2. What rye is worth there. The book's formula for a farm x miles out, when rye sells at a Taler in the town: ((12000 − 150x)·a − 136.92x)/(12000 + 65.88x). At 1½ Taler in the town rye is worth 1.31 at 5 miles, 0.81 at 20, and nothing at 50, where the horses and men would eat the load.
  3. What the land would then pay under his two systems of grain farming, for 100,000 square rods, on soil that yields y Körner: Koppelwirtschaft (1710 − 271(10 − y)) Scheffel of rye less (747 − 53(10 − y)) Taler; three fields (1000 − 152(10 − y)) Scheffel less (381 − 27(10 − y)) Taler.
  4. The ring. The first two by distance alone (gardens and milk to 4 miles, forest to 7, in the standard state). Then Koppelwirtschaft where it pays, and pays more than three fields; the innermost part of that is the third ring (see below); three fields where that pays more; stock farming where neither pays, to 50 miles; then wilderness.

The chart under the map is step 3 drawn out: the two rents against miles of carting, crossing at 24.7 miles, the second reaching nothing at 31.5.

The town's price is not a free choice in the book. Section 24: the town must pay what the farthest farm it still needs requires, so the price follows from how much the town eats. The page's slider for the price does what the book's third plate does and supposes it changed, as by a tax on grain.

Where the page goes beyond the book

  1. The third ring (Fruchtwechselwirtschaft). The book says this system "finds no place in the isolated state": it is displaced by the conditions supposed there, chiefly by soil that is not rich. He marks the ring only for the place it would take under other assumptions, and calls its extent on the plates "assumed". The text gives two figures, both for soil of 10 Körner: 9.4 miles wide at 1½ Taler, and gone at 0.9 Taler. The page takes the ring's width beyond the forest from the plates. On plate III the referee measured it at eight prices between those two and found it shrinking in a straight line with the price; on plate I (8 Körner) I measured it 2.9 miles wide (measure-plate.out); on plate IV the referee found 0.6 miles at 7.47 Körner and none at 7.33. The page joins those points with straight lines and multiplies the effect of price by the effect of soil. The joining and the multiplying are mine. My first rule was different (the ring wherever rye was worth 0.90 Taler or more); it met the text's two figures and missed plate III between them, which is how the referee took it down. The book also says that what would move this ring's boundary most is the price of animal products, a matter it leaves to its second part. Nothing of that is in the page.
  2. The sizes of the first, second and last rings when the price or the soil is changed. The book says the first two stand "in direct proportion to the size of the town, and so to that of the cultivated plain". The page scales their radii (4 and 7 miles) with the radius of the plain, and scales the stock ring's 50 miles the same way, which the book does not say. By the book's own widths for plate III its forest ring at 10 Körner ends at 34.7 − 4.5 − 21.4 = 8.8 miles, and the referee measured 8.4 to 8.8 on the plates, about a quarter of the plain's radius; the page's ends at 7.7, two ninths. Scaling everything by the grain plain also makes the whole state shrink to nothing as the soil falls toward 4.4 Körner, where plate IV still shows a ring two or three miles across: so the page's slider for the soil stops at 5 Körner. Nor does the page let the town grow when a river brings it more land.
  3. Water. The book states its rule for "the worth of the grain on the farm and what follows from it". The page applies it to every ring, the first two included, though gardens, milk and firewood are not grain. And it finds the cheapest way exactly: a cart may slant toward the river, which makes the farm 5 miles from the river and 100 down it 14.97 miles from the town, not the book's 15.
  4. Patches of soil. For a place whose soil differs from the country's, the page reckons the rents and the third ring's width from the place's own yield and the first two rings from the country's. The book's two examples come out as it says: sand 10 miles from the town falls to the sixth ring, and rich land 100 miles down a river belongs to the third.

What is on his plates and not on the page

  • On plate II the first ring stops. The dotted ring of gardens runs down the river some nine or ten miles and ends (shots/plate2-ruler.png). On the page it runs on, narrowing, for forty (river-widths.out), because I gave it the same river rule as grain. The plate's forest band is about two miles wide on each bank all the way down; the page's lies between 2 and 5 miles from the bank at twenty miles down, behind the gardens.
  • On plate II the third ring "widens immensely". The referee measured it about 4 miles wide beside the river and 12 to 15 miles across below the town. On the page it keeps the 2.9 miles it has on the plain.
  • The small town and its territory (the unshaded strip on plate II). The book says the small town must pay each grower what his rye is worth on his own farm, and that the land feeding it sends nothing to the capital. I could not derive the strip from the book's cost of carting, because by that formula two hauls of 20 miles cost far less than one of 40 (rye worth 0.340 Taler after two, 0.241 after one): the wagon on the long haul carries feed for the whole journey. So I left the small town out.
  • Made roads ("Kunststraßen") have "a like effect, though smaller"; the book gives no figure. The water slider, set to a half, will serve for one.

What this does not show

  • It is the second edition, in a reprint, through a machine's reading. The text is the Internet Archive's scan of the 1910 Jena edition, which reprints the edition of 1842. I have not seen the first edition of 1826 and do not know what in sections 1 to 28, or which plates, it already had. Where the machine reading was doubtful I looked at the page image; sources.md lists six places where the scan or the book disagrees with itself.
  • I read about a tenth of the first part (sources.md says which sections). In particular I did not read the long reckonings behind the two rent formulas (sections 5 to 13), nor the sections on forestry and stock farming beyond the passages that give their limits, nor two tables of section 14 that are printed sideways and that the machine could not read.
  • The mile is a German mile; the book's page of measures does not say how long, and I did not look it up.
  • Measurements off the plates. Plate I by measure-plate.py (darkness averaged round half-circles; the scale from the plate's own bar, 20.0 pixels to the mile): boundaries at 4.1, 7.5 and 10.4 miles round the town's mark, and at 24.0, 30.7 and 39.1 miles round a centre a mile above it, where the text has 24.7, 31.5 and (as drawn) 40. So the plate's first two rings are larger than the text's 4 and 7, and its outer rings two or three parts in a hundred smaller than its own bar makes them. Plates III and IV were measured by the referee, not by me, to about half a mile (referee/). Where the gardens stop on plate II is by my eye against a ruler of pixels. All of it is a scan of a 1910 redrawing.
  • Nothing here is about real farming. The page shows what one book's arithmetic implies. Whether land round real towns was ever so arranged is another question, which I did not look into.
