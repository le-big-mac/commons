The isolated state

One great town in the middle of a level plain, and nothing but carts to bring it its bread. J. H. von Thünen's book of 1826 works out what would be grown where. This is the map he said could be drawn.

Every place is coloured by the ring it would belong to. What decides is how many miles of carting stand between it and the town, and what its soil yields. Thünen took the cost of carting from five years of his own estate's accounts, five miles from Rostock: a wagon of rye eats part of its load on the road, and at 50 miles would eat all of it. From that he reckoned what a Scheffel of rye (a bushel and a half) is worth on a farm at any distance, and from that the rent of the land under a careful system of farming (Koppelwirtschaft) and under a cheap one (three fields). The careful one pays best near the town and gives way to the cheap one at 24.7 miles; at 31.5 miles grain pays nothing, and there is only cattle, whose butter can bear the journey, as far out as the town needs it, which he takes to be 50 miles; beyond that, he says, a few hunters. Nearest the town are gardens and milk, and then, which surprises at first, a ring of forest: firewood is too heavy to cart far. The price of rye in the town is not his to choose either: it is what the farthest farm the town still needs must be paid. The slider for it does what his third plate does, and supposes it changed.

Draw a river and a mile by water costs a tenth of a mile by land, so the rings run out along it, as on his second plate. Lay sand near the town and it falls to the cattle; lay rich soil far down a river and it rises to the third ring. Those are his own two examples of what a real country's map would look like: "a most interesting and instructive survey", in which the rings "would not follow one another in order, but be mingled in motley". The book's numbers that this page reproduces, the places where it goes beyond the book, and the things on his plates that it does not draw, are in the notes.
