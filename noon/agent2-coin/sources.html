1. Shallit (2003)

J. Shallit, "What this country needs is an 18¢ piece", Mathematical Intelligencer 25(2) (2003), 20-23; I read his own draft of 8 November 2002 (cs.uwaterloo.ca/~shallit/Papers/change2.pdf), whole. The model, the figures of my sealed lines 1 to 5, Pearson's test, and the counting argument for a lower bound are his.

"We assume that every amount of change between 0¢ and 99¢ is equally likely."

"The unique answer is 32¢; this improves cost(100; 1, 5, 10, 25) = 4.7 to cost(100; 1, 5, 10, 25, 32) = 3.46."

"Unfortunately, none of the optimal sets of denominations in Figure 1 for D ≥ 3 give optimal representations when used greedily."

"Since Pearson's result appeared only in an obscure technical report, we give a few details."

"I don't know the computational complexity of this problem, but it seems quite hard."

He was not the first to the tables, and says so:

"Erik Demaine kindly pointed out that similar results were posted to the Usenet newsgroup sci.math by Jeffry Johnston and Bill Kinnersley in April 2000."

His lower bound (section 4) counts the lists of D coin-counts that add up to at most k, for large ranges. My "counting bound" one way is that argument made exact for a given range; the both-ways bound is the same argument with signs, and is mine.

3. Kippers and Franses (2003), and Kippers, van Nierop, Paap and Franses (2003)

J. Kippers and P. H. Franses, "Do we need all euro denominations?", Econometric Institute Report 2003-39 (repub.eur.nl/pub/1048); read whole. Tables 1 to 3 are what my finding 2 recounts.

"If individuals would behave according to this principle, each amount would be paid such that the number of notes and coins exchanged is minimized."

"An illustration is the amount of € 11.30 that can be efficiently paid along three different ways, that is (i) 10 + 1 + 0.20 + 0.10, (ii) 10 + 1 + 0.50 and 0.20 returned and (iii) 10 + 2 and 0.50 + 0.20 returned."

"In this paper we use it to generate efficient payment schemes for all amounts between NLG 0.05 and NLG 220.35 for the guilder range."

And, seven lines below it, of the same table (the referee pointed this out; I had quoted only the first):

"Table 1 shows the relevant statistics for all payment schemes for the guilder and euro range for NLG 0.05 up to and including NLG 100."

Table 3 is not of notes alone:

"The starting point for our calculations is the complete euro banknote range complemented by a 1-euro coin."

"For example, in the full range, amounts can be paid efficiently with up to a maximum of 18 different payment schemes, while this maximum decreases to 10 or less if a single denomination is left out of the range."

The sentence that cuts against this study:

"In practice, it is unlikely that all actual payments are efficient because individuals might not behave according to the ‘principle of least effort’, nor will they all have the necessary denominations in their wallet."

J. Kippers, E. van Nierop, R. Paap and P. H. Franses, "An empirical study of cash payments", Statistica Neerlandica 57 (2003), 484-508 (repub.eur.nl/pub/2041). Before the referee I had read the section on efficient payments and the appendix that sets out Cramer's algorithm, and not the introduction. The introduction holds the sentences that say whose question this is (section 6 below). My definition of "both ways" is theirs:

"A positive n(A,d) refers to use as a payment, while a negative n(A,d) means that the n tokens of denomination d are given as change."

Their example, which my sealed line 8 says is one short:

"The amount NLG 17.50 has three efficient payment schemes in which three tokens are used."

"Any other combination will need more than three tokens to make up the amount NLG 17.50."

The three they list are 10 + 5 + 2.50; two 10s with 2.50 as change; 25 with 5 and 2.50 as change. The fourth, 25 and 2.50 with 10 as change, also sums to 17.50 with three tokens. By their own algorithm as the appendix describes it (add one token at a time, with either sign, to combinations already found efficient) it would be found: 25 with 10 back is an efficient pair for 15.

"Cramer develops an algorithm for determining all efficient payment schemes for a range of payment amounts and discovered that no Dutch amount up to NLG 100.00 would need more than seven tokens to make the payment and that the amount NLG 16.65 had the most efficient schemes, that is, ten in total."

Seven is what I find too. Ten is what I find for 16.65; but 66.65 has fifteen. This is a report of Cramer (1983), Economics Letters 12, 299-303, which I could not read; the fault, if it is one, may be in the report, in Cramer, or in my reading of which guilder notes and coins he used.

J. Kippers and P. H. Franses, "An empirical analysis of euro cash payments", Econometric Institute Report EI 2003-25 (repub.eur.nl/pub/748). I had fetched this and only read it when my own list of questions asked whether I had read what I fetched. Its pages 1 to 9 I read; the statistical model after them I did not.

"First, 61% of the payments were done efficiently."

What "efficiently" means there is narrower than my count, by these two sentences:

"If this scheme coincides with the one calculated by Cramer for the same amount, we consider the payment to be efficient, irrelevant of the change given back by the cashier."

"We therefore expand the pure efficient payment schemes by the schemes that are efficient, given the restrictions of the wallet."

And on what coinages are thought best, a sentence I cannot check, since I have not read the work it cites:

"It is generally accepted that a 1-2-5 range, like the euro has, is theoretically the denominational structure that approaches the optimal one for a payment system, see also Boeschoten and Fase (1989)."

5. Read in part, or fetched and not used

A. J. Young, "Completely greedy coin sets", arXiv:2407.19137 (2024); pages 1 to 4. From it I took the bound that a least failure of the greedy way lies below the sum of the two largest coins, which it gives to Kozen and Zaks (1994); firstFailure() uses it as a witness for Pearson's test, and a test tries three times as far.

"All cardinality 2 coin sets are greedy and if C = {c_1, ..., c_n} is not greedy the least violator is in (c_3 + 1, c_{n−1} + c_n) [4]."

"For N = 99 this is f*_99 = 7 achieved by {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64} using 3.19 coins on average."

"The optimal, unrestricted, coin set of cardinality 7 uses 2.68 coins on average [2]."

(Those two are averages over 1 to 99: 316/99 and 265/99. Mine are over 0 to 99, as Shallit's: 3.16 and 2.65.)

6. Whose question it is

The referee found these in the introduction of Kippers and others (2003), which I had on disk and had not read. Two schools:

"According to this approach, the optimal range of denominations of notes and coins has the smallest number of denominations with which one can pay any amount in between the smallest transaction and a certain upper bound, see TELSER (1995) and WYNNE (1997) for a discussion of this approach."

"That is, the optimal range of denominations allows the number of tokens exchanged in cash transactions to be minimized, on average, see CAIANIELLO et al. (1982), SUMNER (1993) and HOVE and HEYNDELS (1996), among others."

"The two different views on how to derive the optimal currency system has led to the result that denominations should be spaced apart by a factor of either two or three, but no firm conclusion has been reached."

From the catalogue pages of three papers I could not read (ideas.repec.org), their abstracts:

"One of the most important results of theoretical research on currency systems is that spacing denominations apart by a factor of two is better than a factor of three as this lowers the average number of notes and coins exchanged in transactions."

(Y. Bouhdaoui, D. Bounie and L. Van Hove, "Central banks and their banknote series: the efficiency-cost trade-off", Economic Modelling 28 (2011), 1482-1488.)

"L. G. Telser (1995) and Scott Sumner (1993) argue that the optimal system of denominations of currency would consist of a sequence of denominations that differed from each other by a factor of three."

(A. E. Tschoegl, Journal of Money, Credit and Banking 29 (1997), 546-554. Kippers and others put Sumner with the least-effort school; this abstract puts him with a factor of three. Both may be so. I have not read him.)

"It also argues that finding the optimal denominational structure is a multicriteria optimization problem, in which the principle of least effort should be given the greatest weight."

(L. Van Hove, 2001.) What I take from these: the question "which denominations let the fewest tokens change hands on average" is theirs, asked of regular series. Whether any of them searched every set of coins for a range of amounts, I do not know. The referee also looked at an essay (ericguan.substack.com/p/ optimal-currency-denominations) and arXiv:1306.2060 and reports both as one way only; the second, by its abstract, is about the coins in a person's pocket under spending strategies.
