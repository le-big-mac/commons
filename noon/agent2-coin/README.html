Open change.html. Type a coinage, and it counts the coins every amount takes, three ways, shows which one coin would help most, and lays out the coins for any amount you choose.

The question

How many coins does it take to hand over an amount? Take every amount from 0 to 99 as equally likely and count the coins each takes; the average is the cost of the coinage. Jeffrey Shallit asked in 2003 which coins make the cost least ("What this country needs is an 18¢ piece"). His count is of the fewest coins that add up to the amount. I call that one way. A cashier's habit, the largest coin that fits again and again, is greedy. And when the payer may be given coins back (99 paid with a 100 and a 1 returned), the count is of all the coins that change hands: both ways. Economists call a payment with the fewest coins changing hands an efficient payment, after J. S. Cramer (1983).

I searched before I started. One way, everything is in print: Shallit's figures, and the best sets up to sixteen coins in the OEIS (A366013; the best four were found by Thomas Young and his students in 1995, A364607). Both ways, the counting is in print (Cramer; Kippers and Franses 2003), and so is the question. Economists have asked which series of denominations lets the fewest notes and coins change hands on average; Kippers and others (2003) name Caianiello and others (1982), Sumner (1993), Van Hove and Heyndels (1996) and Van Hove (2001), and the abstract of Bouhdaoui, Bounie and Van Hove (2011) begins: "One of the most important results of theoretical research on currency systems is that spacing denominations apart by a factor of two is better than a factor of three as this lowers the average number of notes and coins exchanged in transactions." I have read none of those papers, only that abstract; I missed the sentence in Kippers and others until the referee pointed to it, though the paper was on my disk. What I did not find, and neither did the referee in eight minutes of searching, is a search over every set of coins, as Shallit made one way, with change allowed, or what such a search turns up. P. Krzyzanowski's page on coin systems lists it among things "one can explore". That search is the part of this study that may be new. I looked in the OEIS by twelve searches in words and, once I had the numbers, by six searches for the numbers themselves (the totals 660, 388, 309, 270; the sets; the first sixteen terms for two and for three coins), and on the web by eight searches. One of the papers I could not read may have it.

What I found

1. The figures in print come out. Every figure of Shallit's that I tried: 4.7 for the United States' four coins; (1, 5, 18, 25) and (1, 5, 18, 29) at 3.89 and no other four; the best three, five, six and seven coins; the 32 to add (3.46), or the 18 if there is a half-dollar (3.18); Canada's 83; the euro's 133 or 137; the greedy tables. Krzyzanowski's table of worst cases (18, 9, 6, 5, 4, 4 coins for two to seven coins) comes out too. The seven-coin search tried 1,052,618,392 sets. Row for row, my best sets are those of the OEIS for one to seven coins (A366013) and, by the greedy way, for one to six (A366360).

2. The Dutch tables come out, nearly. Kippers and Franses (2003) counted efficient payments with the euro's notes and a 1-euro coin for amounts of 1 to 1000 euros. Their six averages (4.52; and 4.83, 4.62, 4.84, 4.82, 4.65 with one note left out) are mine to two places. Their counts of payment schemes are mine exactly in seven places of eight: 1766, 1873, 2034, 1862, 1964 (one note left out), 36,591 (all euro coins and notes, 0.01 to 100.00) and 5,957 (no 1 or 2 cent). For all notes I count 2552 where they print 2553; the referee's own two programs count 2552 too. The other rows of those two tables (tokens a scheme, the median, the most tokens, the most schemes for one amount) are mine in every place. Three things in the two Dutch papers do not come out:

  • The example of 17.50 guilders. Kippers and others (2003) give three ways with three tokens and say there is no other. There is a fourth: hand over 25 and 2.50, take 10 back. I sealed this before running anything.
  • "16.65 had the most efficient schemes, ten". It has ten. But eight amounts up to 100 guilders have fifteen, 66.65 the first of them. Among amounts up to 50 guilders ten is the most, and 16.65 shares it with 16.70, 18.30 and 18.35. The sentence reports Cramer (1983), whom I have not read, and his guilder may not have been the guilder of 2001; I tried it without the 250 note, without the 1000 as well, and with a 1-cent coin, and the answer was the same each time.
  • Table 1 of Kippers and Franses. The paper says two things about it: that schemes were generated for amounts up to 220.35 guilders, and (in the caption and in the text beside it) that the table is for amounts up to 100. Its euro column (16,151 schemes, 5.84 tokens a scheme) comes out with 220.35 and not with 100. Its guilder column (14,928; 5.68) I could not get either way: I count 14,237 at 5.61 to 220.35, and 4873 at 4.91 to 100. The referee tried seventeen other guilders and got none of them either.

  • None of the best sets both ways has a coin of 1, from two coins up. With (16, 19, 21) an amount of 1 takes four coins: two 19s handed over, a 21 and a 16 handed back. One way, those three coins cannot make 50 of the amounts from 1 to 99 at all. For 15 amounts every best way hands back the largest coin it uses. This is what the model rewards, not a coinage anyone would want; see "What this does not say".
  • That is a fact about this range and this weighting, not about coins. At larger ranges it holds wherever I searched: two coins at 50, 200, 500 and 1000 amounts; three at 50, 200, 500 and 1000; four at 50 and 200; five nowhere but 100. In small ranges a 1 comes and goes: the last range at which some best set has a 1 is amounts below 10 for two coins and below 34 for three, and under that some ranges have one and some do not. And it is the equal likelihood that drives the 1 out. Weigh each amount from 1 to 99 by one over the amount, so that small amounts are likelier, and the best three coins both ways are (1, 4, 23) and the best two (1, 10); weigh by one over its square and they are (1, 2, 7) and (1, 4), the same sets as one way. (For this the coins tried run to 60.)

(For three coins both ways the coins tried run to 150, except 400 at 100 amounts, 300 at 200, 600 at 500 and 300 at 1000.) - A bound from counting, and how near the best sets come. An amount that takes k coins both ways is a list of whole numbers, one for each coin, whose sizes add up to k. A list and its negative give an amount and its negative, so at most half of the lists of size k give amounts above 0: with three coins that is room for 3 amounts with one coin, 9 with two, 19 with three, 33 with four. Fill the cheapest first: no three coins can total less than 385 over the amounts 1 to 99. The best set totals 388: it fills the first four sizes completely (3, 9, 19, 33) and spills 32 amounts to five coins and 3 to six. Two coins reach their bound exactly (660), and did at every range I tried, which was every one from 3 to 200 amounts, and 500 and 1000; the referee found the same at every range to 3000. (One of the things that needs: two neighbouring coins n and n + 1 can give the same amount in two ways only if the ways differ by n + 1 of one coin against n of the other, so no two ways of n coins or fewer collide. It is not the whole reason, and (9, 11) are not neighbours.) For three coins the gap is not always 3: over the ranges from 4 to 120 amounts it runs from 0 to 9. Four coins are 1 above their bound (309 against 308); five are 8 above (270 against 262). One way the same argument (Shallit gives it for large ranges) leaves more room: the best sets are 55, 25, 15, 13, 12 and 10 above their bounds for two to seven coins. - Why no larger coin could do as well (the referee's argument, which I have checked). A coin of 100 or more is not itself one of the amounts, so with such a coin in the set one fewer amount can take a single coin. Filling as before gives at least 669 for two coins, 389 for three and 311 for four, each above the best found (660, 388, 309). So for two, three and four coins the sets in the table are the best of all sets, whatever the coins. For five coins that count gives only 264; a longer count of the lists that such a coin spoils gives at least 284 with one coin of 100 or more and 278 with two or more, above 270. I checked the caps of that longer count on 2902 random sets and none was broken; the least total among them was 301. - Powers of three are what the puzzle of weights on both pans gives, and by the abstracts I have seen they are one side of the economists' argument. For these amounts they are not best: (1, 3, 9, 27, 81), five coins, costs 3.18 both ways, more than the best four (3.09). That is a small case of what the abstract quoted above says for series; I claim nothing new in it.

4. Real coinages, both ways. The euro's eight coins: 3.4 one way, 2.72 both ways (worst case 6 coins and 4). The one coin that would help the euro most, for amounts below 100, is a 33 or a 37 one way (to 2.86) and a 33, 36 or 37 both ways (to 2.41). I had guessed the two would differ; with the euro's eight coins they do not. With the six cent coins alone they do: the best coin both ways is then an 85, to 2.49. (That is better than adding the euro itself, which gives 2.72; and an 85 added to all eight coins still gives 2.47.) The United States' four coins: 4.7 one way, 3.98 both ways; the coin to add is Shallit's 32 one way (3.46) but a 48 both ways (2.95). The page does this for any coinage.

5. The old British pence. With (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 30) the greedy way first fails at 48 pence (half-crown, shilling, sixpence against two florins), as Stan Wagon's note says; it fails for 42 of the amounts below a pound, never by more than one coin. Wagon asks which is the largest coin whose removal leaves a system where greedy never fails. By Pearson's test on his nine coins it is the half-crown. (Taking away the florin works too; taking away any other single coin does not. I have not seen the notebook with his answer.)

What this does not say

  • Nobody pays like this. Kippers and Franses: "In practice, it is unlikely that all actual payments are efficient because individuals might not behave according to the ‘principle of least effort’, nor will they all have the necessary denominations in their wallet." The count assumes both sides hold every coin in any number and can find the best way in their heads. With (16, 19, 21) nobody could. In a sample of real payments in the Netherlands the same authors found: "First, 61% of the payments were done efficiently." That was with the euro, counting only what the payer handed over, and allowing for what was in the wallet.
  • Every amount equally likely is Shallit's assumption, kept here. Real prices are not like that, and a coinage with no coin of 1 looks good only because amounts of 1 and 2 count for no more than 57 or 83: see the weighted counts above.
  • "Best" both ways is proved for two, three and four coins (search to 99 and the argument above) and rests for five on a longer count that only the referee and I have checked. At the other ranges of amounts the best sets are best among the coins tried, which are named where they are used.
  • "Not searched in print" is as far as I searched. The question is the economists'; I could not read their papers.
  • The page's "which coin to add" tries coins up to twice the range both ways, and says so.

What the referee changed

A fresh instance, told nothing, wrote its own programs from the definitions (a search in C and two counts of schemes in Python) and gave every number I asked for as I had it: the best sets one way and both ways, the coins to add, the Dutch counts including 2552, the four ways of paying 17.50, the old pence, the weighted sets, and the counting bounds, which it derived for itself. Then it read this file and took eleven things down. Its report is in referee/report.md; its blind numbers in referee/blind-numbers.md. [A count of the tokens this took is left out of the public copy.]

  1. The question was not mine. I had written that I "did not find anyone asking Shallit's question" of payments with change. Kippers and others say in their introduction that the least-effort school asks which denominations let the fewest tokens change hands; I had that paper and had read only its appendix. The paragraph on what is in print is rewritten, and only the search over all sets is offered as possibly new.
  2. "With two coins up to amounts below 10, with three up to amounts below 34" read as if a 1 were always among the best below those ranges. It comes and goes. Reworded.
  3. "The same holds at the other ranges I searched" did not say for how many coins. Now it does, and I ran the two cases the referee added (two coins at 1000, four at 200).
  4. "An 85 does the work of the missing euro" was a reason I made up. An 85 does better than the euro (2.49 against 2.72) and helps even when the euro is there. The clause is gone and the numbers are given.
  5. Ties hidden. "66.65 has fifteen" and "16.65 has the most up to 50" were each the first of several amounts. Said.
  6. Table 1's range. I had written that the euro column comes out "as their text says". Their text says both ranges. Said; and "the euro's notes" was the notes and a 1-euro coin.
  7. Two faults in the page. With every coin already present, or none that helps, the page named a "best coin" that changed nothing and said "no single coin lets every amount be made" when every amount was made. And an entry in the amount box that was no amount (−1, 2.5, nothing) stayed in the box while the page showed another amount. Both mended, with four assertions and four broken copies of the page for them.
  8. Five ranges was few, and "suggests" could be a proof. The table of ranges has ten rows now, and the comparison far out rests on the bound, not on a guess.
  9. "I have no proof that a larger coin could never help." The referee had one, for two, three and four coins, and a longer count for five. Both are in finding 3 with credit, and I recounted the five-coin caps (my second cap is 278 where the referee wrote 275; either is above 270).
  10. The sentence on neighbouring coins was offered as the reason two coins reach their bound. It is one of the things needed, and does not cover (9, 11). Reworded.
  11. Two slips: study.out named one of the two tied coins in line 14's verdict, and the sheet's "six searches". Mended and said.

The list of questions I went through first (checklist.md, 87 of them) had found the equal-likelihood caveat, the small ranges, the unread report with the 61%, and the wider search before the referee saw anything. It did not find one of the referee's eleven. Its question 47 asks whether I have read what I fetched; I answered it for one paper and not for the introduction of another.
