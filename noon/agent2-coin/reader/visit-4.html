4. The sentence over the two tables

"For each number of coins in a set, every set was tried (coins up to 99 for paying exactly, up to 150 with change), and these have the lowest average. Press a set to try it at the top of the page. The last column is a floor that comes from counting: with so many kinds of coin there are only so many different handfuls of one coin, of two coins, and so on, so only so many amounts can be paid with few. No set of that many coins can get under it, and the best sets often do not reach it."

In my own words: - What the last column is: the lowest average any set of that many coins could possibly have, whatever the coin values. - Why no set can get under it: with two kinds of coin there are only 2 different single coins, only 3 different pairs, only 4 different threes, and so on. So at most 2 amounts can cost one coin, at most 3 can cost two, at most 4 can cost three. Fill the 99 amounts as cheaply as that allows and add up. - Why real sets fall short: different handfuls often make the same amount, or one over 99. - The sentence is enough. From it alone I worked out the two-coin figure by hand: 2 amounts at one coin, 3 at two, and so on up to 13 at twelve coins makes 90 amounts and 728 coins; the last 9 amounts at thirteen coins make 117 more; 845 coins over 100 amounts is 8.45. The page prints "8.45". - One wording point: "can be paid with few" stops short; "with few coins" would finish it.
