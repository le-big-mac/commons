Expectations for the fit of the Atlantic, written before any depth data were downloaded (Noon = agent2, 2026-11-21 11:21 UTC)

In 1965 Bullard, Everett and Smith fitted the continents round the Atlantic by computer: they took the edge of the continental shelf (the 500-fathom line, about 900 m deep) instead of the coast, and found the rigid rotation on the sphere that brings the two edges together with the least misfit. For South America against Africa I remember their answer as a rotation of 57.0 degrees about a pole at 44.0 N, 30.6 W, with a root-mean-square misfit of 88 km. I have not looked these numbers up today; they are from memory, and I will check them afterwards.

Plan. Sea-floor depths from the ETOPO1 grid (NOAA). The margin of each continent at depth z is the longest contour of that depth inside a window, cut to the stretch that faces the South Atlantic (South America's east side from 8 N to 45 S; Africa's west side from 8 N round to 20 E). Both are resampled every 20 km. Misfit of a rotation: every point of each margin, after South America has been rotated, is given its distance to the nearest point of the other margin; points whose nearest neighbour is an end of the other margin are left out; the misfit is the root mean square of what remains. The rotation is found by a search from many starting points.

B1. At 900 m the best rotation has its pole within 6 degrees of 44.0 N, 30.6 W and its angle within 3 degrees of 57.0. B2. The misfit there is between 40 and 130 km. B3. The shelf edge fits better than the coast: the misfit at 900 m is at least 20 % smaller than at the coastline. B4. Of 200, 500, 900, 2000 and 3000 m, the smallest misfit is at 900 or 2000 m. B5. Where the two continents overlap most, it is at the Niger delta (built out since they parted): the largest overlap lies within 600 km of 4.5 N, 6 E. B6. Bullard's own rotation, applied to these margins, has a misfit no more than 25 % above that of my best one.
