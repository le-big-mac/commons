0. In short

  • I found no published refit of the isobaths on a modern grid (five searches), so the numbers are new as far as I got, but they sit inside the "Bullard family" of published fit poles (the name Eagles 2007 gave them, per Pérez-Díaz & Eagles 2014).
  • Everything qualitative is in the 1965 paper, including the 20° S ridge and the coast-versus-slope point, which Bullard credits to Carey (1958).
  • The README's account of the northern end is wrong by the field's standards. The gap between the Guianas (Demerara plateau) and the Guinea plateau under a Bullard-type rotation is a known, real misfit, not an artefact of where the margin was cut.
  • "Nearly the same fit" is fair against 1965, not against modern preferred reconstructions, which reject one rigid rotation and deliberately overlap present-day margins by 100 km to a few hundred km.
  • The front page's "until the coasts fit" contradicts both the study and the paper.

2. Findings

C1: FOUND. - Bullard et al. 1965, p. 43 (dl/01/bullard1965.txt): "This was done with the computer EDSAC 2". - Same, p. 41: "Wegener pointed out that the fit should be made at the continental edge, but did not pursue the matter in detail. Carey (1958) was the first to show that the fit of Africa and South America is much closer at the continental edges than it is at the coastline. In spite of this, Jeffreys has expressed a total disbelief in the reality of the fit; he says (1964): 'I simply deny there is an agreement.'" - Moulin, Aslanian & Unternehr 2010, Fig. 5 caption (dl/02/moulin2010.txt): "The first computer-assisted statistical reconstruction (Bullard et al., 1965)". - Pérez-Díaz & Eagles 2014, p. 2 (dl/05/): "Many models have seen the light since Bullard et al. [1965] broke new ground with their computer-assisted reconstruction." - Zahirovic, GPlates Gondwana practical (dl/22/, date not established): "derived the first numerical fit of the circum-Atlantic continents". - Sure of this. One wording point: the 500-fathom line is on the continental slope, not the shelf edge. Bullard: "The real 'edge of the continent' is the continental slope".

C2, C3: NOT FOUND as a recomputation; the values fall inside published ranges. - Bullard Table 1 (p. 45) gives poles from 39.0 to 45.1° N, 29.0 to 30.6° W, angles 50.8 to 60.0°. Its 500 and 1,000 fm rows span 42.5–44.4° N. The README's 200–900 m poles (45.9–46.7° N) lie 1.5–2.3° north of every 500 or 1,000 fm row. - Moulin 2010 Table 1 lists later fits: Rabinowitz & LaBrecque 1979 at 45.5, −32.2, 57.5; Vink 1982 at 47, −33.8, 58; Nürnberg & Müller 1991, Argentina block, at 46.72, −32.67, 56.583, and northern blocks at 49.23, −31.83, 55.885. - By my script (compare.py, compare.out), the README's 900 m pole is 1.0° from Nürnberg & Müller's Argentina pole, 1.4° from Rabinowitz & LaBrecque, 2.8° from Bullard fit 9 (2.77°, so the README's figure reproduces) and 3.9° from fit 2. - The 2.8° and 0.7° difference from fit 9 moves margin points by 30 km (Santos) to 172 km (Demerara). That is the size of the misfit itself. - The fall of angle with depth (61.2° to 55.2°) is also in Bullard's table (60.0, 57.1, 55.8, 50.8° at 100 to 2,000 fm). - Fairly sure no refit is in the open literature I could reach; the two teaching pages found do not refit. The Imperial College primer (dl/16/, © 2020) rotates points with a given pole; the GPlates practical says the computed fit "is beyond the scope of this activity – we are happy to do it visually!"

C4: 88 km FOUND; the README's own figures NOT FOUND. - Bullard p. 46: "The misfit for the 500 fm. fit on BB is 88 km." The abstract gives "30 to 90 km". - Three things the README does not say: - Bullard's km are angular misfits converted at mid-line (s = 111 δφ sin ψ, ψ = 70°). - Bullard's lowest figure is 69 km (fit 7). With delta and ridge left in it is 90 km (fit 2), which is the like-for-like row for the README's 89 km. - Bullard's Figure 4, which I read by eye from the scan (img/bullard_fig4_zoom-07.png, ±3°), shows stretch BB running from about the mouth of the Amazon to about 46° S, and from about Liberia to about Cape Agulhas. Bullard never fitted the Guianas. The README's after-the-fact "middle 8,000 km" (38° S to 3° N) is close to Bullard's own stretch (the 3° N end to BB, the 38° S end to the shorter stretch AA). - One caution: Smith et al. 2007 (dl/18/) say "a root-mean-square misfit of about 130km for South America to Africa". That is a misreading of Bullard's abstract, where 130 km is the fit between the northern and southern blocks. It has nothing to do with the README's 130 km.

C5: FOUND IN PART; one contradiction. - Bullard's stated conclusion (p. 45): "On the whole, fitting the 500 fm. contours seems the best; it gives the closest fit and usually lies on the steepest part of the slope". - The README's best depth is 2,000 m, about 1,090 fm. - "The same shape" holds for the km column with features included (140, 90, 85, 190 km at 100, 500, 1,000, 2,000 fm), not for Bullard's conclusion. - Bullard's table has no coastline row; its shallowest is 100 fm (183 m). The coast point is his prose, credited to Carey (1958). - I would believe both sets of numbers. The README's 129/130/136 km differences carry no error estimate and should not rank those depths.

C6: FOUND, all three. - Bullard pp. 45–46: "All solutions showed a substantial misfit of up to 270 km at the Niger delta; this is not surprising as this delta has been built out in the Tertiary and is certainly a recent addition to the continental edge. The point of junction of the African coast with the Walvis ridge shows a misfit of up to 130 km". - Same: "The projection in the contour off South America in latitude 20° S, which overlaps the African coast, is connected with a ridge running out to Trinidade island, this is presumably volcanic and Tertiary and should perhaps have been omitted, it was not." - Smith et al. 2007, describing Lawver et al. 1999: "Lawver, et al., mentioned three overlaps: two deltaic (the Niger and Amazon Deltas) deposits are accounted for as they are post-break-up features. The third overlap is in the region of the Abrolhos and Royal Charlotte Banks, which are a result of magmatic activity post-dating break-up by 80 – 100 million years." - Moulin 2010 also excepts "the Amazon and Niger deltas". The README does not mention the Amazon. - The 333 km and 180 km figures are the README's own. Sure.

C7: FOUND IN PART. - Bullard says Tertiary for all three; for the Walvis ridge that is out of date. - Wikipedia, "Walvis Ridge" (dl/17/, a tertiary source): the hotspot track "first formed over the mantle plume that formed the Etendeka-Paraná continental flood basalts" at 135–132 Ma, and "The eastern section of the ridge is thought to have been created in the Middle Cretaceous period", 120–80 Ma. So the landward end is about as old as the breakup there. - Moulin 2010: "important accumulation of Cenozoic sediments" in the Niger and Amazon deltas. - Abrolhos at 54–44 Ma rests on a search snippet only. - Moderately sure; "after" is right for the delta and the Brazilian banks and loose for the Walvis ridge.

C8: the pairing FOUND; the explanation CONTRADICTED. - Moulin 2010 §2.3: "In Bullard's model, for instance, the fit of the South Atlantic implies a huge gap in the Equatorial segment". - Same, Fig. 5 caption: Bullard's fit "shows a good fit in the southern part of the South Atlantic Ocean but has dramatic consequences in the Equatorial Atlantic Ocean (huge gap between Demerara and Guinea plateaus) This illustrates the impossibility to close together the Equatorial and South Atlantic oceans." - Same: Rabinowitz & LaBrecque's fit "is close to Bullard's pre-drift reconstruction, with a 200 km-gap between the Guinea and Demerara plateaus". - Smith et al. 2007: "the remaining misfit was a vexing issue until the proposition of intra-plate deformation along the Benue Trough-Niger Rift in Africa and in the southern parts of South America." - Pérez-Díaz & Eagles 2014, p. 9: "It has long been recognized [Burke and Dewey, 1974; Fairhead, 1988; Unternehr et al., 1988] that intracontinental deformation can be surmised from the occurrence of unacceptable misfits of breakup markers on the continental margins." - My check (ends.py, plateau coordinates from memory): the README's rotation leaves the nose of the Demerara plateau about 400 km from the Guinea plateau, and Bullard's about 490 km. The equatorial rotations of Heine 2013 and Moulin 2010 leave 140–170 km; Heine reports "a gap of ≈ 50 km between the Demerara Rise and the Guinea Plateau". - I believe the literature. The README's own second look (misfit rises to 161 km, rotation barely moves) is what a real gap produces. - I did not find in Bullard's text the statement Moulin attributes to it ("The authors pointed out a geometrical problem").

C9: FOUND IN PART. - Heine 2013, §4.2: "Our fit reconstruction for the southernmost South Atlantic places the Malvinas/Falkland Islands immediately south of the Aghulas Arch/Outeniqua Basin." This is the geometry the README describes. - The field's account of a residual southern gap under a rotation that suits the centre is deformation inside South America. Moulin 2010 on Curie 1984: an intraplate discontinuity "allowed Curie to close the southern part of the South Atlantic independently". Smith et al. 2007 say Nürnberg & Müller "focussed on the Parana-Chacos, Colorado, and Salado basins". - Moulin calls Bullard's own fit "good" in the south. The README's rotation differs from Bullard's by 64–97 km there by my script. - Moderately sure.

C10: FOUND, exact. - Table 1 row 9: "500 500 removed B-B 44.0 30.6 57.0 0.93 1.6 88". There are twelve rows. - Figure 4 caption: "80 points were taken on AA and 100 on BB." - Bullard p. 44 also expects other criteria to give much the same: "the mean square misfit measured perpendicular to the contours might be minimized. It seems unlikely that such criteria would give appreciably different results".

C11: FOUND. - The table is Table 1 of Smith, Kurtz, Richards, Forster & Lister, "A re-evaluation of the breakup of South America and Africa using deformable mesh reconstruction software", Journal of the Virtual Explorer 25, paper 1, 2007 (dl/11/, dl/18/): "Bullard et al. (1965) | U. Jurrasic | 42.9 | -30.1 | 57.1 | Africa". - The same table gives 44, −30.6, 57 to Smith and Hallam (1970). - Moulin 2010 Table 1 gives Bullard as 44.0, −30.6, 57. - Sure.

C12: FOUND; it is the field's main point, and stronger than the README puts it. - Pérez-Díaz & Eagles 2014: "Even within the confidence regions, none of the determined rotations overlaps with any of the "Bullard Family" [Eagles, 2007] of full-fit rotations that concentrate on optimizing continental margin fits." - Heine 2013, §4.2: "We allow on average 100 km of overlap between the present-day continental margins in the EqRS (Fig. 14), compared to a few hundred km in the central part of the SARS." - So minimising gaps and overlaps of present-day isobaths is no longer the criterion for a pre-rift fit.

C13: FOUND. Bullard pp. 41–42: "There is, of course, some arbitrariness and personal judgement in choosing what is to be fitted."

What a plate-reconstruction person would object to in the method: - One rigid rotation. Africa is treated as three plates and South America as several in current models. - Present-day isobaths as the edge. They are altered by sediment and volcanism and sit on stretched crust; the field fits continent–ocean boundaries, hinge lines or restored margins. - Unsigned nearest-point misfit. It lets the margins slide along each other with no tie points (fracture zones, conjugate lineaments), pools gaps with overlaps, and has a trimming rule that changes which points count as the rotation changes. - Stretches. The README's take in the Guianas, which Bullard left out, and stop short of both the Guinea plateau and the Falkland plateau. - Which measure leads. The README's "How well" bullet opens with the after-the-fact 89 km beside Bullard's 88 km. The measure fixed beforehand gave 130 km, and Bullard's own rotation scores better (80 km) on that stretch.

3. Credits missing or wrong

  • Lines 6–8 and 17–18: Carey (1958) for edge-fits-better-than-coast, as Bullard himself credits (p. 41), and Wegener for the idea. Jeffreys (1964) is the "old argument". The README credits the coast result to "Bullard's own table", which has no coast row.
  • Lines 19–21 and 63: say that Bullard named the 20° S ridge ("a ridge running out to Trinidade island") and left it in. Lawver et al. 1999, as reported by Smith et al. 2007, list the Abrolhos and Royal Charlotte Banks, and with Moulin 2010 the Amazon delta.
  • Line 21: no source is given for the ages.
  • Lines 59–60, 65–66, 73–75, 99–100: Burke & Dewey 1974; Unternehr et al. 1988; Nürnberg & Müller 1991; Moulin, Aslanian & Unternehr 2010 (its Table 1 tabulates the historic fits); Heine, Zoethout & Müller 2013; Pérez-Díaz & Eagles 2014; Eagles 2007 for "Bullard family".
  • Lines 70–72: say that the middle stretch nearly coincides with Bullard's own stretches.
  • Lines 93–95: name the table (Smith et al. 2007, Table 1).
  • Lines 114–115: the citation is correct (vol. 258, no. 1088, 28 Oct 1965, pp. 41–51; Table 1 on p. 45).

4. Sentences that say more than the evidence allows

  • Front page, "until the coasts fit". Fair: "Sliding South America against Africa by computer until the edges of the continents, about 900 m down, fit, as Bullard, Everett and Smith first did in 1965. On today's sea-floor data much the same rotation comes out, within about 3° of pole and 1° of angle, which is 30 to 170 km at the margin. Geologists have since shown that no single rigid rotation is the true fit."
  • Line 12, "They fit, and where Bullard said." Fair: "The best single rotation is within 2.8° of Bullard's pole and 0.7° of his angle, inside the spread of later published fits."
  • Lines 14–15. Lead with 130 km. Then: "on a middle stretch chosen afterwards, close to the one Bullard fitted, 89 km, against Bullard's 90 km with delta and ridge left in and 88 km with them removed; his own rotation scores 80 km there."
  • Line 18, "Bullard's own table has the same shape." Fair: "Bullard's table, which starts at 100 fathoms, is lowest in km at 1,000 fathoms and worst at 2,000; he judged 500 fathoms best; mine is lowest at 2,000 m."
  • Line 21. Fair: "The delta and the Brazilian banks were built long after the continents parted; the Walvis ridge began about when they did."
  • Lines 59–60 and 73–75. Fair: "This is the known gap between the Demerara and Guinea plateaus: a rotation that closes the South Atlantic does not close the Equatorial Atlantic, which is why later reconstructions break both continents into blocks."
  • Lines 103–104, "the agreement is the point". Add that agreement between two least-squares fits of similar margins is expected, and Bullard said so on p. 44.

5. Terms

  • "Edges of the continental shelves" and "shelf edge" (line 7 and B3) for the 900 m line: that line is on the continental slope; the shelf edge is the shelf break near 200 m.
  • "Depth of the line": isobath.
  • "Pole" and "rotation": Euler pole, finite or total rotation.
  • "The fit": the Bullard fit; full-fit, tight-fit or pre-rift reconstruction.
  • "Partner": conjugate margin.
  • "The Guianas'" feature: Demerara Plateau or Rise.
  • "The ridge running out from Brazil at 20° S": Vitória–Trindade Ridge, Abrolhos Bank.
  • "South of the Cape": Falkland (Malvinas) Plateau, Agulhas–Falkland Fracture Zone.
  • "Stretched at their edges": rifted-margin extension; continent–ocean boundary or transition.
  • Also missing: intraplate (intracontinental) deformation, Benue Trough, and the Equatorial, Central and Austral segments.
  • "Tertiary" is Bullard's word; today Cenozoic or Paleogene.
