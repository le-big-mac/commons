In brief. On what day is a league title, a top-four place or relegation certain on points alone? Over ten Premier League seasons a simple count that forgets who still plays whom was late 10 times in 259 on three such questions (out of the title race, sure of the top four, relegated), never by more than ten league matches or fifteen days, and 50 times in 170 on a fourth, whether a club is safe from relegation. For one season of ice hockey a paper of 2008 found such a count late for two teams, by a day or two; the count for this league I did not find in print.

What follows is a working record, long and full of numbers, kept as it was written. The short account, and everything else, is on the front page; the words these records use are explained on another.

Near the end of a football season the papers say a club is "mathematically" champion, safe or relegated. The usual arithmetic is simple: the most points a club can still reach, against the points the others have now. It forgets that the others must still play each other, and somebody has to take those points. The exact answer is a small integer programme over the remaining fixtures. This asks how often, and by how much, the exact answer comes first. (26 November: none of that framing is mine. The simple count is what Cheng and Steffy (2008) call the "most common rule used by the press"; for baseball an exact method that uses the fixtures dates from the 1960s (Schwartz 1966, as Wayne 2001 reports; I have not read Schwartz); and the exact answer by integer programming for football with three points for a win is Ribeiro and Urrutia's (2005) and Gotzes and Hoppmann's (2020). I did not check what English papers print. "Exact" here means exact on points alone: see "What this does not show".)

In short: seldom, on the questions people ask: 10 times in 269, and never by more than a round. (26 November: a round is my unit, ten league matches. In days the ten delays were 1, 1, 1, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 7 and 15. And ten of the 269 are titles, which cannot be late: 10 in 259 is the fairer rate. days.py → days.out.)

  • Ten Premier League seasons, 2014–15 to 2023–24. For every club and every day, five matters: sure of the title, out of the title race, sure of the top four, relegated, safe from relegation.
  • On the first four the simple count was late 10 times in 269, and never by more than one round of matches. (26 November: by matter, never in 10 titles, 5 times in 190 for being out of the title race, 4 in 39 for the top four, which is one in ten, and once in 30 for relegation.)
  • The title: never. That one follows from the definitions in two lines (Adler and others said the same of baseball in 2002), so it is a test of the program and not a finding.
  • The one matter where it is often late is one that is asked as a joke: is a top club safe from relegation yet? (26 November: I had written "the one nobody asks". A German satirical website made the joke about Bayern Munich in 2014 and 2018, and Gotzes and Hoppmann (2020) say their paper set out from it; they answer it exactly.) There the exact answer came first in 50 of 170 cases, by up to three rounds. Manchester City in 2017–18 were truly safe on 30 December and by the simple count on 21 January. (26 November: in days the longest is not that one. Leicester in 2019–20 were safe on 9 March and by the simple count on 20 June, 103 days and 0.8 of a round apart: the match file has no league match from 9 March to 17 June of that year, a hundred days (the cause, the pandemic, I give from memory). The median of the fifty is 7 days.)
  • In the one case I looked up, the reports did the exact reasoning themselves. Stoke City in 2018 were truly relegated on 5 May; a bare count of points would have waited until 8 May, because the two clubs Stoke could still catch had to play each other. The reports of 5 May said relegated. They were right. (26 November: the Premier League's own report of that day gives the reason: Stoke were "three points from safety with one match to play, and with no way of moving above both Southampton and Swansea City".)
  • Three of six expectations held (one of them, the title, by definition). I expected the simple count to be late much more often.

The five matters

(26 November: the field's words for these are "clinched" or "mathematically qualified" for my "sure of", and "mathematically eliminated" for my "out of" and "cannot"; the simple count for a title is close kin to baseball's "magic number", which guarantees "at least a tie for the top spot" where my title is outright. A "round" below is my own unit, ten league matches, and not a matchday.)

Each is asked of one club on one day, given the points so far and the fixtures left. A tie on points counts against the club in the "sure" matters and for it in the "cannot" ones, so goal difference never enters.

matter exact the simple count
out of the title race no set of results lets the club finish level with or above everyone its largest possible total is below the leader's points now
relegated no set of results leaves at most 16 clubs above it 17 clubs already have more than its largest possible total
safe no set of results puts 17 clubs level with or above it at most 16 others can still reach its points now
sure of the top four no set of results puts 4 clubs level with or above it at most 3 others can still reach its points now
sure of the title no set of results puts any club level with or above it nobody can still reach its points now

The simple count is always safe: when it says settled, it is. The question is only whether it is late.

Method

  • Data: every match with its date, from https://www.football-data.co.uk/mmz4281/SEASON/E0.csv for the ten seasons (380 matches each; hashes in data.sha256).
  • Exact answers: clinch.py. For a "cannot" matter the club is given a win in every game it has left, and the solver looks for results of the other games that keep enough clubs at or below it. For a "sure" matter the club loses every game left, and the solver looks for results that bring enough clubs level. Before the solver runs, results that can never hurt are fixed and clubs that cannot matter are dropped; without that, a few mid-season cases did not finish. (26 November: that is a fact about my model and solver, not about the problem. Gotzes and Hoppmann solved every club on every matchday but the early ones of fifteen Bundesliga seasons with a stock solver, the slowest case in 11.84 seconds. In general the question is NP-complete under three points for a win, as they report of Bernholt and others (1999) and of Kern and Paulusma (2001), and Ribeiro and Urrutia of the first; I have read neither.)
  • The date of a matter is the first day on which it is settled, found by bisection (once settled, it stays settled; Ribeiro and Urrutia say the same of the two scores they compute, one of which can only fall and the other only rise). The lead of exact over simple is the number of league matches played in between, divided by ten.
  • Check: on 10,000 questions about small made-up leagues (five clubs, twenty games), the program agrees with trying every possible set of results, every time. In 116 of those the truth was settled before the simple count said so, so the test does exercise the difference.

Why I was wrong, as far as I can see: for the simple count to be late, several clubs must all be within reach of the same line at the same time and due to play each other. At the top and bottom of this league, late in the season, there is usually one club that matters, and for one club the simple count is exact.

(26 November: this reason was in print for ice hockey eighteen years before I offered it. Cheng and Steffy 2008, p. 191: "When a single team is doing very well (or poorly) the simple rule can detect qualification (elimination) accurately as early as possible. Our method outperforms the simple rule when there were several teams with close scores fighting for the last couple of playoff spots.")

What this does not show

  • Other leagues or other points systems. Tighter leagues, or two points for a win, may behave differently.
  • What the papers actually printed. "The simple count" is my reconstruction of the usual arithmetic, which turns out to be Cheng and Steffy's "simple rule". In the one case I looked up (Stoke, 2018) the reports had the exact answer on the day.
  • Goal difference. Ties on points are counted against or for the club as the question requires, never resolved. (26 November: so "exact", "truly" and "mathematically" in this write-up mean on points alone. The field counts the tie-breaking rules as part of the problem: Russell and van Beek (2008) solved two seasons of ice hockey "while respecting all tie-breaking rules". By my convention a place decided on goal difference is never settled, even after the last match, as the 39 above shows. Cheng and Steffy have a sentence on exactly this convention, a rule under which a team must be "strictly greater" on points: "This condition is of course sufficient but too strong to be of any practical value. In fact, our experiment shows that this simplification is often worse than the simple rule used by the media." So against a press rule that counts a tie-breaker, as the hockey press's does with wins ("slightly improved by tallying the wins of each team in addition to their score and using this to break ties when they occur"), both of my columns may be late; whether English papers count goal difference before the last day I did not check. This page compares the two columns only with each other.)

What was already in print

Added on 26 November 2026 from prior-art-26nov.md. I checked each quotation below against the text the reader saved. The papers are named as the reader found them; I have read the quoted passages and not the papers through.

  • The simple count and its fault. Wayne (2001), on baseball: "The problem is not as easy as many sports writers would have you believe, in part because the answer depends not only on the number of games won and left to play but also on the schedule of remaining games. In the 1960's, Schwartz showed how to determine whether one particular team is eliminated using a maximum flow computation." Cheng and Steffy (2008), on ice hockey: "The most common rule used by the press, or by curious fans, is simple and can be computed by hand", and it "does not give a false positive result ... but as mentioned it can fail to identify a qualified team". That is what I had put as "The simple count is always safe: when it says settled, it is. The question is only whether it is late." Their own answer is exact only up to the first tie-breaker, the number of wins: "These MIPs are used to determine qualification/elimination with respect to R1", and for the full rules, "we ran into numerical difficulties and were unable to get reliable results".
  • How often and by how much, in other sports. Baseball, at second hand: "Robinson (1991) showed that such an optimization approach would have eliminated teams an average of three days earlier than the wins-based criterion during the 1987 season" (Adler, Erera, Hochbaum and Olinick 2002). This is the "paper of 2002 on baseball" that the referee of 23 November half remembered. Ice hockey, 2003–04: "For most of the teams the simple rule was able to detect qualification and elimination at the same time as our method, but our method determined elimination of one team early and qualification of one team early", the qualification by two days and the elimination by one (Cheng and Steffy 2008). Ice hockey against what a newspaper printed: "we were able to show qualification earlier for nine teams during the 2005-06 season and for four teams during the 2006-07 season", by as much as five days (Russell and van Beek 2008), with their own caution about the largest gaps: "It is possible that this is due to the paper not realizing that teams had clinched playoff spots. Even disregarding these results, we still found discrepancies as large as four days and two days". And the contrary tendency, for elimination in baseball: "often teams may be eliminated earlier than the official declaration" (Adler and others 2002).
  • Football, exactly. Ribeiro and Urrutia (preprint of 2003, printed 2005) give the integer programmes for the Brazilian championship. Gotzes and Hoppmann (2020) do the Bundesliga and find, for instance, "that in 2014 FC Bayern managed to avoid relegation not until matchday 20". Neither sets a simple count beside the exact answer. Raack and others (2013) treat a season's extremes for the same league ("Are 40 points enough to avoid relegation in the Bundesliga?"), not its dates day by day. For baseball the reader's report names Hoffman and Rivlin (1970) after Schwartz, at second hand.
  • Not found: a count, for the Premier League or any football league, of how often the simple count is late. That count (the two tables above) is what is left of this study as its own.
  • Not reached by the search: Robinson (1991) itself and a paper of 2021 on basketball (their publishers refuse automated readers); a German paper of 2002 by Bernholt and others on the Bundesliga, which by its title is the likeliest place for such a count; anything in German, Portuguese or Spanish beyond one query; blogs and theses.
