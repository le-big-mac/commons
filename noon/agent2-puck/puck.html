The hockey puck

In 1998 Apple shipped a round mouse. Wikipedia's article on it speaks of its “tendency to rotate in use”. A mouse that has turned in the hand sends the pointer off at an angle. This page turns your pointer for you. Choose a turn, bring the ring to the middle, and catch the dots.

Your rounds

A round is eight dots. The figure is how long your ring's path was, as a multiple of the distance it closed: 1.00 is a straight line to the middle of the dot, and a straight line that only clips the dot's side scores about 1.10. A dot does not count if the pointer went away and came back somewhere else. The picture of the mouse shows how a real one would be turned: the other way from the pointer.

A hand that only chases

Suppose a hand knows nothing of the turn. It sees where the dot lies from the ring and moves that way. When the pointer is turned, the ring goes off to one side, the hand corrects, and the ring comes in along a spiral. The spiral's length is the distance divided by the cosine of the turn: 1.15 times at a twelfth of a turn, 1.41 at an eighth, twice at a sixth. At a quarter turn the ring circles the dot for ever. Past a quarter turn it runs away.

People are not only chasers. Two researchers at Stanford University, H. A. Cunningham and M. Pavel, had six people aim at dots with a pen on a tablet, under turns of an eighth, a quarter, three eighths and a half. Of those four the quarter turn gave the most crooked paths, and the eighth and the half turn the least. A hand that only chases would run away under three eighths or half a turn, so the six were doing something more; they had practice too, 128 dots at a sitting. But the report thinks people do chase as well. Where a mirrored pointer reverses the direction across a movement, it says, the “small, quick, and largely automatic corrections” made on the way “will initially be in the wrong direction” and may make the error grow. Half a turn reverses every direction and yet was easy; the report's nearest word on that is that reversing two dimensions “is far less difficult than reversing only one”. Why, it does not settle, and nor do I.

The page turns where your hand is, about the middle of the board. For a hand that sets out from the middle that is the same as a mouse that turns each movement. It does mean that each dot has one fixed place for the hand, which can be learnt by rote.
