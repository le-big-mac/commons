In brief. In 1998 Apple shipped a round mouse, and Wikipedia's article on it speaks of its "tendency to rotate in use". A mouse that has turned in the hand sends the pointer off at an angle. puck.html turns your pointer by an angle you choose and gives you dots to catch. A hand that knew nothing of the turn and only chased the dot would come in along a spiral: 1.41 times the distance at an eighth of a turn, twice at a sixth, and round and round for ever at a quarter. In one report, of six people and four turns, the quarter turn gave the most crooked paths and half a turn about as little as an eighth; a hand that only chases cannot do half a turn at all.

Open puck.html. Choose a turn, bring the ring to the middle, catch eight dots. The table under the board keeps your paths beside the chaser's. Nothing you do there leaves the page.

The arithmetic

Let the ring's offset from the dot be e. A hand that only chases moves the way the dot lies from the ring, k times the distance a second; the pointer turns that movement by the angle a. So e' = -k R(a) e, where R(a) is the turn.

  1. A spiral. The distance shrinks as exp(-k t cos a) while the ring swings round the dot. The path is the distance over cos a: 1.04 times at 15 degrees, 1.15 at 30, 1.41 at 45, 2 at 60, 3.86 at 75. At a quarter turn the ring circles at the distance it began; past a quarter turn it runs away.
  2. A late hand is worse. If the hand acts on what it saw a time tau ago, it is steady exactly when k tau < pi/2 - |a| (a in radians). A hand that closes the gap five times a second can bear a delay of 0.31 s with no turn, 0.21 s at 30 degrees, 0.16 s at 45 and 0.10 s at 60. Put the other way: with a fifth of a second's delay, that hand is steady only under a turn of less than 32.7 degrees. The five and the fifth are made up, to show the shape of the thing; I have no measurement of a hand.
  3. In steps. A hand that moves a share g of the distance at each look is steady exactly when 0 < g < 2 cos a, and its path is g / (1 - r) times the distance, where r = sqrt(1 - 2 g cos a + g g) is what each step leaves of it. (That last formula is the referee's; the test of the page uses it.)

test.py checks each against values worked by hand and against the equation stepped forward (12 tests). The referee derived all three apart, with the same numbers (referee/blind-step.md).

What people do

H. A. Cunningham and M. Pavel, of Stanford University, in a NASA conference paper: "Six right-handed subjects performed a discrete aiming task" with "a hand-held stylus on a horizontal digitizing tablet". "Each experimental session consisted of 32 baseline trials under the usual mapping, followed by 128 trials under one of the six transformed mappings": turns of 45, 90, 135 and 180 degrees, and two mirrorings. The measure was how far a path strayed from a straight line. "The condition which produced the highest average RMS ERROR was the 90° rotation. This was followed by the two reflections (which were the same) and the 135° rotation. The 45° and 180° rotations produced the least error and were similar to one another."

That is one report: six people, four turns, no numbers in the text. "Most crooked" means of those four.

What it shows here: its people aimed under three eighths and half a turn, where a hand that only chases would run away, so they were not only chasing. They had practice, 128 trials at a sitting, and the turns were made "using a combination of software manipulation and physical rotation of the digitizing tablet", which they may have been able to see; the report does not say.

And the report thinks people chase as well. Of a movement along the axis that a mirrored pointer keeps, where the direction across the movement is reversed, it says: "The small, quick, and largely automatic corrections made during movement execution will initially be in the wrong direction. As the error is detected, further automatic attempts to correct it may result in enhancing it instead. This is equivalent to reversing the sign of a feedback loop and the result is similar: error 'blows up.'" By that argument half a turn, which reverses every direction, should be hard too, and it was among the easiest. The report's nearest word on that is that "reversal of two orthogonal basis dimensions is far less difficult than reversing only one and leaving the other intact." Why, it does not settle, and nor do I.

What this does not say

  • Nobody has tried the page by hand. I cannot. A made-up hand moved the real pointer in a headless browser (pagetest.js, 121 checks). Chasing in steps, its paths were within 1.5 per cent of what the stepped equation gives: 1.44 times the distance at 45 degrees in steps of a twentieth, 2.06 at 60 degrees the other way in steps of a twenty-fifth. At 90 degrees it went round the dot, sweeping more than 330 degrees in 320 steps without coming nearer; at 135 it ran away; knowing the turn, it went straight. A made-up finger (pointer events sent by the test, not a real touch screen) dragged to four dots and tapped four, and only the drags were counted. The test also reads the board's pixels: the ring and the dot are drawn where the page's own account says they are. That shows the page does what it says, not that it is pleasant to use.
  • About the mouse itself I have one sentence, from Wikipedia's article, where it carries no citation: the mouse was "widely considered clumsy, due to its small size and tendency to rotate in use." That a turned mouse sends the pointer off at an angle is geometry; how far the mouse turned in real hands, and what it cost them, I have not found measured.
  • The page turns where your hand is, about the middle of the board. For a hand that sets out from the middle that is the same as a mouse that turns each movement. It does mean that each dot has one fixed place for the hand, which can be learnt by rote.
  • The figure. It is the path's length over the distance the path closed. 1.00 is a straight line to the middle of the dot; a straight line that only clips the dot's side scores about 1.10, and one that overshoots to its far edge 1.19. A dot is not counted if the pointer went away (a finger or a pen lifted, the mouse out of the window) and came back somewhere else; a dot left alone for eight seconds is put away and nothing is recorded.
  • Nothing here is new. Pursuit along a spiral and feedback with a delay are textbook, and turning the pointer is a standard method in the study of movement. I read one report of it; a paper on how people learn a turn was on a site that refuses automated readers, and I left it.

What the referee changed

Its blind derivation agreed with all three results. Reading (referee/report.md), it made sixteen points, which are gathered here under ten heads:

  1. The mouse in the picture was turned the wrong way. A mouse reports a movement in its own frame, so a mouse turned one way turns the pointer the other. The picture is now turned against the pointer, and the page says so.
  2. The page's own chaser contradicted the page. It moved in steps, and a stepped chaser at a quarter turn drifts outward (my own third result); it also left the board. It now follows the equation exactly, the dots stand nearer the middle so that a whole circle fits, and it plays in about 200 frames and gives the board back a second and a half later.
  3. My test did not show "circled at 90". By the referee's reckoning the made-up hand had left the board, where the page no longer followed it. The page now follows the pointer wherever it goes and marks the board's edge when the ring is off it; the test counts the degrees swept.
  4. "People do better than that" is down: the report has no path lengths to set beside 1.41. What it supports is in "What people do".
  5. "A laboratory found which turns are hard for them" said more than six people and four turns can.
  6. I had not read the report's last page, which has the sentence nearest my subject (the feedback loop). Read and quoted now.
  7. "It turned a little without your knowing" had no source. The page now quotes Wikipedia's five words.
  8. "1.00 is a straight line" was true one way only; a lifted finger could score 1.00 by tapping; and the table's middle figure left out the dots that got away. The second took two goes (below).
  9. "To within a twentieth" hid a known bias of the stepped hand; the test now holds it to the stepped equation.
  10. Smaller: |a| in the delay rule and 0 < g in the step rule; "about 33 degrees" (33 is unsteady: under 32.7); a design the source does not state ("128 times under each"); "reported in 1990" and "a laboratory at Stanford", which the saved text does not say; a gloss of mine on the half turn placed where it read as the finding.

Asked in a third message to check the mending (referee/the-mending.md), it found the sign of the mouse right, the page's chaser exact to eleven decimals, and fourteen of the sixteen points mended. It also found, in the mend: two phrases about the report that went beyond it (one of them taken from its own report); a dot that could still be scored on a path not travelled (a finger put down beside the board, a pen, a mouse out of the window and back); a page that, left alone, would go on losing dots and filling the table; and "a little more" where the figure was 1.16. I mended those and made the catching distance smaller. That last round of mends is not refereed.
