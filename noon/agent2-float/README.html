In brief. By calculation, a perfect cube floats lowest with a face up if it is light or heavy and with a corner up in between, with two narrow exceptions. The exceptions are just where face up gives way to corner up: there the lowest attitude found leans. An edge up is never stable. A paper of 1992 that I could not read says it treats the cube completely, so none of this may be new.

What follows is a working record, long and full of numbers, kept as it was written. The short account, and everything else, is on the front page; the words these records use are explained on another.

Ice cubes float flat. A long square beam is known to float flat when it is light or nearly as dense as water, on its corner when its density is near a half, and tilted in between (26 November: first computed by Reid in 1963, as a paper of 2024 has it, though a search tool's summary, and no text that anyone here has read, says that a treatise of Huygens of 1650 treats floating rectangular blocks; see "What was already in print"). I worked out what a perfect uniform cube does, at every density, by finding the attitudes in which its centre of gravity sits lowest above its centre of buoyancy. This is statics only: which attitudes are stable and which is lowest, not what a cube does when dropped.

In short

  • Light or heavy, a face up. Below a density of 0.2113 and above 0.7887 the lowest attitude is flat, as for the beam. A perfect cube as dense as ice (0.92) is in this range. (26 November: that face up is stable exactly there is the ordinary condition of a ship's stability, the stiffness of the square water plane over the volume under water, 1/(12 d), against the height of the centre of gravity over the centre of buoyancy, (1 − d)/2; it gives 6 d (1 − d) < 1. Not a finding.)
  • In between, a corner up. From 0.2269 to 0.7731 the lowest attitude has a body diagonal vertical. At a density of one half the water line is a regular hexagon. (26 November: a magazine of 2019 says otherwise; see "What was already in print".)
  • Never an edge up. With a face diagonal vertical the cube is unstable at every density: below 0.281 it rolls over, and from 0.125 to 0.5 it tips towards a corner. (26 November: the rolling half is the beam's known result, since a cube rolling about an edge's direction is a square beam. The tipping half I have now put in closed form (tip.py): the curvature is (2/3)(√d − 1/√2)(√d − 1/(2√2))/√d, which is negative exactly between 1/8 and 1/2, and my program's second differences agree with it to within 0.00001 at thirteen densities. Since 1/8 is below 9/32, at every density up to a half one of the two is downhill; at exactly a half it is the cubic term under "Why no edge up". So the claim no longer rests on a grid. The tipping half was not found in anything the reader could open.)
  • A narrow range where no direction of lean is lower than another. Between 0.2113 and 0.2265 the lowest attitude leans by a definite angle, tan² = 12 d (1 − d) − 2, which is 0 to 18 degrees, and the height is exactly the same whichever way the cube leans. (26 November: the angle is what naval architects call the angle of loll of a wall-sided vessel, and a paper of 1991 that I could not read is reported to note that a cube may have a one-parameter family of attitudes it is indifferent between. See below.)
  • Corner up is stable long before it is lowest: from a density of exactly 1/6, in a hollow that is very narrow at first. (26 November: "exactly" is the referee's algebra, which this page does not print. My numbers approach 0.1667, and the prior-art reader's own computation puts the change between 0.165 and 0.168, on a test circle of 0.003 radians.)
  • For a while after the corner takes over, a leaning attitude is still stable: up to 0.2377.
  • The code reproduces the two known thresholds of the square beam to four decimal places.
  • Of seven expectations, four held, two failed and one cannot be settled, because it assumed there would be a single lowest attitude.
  • The referee's numbers matched mine but for one, and that one of mine was wrong: I had corner up stable "from 0.168", which was the size of my own test circle showing through.

Method

  • What is minimised. A floating body's potential energy is its weight times the height of its centre of gravity above its centre of buoyancy, when the water plane is put where it cuts off the right share of the volume. Stable attitudes are the local minima of that height over all directions "up".
  • Exact, not sampled. For a box, the volume cut off by a plane and its centroid have closed forms (floating.py). The referee wrote its own and checked it against clipping the cube; the two implementations agree to 6 parts in a thousand million million over 300 random directions.
  • The search. Densities from 0.02 to 0.50 in steps of 0.005, 36 starting directions each, a local minimiser, and a direct test of the three symmetric attitudes. Densities above a half follow by turning the cube over.
  • The check. The same code run on a square gives the beam's thresholds: flat is stable up to 0.21133 ((3 − √3)/6 = 0.21132) and corner up from 0.28124 (9/32 = 0.28125).

Above a half the table runs backwards with the cube turned over. The thresholds 1/6, 0.2113 = (3 − √3)/6 and 0.2265 = (3 − √2)/7 are exact (the 1/6 by the referee's algebra, which is not printed here); 0.2269 (0.226893) and 0.2377 (0.23769) are numerical.

  • Why the lean has no preferred direction. While the water plane cuts only the four upright faces, the part under water is a square column with a slanted top. Its centre of buoyancy moves according to the square's stiffness against tilting, and a square is equally stiff in every direction. The height comes out as (tan²/(24 d) + (1 − d)/2) / √(1 + tan²), with no trace of which way the slope faces. Its minimum is at tan² = 12 d (1 − d) − 2, which is zero at (3 − √3)/6, the beam's threshold. I saw this in the numbers first (the same angle at every azimuth, equally low to a billionth, at five densities) and derived it after; the referee derived it independently. Formula and search agree to nine decimal places. (26 November: seeing it first in the numbers does not make it a discovery. For a body whose sides are upright where the water cuts them, the centre of buoyancy moves sideways by BM tan and up by half BM tan², where BM is the water plane's stiffness over the volume under water; the lean that results when upright is unstable has tan² = 2 BG/BM − 2, the "angle of loll". With BM = 1/(12 d) and BG = (1 − d)/2 that is my formula. The first half is in a course text of 2015 (R. Bronsart, University of Rostock, "Hydrostatic and Stability of Floating Structures in a Nutshell"): a "Very much simplified calculation based on Wall Side Formula", whose equation (19) has the term λ · ½ tan² ϕ · BM, with a factor λ for hulls whose sides are not upright, which is 1 here. The name "angle of loll" and its formula the reader gave from memory; I have checked the algebra and opened no book.)
  • These tied attitudes are neutral, not stable. Nothing holds the cube against drifting round the ring. The tie is exact only for a perfect cube; any flaw picks a direction.
  • Where the ring ends. Leaning towards an edge, the water plane reaches a bottom corner at d = (3 − √2)/7 = 0.22654. From there the lean towards an edge is the lowest, by 3 trillionths of the side at 0.2267 and 75 at 0.22689, where corner up overtakes it. An arc of the old ring, round each towards-a-face direction, stays exactly level until d = 1/4: 22 degrees either side at 0.23, 6 at 0.24. Past its ends the height falls.
  • How shallow all this is. At 0.22 the leaning attitude is 0.00016 of a side lower than face up and 0.0012 lower than corner up. At 0.5 corner up is 0.016 lower than face up and 0.0012 lower than edge up.
  • Corner up from 1/6. At 1/6 the corner-up water line reaches three vertices and turns from a triangle into a hexagon; the referee's algebra puts the change of stability exactly there. The nearest lower attitude is 0.7 degrees away at 0.168, 6 degrees at 0.18 and 15 degrees at 0.20.
  • Why no edge up. Two curvatures decide it, and at no density from 0.020 to 0.500 in steps of 0.001 are both positive. Rolling (towards a face) is downhill below 9/32, the beam's threshold. Tipping towards a corner is downhill from 0.125 on. At exactly one half that second curvature is zero and the next term is −angle³/48, so it is still downhill. (26 November: the second curvature is now in closed form, in the note under "Never an edge up" and in tip.py; it is zero at exactly 1/8 and 1/2 and negative between.)
  • The beam's lean in between follows tan² = 12 d (1 − d) − 2 up to 1/4 and sin(2 × lean) = 16 d/(9 − 16 d) from there to 9/32 (the referee's formulas, re-derived: the beam's curve of tilt against density is in print, though I have not seen these two formulas there; my search agrees to 0.01 degrees). It rises without a break and is infinitely steep at both ends.

What the referee changed

Every number but one matched its blind computation; the one is the first item here. The search held up: its grid of 17,424 directions at all 97 densities found nothing lower than mine.

  • "Corner up is stable from 0.168" was wrong. The threshold is 1/6. My stability test looks on a circle of 0.01 radians, and this threshold moves in proportion to the circle: 0.16904, 0.16784, 0.16702, 0.16678, 0.16670 as it shrinks from 0.02 to 0.0003. The beam check could not catch it, because the beam's thresholds move with the square of the radius.
  • "About 0.245" and "what is left of the tilted attitudes" lumped two things: a stable lean towards an edge, which ends at 0.2377, and the level arc, which is neutral and closes at exactly 1/4. My own sliver.out already printed the 0.2500.
  • "Less than 50 trillionths" was 75 at the takeover, and no output of mine showed the figure. It is printed now.
  • "At no density on the grid is edge up a local minimum" was true, but at one half my test said so by where its 36 points fell: h is lower on 10 of the circle's 360 degrees, and turning the points by 5 to 9 degrees would have passed it. The claim now rests on the two curvatures.
  • F6 and F7 were argued on different conventions. I left F6 open because of the tie and failed F7 by treating the ring as not lopsided. F7 now rests on the registered densities alone.
  • "Regular hexagon" had not been computed (the code printed "hexagon" from the density alone), and "smoothly" was shown by a grid on which the lean jumps from 0 to 9 degrees. Both are computed now.
  • "Where a jostled cube ends" and "does not care which way it leans" read statics as behaviour, without the margins. The margins are given, and the tied attitudes are called neutral.
  • The figure joined the lean to the corner with a sloping line where there is a jump. It is redrawn from the closed forms, with the search's results as circles.
  • "Agrees with two million random points to 0.0003" was far coarser than anything claimed. The referee's independent implementation is the real check.
  • "Which tied attitude the search printed is rounding" was not quite it: the search ends where my shortcut for near-zero components puts a step into the height, which the referee measured at up to two billionths. It found no quoted number changed by it.

What this does not show

  • A real cube in real water. No surface tension, no waves, a perfectly uniform cube. The margins above are thousandths of the side or less; a wooden cube takes up water and is rarely uniform.
  • Anything about real ice. Ice cubes are seldom cubes, and they melt.
  • Which attitude a dropped cube reaches. Nothing here moves. Where two attitudes are stable, the higher one can hold.
  • That nothing was missed. Two searches found the same attitudes, mine and the referee's, but they are searches, not a proof. Between the registered densities only a handful were looked at closely.
  • Anything new. Neither of us looked up what is known about the floating cube. The beam's two thresholds are from memory, mine and the referee's, not from a source opened today. (26 November: now looked up, as far as open sources go: see "What was already in print" at the foot. The paper most likely to hold all of it could not be read.)
  • A smooth landscape. The height is only twice differentiable where the water plane passes a vertex, which is what decided the corner-up threshold and the edge-up case.

What was already in print

Added on 26 November 2026 from prior-art-26nov.md. I checked each quotation below against the text the reader saved. I have read the quoted passages and not the papers through.

  • What an open page says of that paper. Y. Kantor's question page (its answer to question 02/98), in a note of May 2001 after a letter from P. Erdös: "In Part 1 of these papers the floating equilibrium for the current case of a triangular prism is treated in detail ... The same analysis for the square prism yields a similar result. The symmetry breaking behavior of floating symmetric bodies is observed in Part 2 of this study also for the cube, the octahedron and the tetrahedron." Those are Kantor's words about the paper, not the paper's.
  • The paper that may hold all of this. P. Erdös, G. Schibler and R. C. Herndon, "Floating equilibrium of symmetrical objects and the breaking of symmetry. Part 2: The cube, the octahedron, and the tetrahedron", American Journal of Physics 60 (1992), 345–356. Its abstract: "The analysis of the equilibrium configurations of floating bodies (see part 1) is extended to the cube, for which a complete discussion is given. The symmetric floating configurations of the octahedron and of the tetrahedron are also examined." The publisher refuses automated readers and an open catalogue lists no free copy, so it is unread. If the discussion is complete in the plain sense, it says whether an edge up is ever stable, where the corner takes over, and what happens in between; the abstract does not say in what sense it is complete.
  • Two more unread. E. N. Gilbert, "How things float", American Mathematical Monthly 98 (1991): summaries made by a search tool report it as saying that a cube may have a one-parameter family of attitudes it is indifferent between, which would be the ring above; those are a tool's words and may not be Gilbert's. The magazine piece below has Gilbert among its references. K. Spyrou, "The stability of floating regular solids", Ocean Engineering 257 (2022), which cites both papers of 1992: closed.
  • The beam. "The stable floating orientations of this shape were first computed by Reid" (Anderson and others 2024, of the long square beam; Reid's paper is of 1963 and unread). The abstract of T. Igarashi's paper of 2000 on a long square bar in still water has the two thresholds (printed there as "(3-/3) /6 to 9/32", the page having lost the root sign), between which "the angle φ changes drastically from 0 to 45°"; a paper of 2021 by Feigel and Fuzailov, unread, is said to compare experiment and theory. An unsigned course page at an address of the University of Tennessee, last modified in January 2001: "If the density of the beam is at most (3-sqrt(3))/6, approximately 0.21133, then the beam will float with a side of the square horizontal. ... However, if the density is larger than 9/32, namely 0.28125, then the beam floats with a corner pointing downward. But in between, the beam will do neither", and "I have NOT invented this problem, but saw it many years ago". Part 1 of the 1992 paper treats the prisms. So the two thresholds I called "known" from memory are known, and the tilt between them is in print too: Bogner and Rüde (2012), simulating a square beam that they call a cube, "With the given densities 0.25 and 0.75 a stable angle of heel is found at 26.565◦ from the upright axis of the cube"; a tangent of one half at a density of 1/4 is what the formula on this page gives.
  • A magazine that says otherwise. H. Pollack, "Tip of the iceberg", Physics Today, December 2019: "But a cubic body has well-defined stable orientations relative to the fluid surface that over a wide range of densities do not include the intuitive orientations—floating with a face or edge parallel to the fluid surface or with a corner pointing upward, perpendicular to the surface." It gives no density and no calculation for the cube. This study has a corner up lowest over more than half of all densities. The reader computed it a third way (volumes and moments by inclusion and exclusion over the eight corners, sharing no code with mine or the referee's, on a grid of 41 by 41 directions) and found a corner up lowest at densities of 0.25, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5, a face up at 0.15 and 0.2, and a lean at 0.22. Three computations are not a reading of what the magazine's sources say, which I have not seen.
  • Not found in anything the reader could open: that an edge up is never stable; the density 1/6; the numbers 0.2265, 0.2269 and 0.2377. "Not found" here means not found in open sources, with the likeliest source closed. And 0.2265 is not much of a find: it follows in two lines from the wall-sided formula above. Leaning towards an upright edge, the water plane reaches a bottom corner when tan² = 2d², which with tan² = 12 d (1 − d) − 2 gives 7d² − 6d + 1 = 0, and so 0.2265; leaning towards a face it reaches the bottom edge when tan² = 4d², which gives 8d² − 6d + 1 = 0, the level arc's 1/4. The reader adds a short argument for the 1/6, which I have checked by its numbers and not by hand: below 1/6 the part under water is a corner pyramid of edge (6d)^(1/3), and BM − BG (the water plane's stiffness over the volume, less the height of the centre of gravity above the centre of buoyancy) is (√3/2)((6d)^(1/3) − 1), which is nothing at exactly 1/6. That shows a corner up unstable below 1/6 and neutral at it; that it is stable above rests on numbers (the reader's at 0.167, 0.17 and 0.18, and my search, whose threshold closes on 1/6 as its test circle shrinks: see "Corner up is stable from 0.168" above).
