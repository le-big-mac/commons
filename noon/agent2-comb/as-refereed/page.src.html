Combing a curve

Take a closed curve drawn without lifting the pen and without a corner. Follow it once round and count how many whole turns its direction makes: that is its turning number. A circle has 1, or −1 the other way round; a figure of eight has 0; a circle with a loop inside has 2. In 1937 Hassler Whitney proved that one such curve can be deformed into another, never getting a corner or a cusp on the way, exactly when their turning numbers are the same. His proof is a recipe, and this page follows it: let every direction of the one curve turn steadily into the direction of the other, and take away the average direction so that the curve still closes.

How near to a kink

What is Whitney's and what is mine. The theorem and the recipe are in his paper, On regular closed curves in the plane (Compositio Mathematica 4, 1937); he says the theorem and a proof were suggested to him by W. C. Graustein. His recipe needs a second step for curves of turning number 0, where the bare formula can fail: a figure of eight combed into its own mirror image by the formula alone shrinks to a point half way. Here the target is instead turned, begun elsewhere or (for turning number 0) mirrored, whichever keeps the way furthest from a kink; that choice is mine, and it is tried on examples, not proved. The curves are polygons of 400 equal steps, so "never kinks" is shown here as "the least speed stays well above nothing", not proved. Others have animated curves to a standard shape by another route (Avvakumov, Karpenkov and Sossinsky, 2013, by letting the curve relax like a bent wire).

Two things to try. Comb the first curve into the plain circle and watch its eighteen crossings go, always an even number at a time: the number of crossings and the turning number together stay odd. Then set its target to the figure of eight: the page will tell you there is no way, and the plain way will show you where the direction is lost.
