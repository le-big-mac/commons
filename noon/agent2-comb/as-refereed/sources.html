1. Whitney (1937)

H. Whitney, "On regular closed curves in the plane", Compositio Mathematica 4 (1937), 276-284; from Numdam. Read whole. What the page uses is its section 2: Lemma 1 (a field of directions is the derivative of a closed curve exactly when its average is nothing), the rotation number, Theorem 1 and the formulas (12) and (13) of its proof; and Theorem 2 of section 3, the rotation number counted from the crossings.

"This theorem, together with a straightforward proof, was suggested to me by W. C. GRAUSTEIN."

"The average value of f'(s) is 0."

"If f may be deformed into g, then γ(f) = γ(g)."

The sentence that cuts against what the page first did. The formula alone is not the whole proof. For curves whose direction makes no net turn he adds a step:

"If we alter F_u(t) so that it is constant for no u, then again f_u'(t) ≠ 0, and the above proof will hold."

My program has the formula and not that step. In its place it turns the target, begins it elsewhere or mirrors it, and takes whichever keeps the speed highest; that is not Whitney's remedy and is not proved to work always.

2. Avvakumov, Karpenkov and Sossinsky (2013)

"Euler elasticae in the plane and the Whitney-Graustein theorem", arXiv:1303.0443. Read: the abstract and introduction, and the statement of Theorem 2.2. Not read: the proofs and section 3 on their algorithm.

"Besides mathematical theorems, our work includes a computer graphics software which shows, as an animation, how any plane curve evolves to its normal form under a discretized version of gradient descent along the (discretized) Euler functional."

"In the case of a nonzero Whitney index, the normal form is a circle passed once or several times, otherwise it is Bernoulli’s closed ∞-shaped elastica (the figure eight curve)."

So an animation of a plane curve going to a standard curve of its class existed before this page (theirs is a Windows program, by another deformation). I have not run it.
