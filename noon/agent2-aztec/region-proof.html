1. Where it is in print

Jockusch, Propp and Shor, "Random domino tilings and the arctic circle theorem" (dated 1 March 1995; arXiv math/9801068, version 1 of 1998, pages 9 and 10). They call the polar region the arctic region, a facing pair a bad block, and a filled hole a good block.

  • The region is a staircase. They add 2n + 1 dominoes outside the two upper edges of the diamond, give those and the region's own dominoes a row and a column, and find that the set S of these "has the property that for all (i, j) ∈ S with 1 ≤ i, j ≤ n, both (i − 1, j) and (i, j − 1) are in S", the Ferrers diagram of a partition. (Their S is a set of dominoes and has nothing to do with the S of this page; the added dominoes sit at (0, k) and (k, 0), so their (i, j) is my block (i − 1, j − 1).)
  • It moves whole. "No domino in the arctic region belongs to a bad block. Thus, north-going dominoes in the arctic region slide en masse to form a neighborhood of the “north pole” in ever-larger Aztec diamonds, and remain part of the arctic region forever."
  • Nothing joins it by moving. "No domino can join the arctic region by sliding; the only way the arctic region can grow is by creation of good 2-by-2 blocks."
  • Where it grows. At each inner corner of the staircase there is an empty block after the slide, and "there is a 1/2 chance that this empty block will be tiled by two horizontal dominoes, in which case a new domino will get added to the arctic region". (The file prints the fraction as "21".)

From those to this page's statement is a short step, and the step is mine. A block of the new S holds a slid domino, since a created one shares its block with a second and is not alone; it is joined to the corner through north-going dominoes, so it is in the new arctic region; and having slid and not been created, it was in the old one ("No domino can join the arctic region by sliding", which the paper states and does not argue). The other way, the old region slides whole, each of its dominoes alone in its block and still joined up, so all of it is in the new S. So this page's S at order n + 1 is their arctic region at order n. The four quotations are theirs; this paragraph is not.

2. The reader's proof, written blind (for any tiling, without the growth)

In the reader's notation, lightly edited; its own programs checked each step on all 33,866 tilings of orders 1 to 5, the square of four in a second program written after the blind reply.

Write T(i, j) for the top corner of block (i, j), which is the bottom corner of (i − 1, j), and L(i, j) for its left corner, which is the right corner of (i, j − 1). Then a(i, j) joins T(i, j) and L(i, j); b(i, j) joins T(i, j) and L(i, j + 1); c(i, j) joins T(i + 1, j) and L(i, j); d(i, j) joins T(i + 1, j) and L(i, j + 1).

  • A square of four. If (i − 1, j − 1), (i − 1, j) and (i, j) hold a, so does (i, j − 1). The corner T(i, j − 1) can be covered by four dimers, and three are blocked: b(i, j − 1) needs L(i, j), taken by a(i, j); c(i − 1, j − 1) needs L(i − 1, j − 1), taken by a(i − 1, j − 1); d(i − 1, j − 1) needs L(i − 1, j), taken by a(i − 1, j). The mirror statement, with (i, j − 1) given and (i − 1, j) concluded, goes the same way at the corner L(i − 1, j).
  • P is a staircase. Let Y be the blocks of P whose whole rectangle back to (0, 0) lies in P; Y is a staircase. If P were more than Y, some block p of P outside Y would touch a block y of Y, and p would be below y or to its right. Say p = (i, j) is below y = (i − 1, j). Rows 0 to i − 1 of columns 0 to j lie in P. As p is not in Y, some (i, j′) with j′ < j is not in P; take the largest such j′. Then (i, j′ + 1), (i − 1, j′ + 1) and (i − 1, j′) hold a, so (i, j′) holds a by the square of four, and it touches P, so it is in P: a contradiction. The other case is the mirror.
  • So nothing stands behind. For (i, j) in P with i, j ≥ 1 the block (i − 1, j) is in P and holds a, which takes L(i − 1, j); so (i − 1, j − 1) holds no d.
  • So P is the next S. After the slide a block holds exactly a just when the old block held a and the old block behind it held no d; filling adds only pairs. So the blocks holding exactly a at order n + 1 are the old blocks holding a, less those with a d behind, and P lies among them, joined up. Being inside the old blocks that hold a, their part joined to (0, 0) cannot be more than P.

3. My proof, written first (by induction along the growth)

The words

  • Blocks. A tiling of order n is held as an n by n array of blocks (i, j), row i and column j counted from the corner block (0, 0). A block is a small diamond with four corners: top, left, bottom, right. The bottom corner of (i, j) is the top corner of (i + 1, j); the right corner of (i, j) is the left corner of (i, j + 1).
  • Dimers. A block can hold four dimers: a joins its top and left corners, b its top and right, c its bottom and left, d its bottom and right. In a tiling every corner is covered by exactly one dimer.
  • P, the polar region (the papers' north polar region, for this corner): the blocks that hold a, alone or with d, and are joined to (0, 0) through side-by-side blocks that hold a.
  • S, the frozen corner (this page's): the same with "hold exactly a".
  • A staircase: a set of blocks such that with any block it contains the block above and the block to the left, where those exist.
  • Behind a block (i, j) is the block (i − 1, j − 1).

Three facts follow from "every corner is covered once", and are used throughout.

  • F1. If (i, j) holds a, the block above it holds neither c nor d (its bottom corner is taken) and the block to its left holds neither b nor d (its right corner is taken).
  • F2. If (i, j) holds d, the block to its right and the block below it do not hold a.
  • F3. A block that holds a holds neither b nor c.

The step

One step of the growth (shuffle and fill in aztec.py) takes a tiling of order n, "old", to one of order n + 1, "new". Before any toss:

  • new a at (i, j) exactly when old a at (i, j) and no old d behind it;
  • new d at (i, j) exactly when old d behind it and no old a at (i, j);
  • new b at (i, j) exactly when old b at (i, j − 1) and no old c at (i − 1, j);
  • new c at (i, j) exactly when old c at (i − 1, j) and no old b at (i, j − 1).

Then each hole is filled, by a toss, with a and d together or with b and c together. A hole is one of the blocks that holes in aztec.py picks: empty after the slide, none of its four corners covered, and no two of them overlapping. The proof uses only that a hole is empty.

Lemma 1: in a staircase, nothing stands behind

If P is a staircase, no block of P has a dimer d in the block behind it.

Take (i, j) in P with i, j ≥ 1. The block above it, (i − 1, j), is in P and holds a, which covers its left corner. That corner is the right corner of (i − 1, j − 1), so no d there (F1).

Lemma 2: if P is a staircase, P is the next order's S

Let T be the blocks that hold a after the slide. By the first rule of the step, T is contained in the old blocks that hold a; and by Lemma 1 it contains P. So the blocks of T joined to (0, 0) through T are exactly P: they include P, which is joined up inside T, and they cannot be more than the blocks joined to (0, 0) through all the old blocks that hold a, which is P.

After the fill, the blocks that hold exactly a are the blocks of T: a block of T holds a and not d (the first two rules exclude each other) nor b nor c (F3), filling touches only empty blocks, and a filled block holds two dimers. So S at order n + 1 is the part of T joined to (0, 0), which is P.

The theorem: P is a staircase at every order of every growth

By induction on the order. At order 1 there is one block.

Suppose the old P is a staircase. The new blocks that hold a are T together with F, the holes filled with a and d. Call the new polar region P′.

(α) A block of F has no neighbour holding a to its right or below (F2). Its upper and left neighbours, where they hold a, hold exactly a (F1, F3), so they are in T.

(β) If f = (i, j) is in F and its upper neighbour is in P, so is its left neighbour, if there is one; and the other way round. Say (i − 1, j) is in P and j ≥ 1. Then (i − 1, j − 1) is in P, since P is a staircase. In the old tiling that block holds a and so does its right-hand neighbour, so it holds a alone (F1, F3), and its bottom corner, which is the top corner of (i, j − 1), must be covered from below: by old a at (i, j − 1) or by old b there. (If i = n there is no row below in the old tiling and that corner could not be covered at all, so the case does not arise.) An old b at (i, j − 1) would become a new b at (i, j), by the third rule, unless there were an old c at (i − 1, j), which there is not, since that block holds a (F3). Then (i, j) would not be empty after the slide, and f is a hole. So (i, j − 1) holds old a, and being next to (i − 1, j − 1) it is in P. The other case is the mirror image: (i, j − 1) in P and i ≥ 1 give (i − 1, j − 1) in P, holding a alone because the block below it holds a; its right corner, the left corner of (i − 1, j), is covered by old a at (i − 1, j) or old c there; an old c there would become a new c at (i, j) by the fourth rule, since (i, j − 1) holds a and so no b; so it is old a, and (i − 1, j) is in P.

(γ) The blocks of P′ that lie in T are exactly P. If (0, 0) is in F, it was empty after the slide, so it is not in T, so the old P was empty (P is inside T); and by (α) nothing is joined to (0, 0), so P′ is that one block. Otherwise follow any path of new blocks holding a from (0, 0). While it stays in T it stays in P (Lemma 2). When it steps into a block f of F it comes from an upper or left neighbour, which is in P; by (α) it can only leave through the other one, which by (β) is in P too. So every block of T on the path is in P.

(δ) P′ is a staircase. By (γ), P′ is P together with the blocks of F that have an upper or left neighbour in P (or the single block (0, 0)). For a block of P the blocks above and to the left are in P already. For a block of F in P′ they are in P by (β).

4. What follows, and what this is not

  • In every growth, at every order, P at order n and S at order n + 1 are the same set of blocks. That is the sealed line G1 of region-prediction.md, which I had put at even odds; it was a guess about a statement printed in 1995.
  • No block of the polar region ever has a d behind it, though among all the blocks that hold a that is common; and the polar region is always a staircase, though the blocks that hold a are not as a whole (in 1,450 of the 32,768 tilings of order 5 they are), nor is every other joined-up group of them from its own corner (67,040 of 68,672 at order 5). staircase.out.
  • The same holds at the other three corners with b, c and d, by the symmetry of the step; staircase.out has it for every step between orders 1 and 5 (116,440 steps, each corner). So this page's "frozen fraction" at order n + 1 is the four polar regions of order n over (n + 1)².
  • Not new, and not checked by a person. Who has read it is said in the README, under "The whole region".
