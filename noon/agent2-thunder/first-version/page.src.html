Why thunder rumbles

Lightning is over in a moment, and its whole length makes its sound at once. The sound from each part reaches you when it has had time to travel, so the stroke is read out to you from its nearest point to its farthest. Press Strike and watch the ring: it is the sound you are hearing at that moment, and it marks where on the stroke that sound set out.

Things to try.

  • Drag the listener along the ground. Far off, the first sound comes later (about three seconds a kilometre), and the thunder is quieter, shorter and more even: all rumble and no crack.
  • A bow about you: every part of the stroke is the same distance away, so all of its sound arrives together. That is a clap.
  • Along the cloud: the stroke points away from you, its sound is spread over many seconds, and no two parts arrive together. That is a rumble.
  • A straight one, with "crooked" switched off: almost silent, except where the ring first touches it and at its two ends. The roughness of thunder is the crookedness of lightning.
  • Draw your own, then drag across the sky.

What this is: each point of the stroke gives off one short pulse of pressure, shaped like the letter N and five thousandths of a second long; the pulses travel at 343 metres a second, weaken as one over the distance, lose their high notes to the air as they go, and add up. That is the model of H. S. Ribner and D. Roy (1982) as A. Glassner describes it (2000), and Glassner's article already says that a clap comes from a stretch of channel lying on a circle about the listener. What this leaves out: the stroke is flat, in the plane of the picture; there are no branches, no echoes from hills, no bending of sound by wind and warm air (which is why real thunder is seldom heard beyond some 25 kilometres), and no second and third strokes down the same path. Most of this sound lies below 200 cycles a second, which small loudspeakers hardly give: use headphones if you can.
