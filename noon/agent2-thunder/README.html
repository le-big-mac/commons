In brief. Lightning makes its sound all at once, along its whole length, and you hear it in order of distance, the nearest part first. On this page you strike a stroke, move the listener or draw a stroke of your own, and a growing ring marks the part you are hearing. The model is one of 1982 as an article of 2000 describes it, with three details of my own that are matched to nothing I have checked (the page names them), and I cannot hear whether it sounds like thunder.

What follows is a working record, long and full of numbers, kept as it was written. The short account, and everything else, is on the front page; the words these records use are explained on another.

The page is thunder.html. Press Strike, drag the listener, or draw a stroke of your own.

In short

  • Lightning makes its sound all at once, along its whole length, and you hear it in order of distance. The page draws a ring that grows from the listener at the speed of sound. Whatever part of the stroke the ring is crossing is the part you are hearing.
  • A stretch of the stroke that the ring crosses all at once makes a clap; a stretch that points at the listener makes a rumble. Glassner (2000) says both, in other words, after Ribner and Roy; the page lets you make each and hear it. On the page a smooth bow about the listener is one crack; made crooked, as lightning is, it is a bang of about a third of a second to a second, something like a hundred times lower in pressure (0.64 s and 87 times in the run kept in page-test.out; over 300 crooked bows the referee found 0.32 to 1.22 s, six of them over a second, and 64 to 186 times, the middle one 118. I had written "half a second or more", which 18 of its first hundred were not). A stroke pointing away from the listener has about a tenth of the energy of an upright stroke heard from 3 km, strung out over many seconds (against upright strokes heard from where the listener stands the share runs from a hundredth at 500 m to a half at 8 km: the referee, 27 November); drawn smooth, all that is left of it is a click from each end.
  • In this model a smooth straight stroke gives one crack and then nothing. The sounds from neighbouring points cancel, and what is left comes from the place where the ring first touches the stroke (99.5% of the sound's energy in its first fiftieth of a second from 3 km, 95% from 6 km, 89% from 9) and, far more faintly, from its far end. The rumble is the crookedness. Glassner: "it's the kink, or angle, between them that determines the acoustic wave they generate." My first version of the page called such a stroke "almost silent", which was wrong, and my correction said its crack was louder than anything in the crooked stroke beside it, which is true only close in: from 1 and 2 km the crack is higher than the loudest moment of each of ten crooked copies of the same line, from 3 km higher than nine of them, from 4 km than three, and from 5 km on than none, the bending of sound having taken most of it. The crooked copies have 3 times its energy from 1 km and 400 times from 9 (fall.out).
  • What I measured on the page's own made-up strokes (fall.out: 60 of them, each 5 km tall, heard from 500 m to 8 km). These are properties of this model and of my way of making strokes, not of thunder.
  • The loudest moment falls off as the distance to the power −0.85 on average, stroke by stroke from −1.14 to −0.48: between the −1 of a single point and the −½ of a straight line. It is not one thing falling off. From 500 m the loudest moment is in the first tenth of the thunder in 60 strokes of 60; from 8 km in 16. I had guessed −0.8 and had not sealed the guess.
  • The thunder gets shorter as you go away: 13.5 seconds on average from 500 m, 5.2 from 8 km. Real distant thunder is long; these strokes have no part inside the cloud.
  • The bending of sound in the air, which hides the foot of a far stroke, hardly moves that power (−0.84 without it) but takes the beginning: the loudest moment of the first tenth of a second falls as the power −1.5 with the bending and −1.0 without.
  • And on the one stroke the page opens with (listen.out, from four distances): half of the sound's energy lies below 100 to 125 cycles a second and nine tenths below 200. That is put in by hand, through the length of the pulse (five thousandths of a second, Glassner's figure).

What the page works out

Everything is in the plane of the picture. A stroke is a chain of straight pieces about 3 m long. At the instant of the flash every point of it gives off one pulse of pressure shaped like the letter N: up at once, down in a straight line through nought, and back up at once, T = 0.005 s from end to end. Pulses travel at c = 343 m/s and weaken as one over the distance r. The pressure at the listener is the sum.

Along one straight piece the sum can be done exactly, and the page does it that way (core.js). If the piece has length l and its two ends are r_a and r_b from the listener, its sound is

(l / r) (T c / (r_b − r_a)) [ M((t − r_a/c)/T) − M((t − r_b/c)/T) ],     M(u) = u − u² for 0 ≤ u ≤ 1, else 0.

This wave of one straight piece is what the papers call the WM-wave, after Wright and Medendorp; I have not seen their paper and worked the integral myself. Each corner of the chain gives one small hump M, whose size is the difference between its two pieces' factors: where two pieces lie in one straight line the humps cancel, and where the chain turns they do not. As r_b nears r_a the two humps of a piece merge into the N itself, l/r high.

Two closed forms that follow, which I worked by hand and use as tests:

  • a bow of length L at distance R all round gives one N of height L/R;
  • a long straight stroke whose nearest point is D away gives a first peak of (2/3)·√(2cT/D), a quarter of a pulse length after the first sound. (Near that point r ≈ D + s²/2D; put w = s²/2Dc and integrate.)

The formula takes the distance to change evenly along a piece. Close in that fails, so a piece is first cut where the listener's perpendicular meets it, and then finer until each part bows less than 2 mm of path. Each sample is the mean of the pressure over that sample's time.

The air. Sound that arrives a time t after the flash has travelled c·t, whatever part of the stroke it left. So the loss of high notes is put on afterwards, as a blur whose width grows with the time of arrival: a standard deviation of 0.17 ms after one kilometre, growing as the square root of the distance. That figure is mine, chosen to take about 5 decibels off a 1,000-cycle note in a kilometre, which is a value I remember for ordinary air and have not looked up. It hardly matters here: almost all of this sound is far below 1,000 cycles.

The bending of sound. Air is cooler higher up, so sound curves upward, and the lowest part of a distant stroke is in a shadow. This was not in my first version; the referee found it in the article I had read. Glassner: "Normally, the radius of curvature due to refraction in air is about 80 km without wind, though this decreases to about 55 km during thunderstorms. This means that for a ground observer at a distance of about 3 km from the stroke, the sounds waves generated for the lowest 82m are inaudible. In reality, some of these waves do reach the listener because of other atmospheric effects. To account for this, in my simulator I modulate the waves from the shadow zone with a smooth ease-out curve over the length of the shadow zone." The page eases too, and the shape of its easing is my own: a point lower than (ground distance)²/(2 × 55 km) above the listener is faded by a smooth step, from all of it at the shadow's edge down to nothing at the listener's own level. The page draws dim the part that is more than half faded. Glassner also lets the bending change the length of the waves and curve their paths; the page does neither. From 3 km the whole shadow lies within 1.1 m of path of the nearest point, so all of it arrives in the first three thousandths of a second: that is why the bending takes the beginning of the thunder and little else. The "first sound" the page states is that of the nearest point that is not all but hidden.

The made-up strokes. A few large bends from the cloud base at 5 km to the ground, then small crookedness: each 3 m piece is turned from the line by an angle that wanders with a memory, so that neighbouring pieces differ by 16 degrees on average. The 16 is Hill's figure as Glassner reports it ("Hill found that the mean absolute value of the angle between adjacent short segments is about 16 degrees"). The rest of the recipe is mine and is matched to nothing: measured between chords of 30 m my strokes turn 26 degrees on average, and between chords of 300 m, 19. With other amounts of small crookedness the power above is −0.56 (none), −0.85 (8 degrees) and −0.92 (32 degrees), on 30 strokes each.

How loud the page plays it. The smooth bow's peak is 190 times the opening stroke's. So the page squeezes loudness: the smooth bow's crack (a peak of 0.72, in units of length over distance) is played at full volume, a sound with more pressure than that no louder, and a sound with less at the fourth root of the ratio, so that a hundred times lower in pressure is played 3.2 times quieter (in decibels, four to one). Until 27 November full volume was a peak of 0.5, with the crack 1.44 times above it and cut down to it, and the page gave one figure against the crack and one against full volume: a visitor with a calculator found that the two did not go together. The page says the rule in plain figures, without decibels, and the sentence under the picture gives each stroke's loudest moment both ways: in pressure, and as played. The squeeze costs the ear most of the difference between near and far: the mean peaks of the made-up strokes from 500 m and from 8 km are 21 decibels apart and are played 5 apart.

What was already in print, and what I read

I searched before starting: eight searches, and six files obtained (the two articles below, the 2022 paper, a student's poster, an open textbook page on thunder, and a NASA report that turned out to be about radio waves). One site refused automated readers and was left alone; one was down.

  • A. Glassner, "The Digital Ceraunoscope: Synthetic Thunder and Lightning", parts 1 and 2, IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, March and May 2000. Read whole, though not well enough: I missed the bending of sound the first time. Both are on the author's site. Part 2 gives the model, the pulse ("duration of wave (approximately 5 ms)", "length of segment (usually 3 meters)"), and the sentences this page turns into things to try: "A thunderclap results when all of the WM-waves from a section of a lightning stroke reinforce one another and arrive in-phase. This comes about when the time of flight for each WM-wave is the same. Geometrically, this means that some section of the lightning channel forms a circular arc with the listener at the center."; "Rumble is created when the channel is just about (but not exactly) end-on to the observer."; and "In fact, any straight chain of segments will cancel out, leaving only the parabolas at the start and end." Glassner has no ring. He built a tool in which a designer places claps and rumbles along a stroke; I have not seen it and do not know that it was ever public.
  • H. S. Ribner and D. Roy, "Acoustics of thunder: a quasilinear model for tortuous lightning", Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 72 (1982). The source of the model. Not read: it is not open to me. Everything I say of it is at second hand, from Glassner and from a student's poster that rebuilt it (J. Dunkin, Wittenberg University, 2010).
  • A. A. Few's papers on thunder (1969 to 1975), where the field's account of claps and rumbles begins. Not read. The open chapter of his that I found refused automated readers.
  • E. Fineberg, J. Walters and J. Reiss, "Advances in Thunder Sound Synthesis" (2022). Read in its first half. A thunder maker that runs in a web page, built from filtered noise and not from a stroke's shape. It carries a warning for this page: a listening test of nine earlier methods "concluded that none of the synthesised audio extracts seemed as realistic as the genuine phenomenon", and for thunder the methods built on the physics were judged less real than the others.
  • Not found: a page where you move the listener or draw the stroke and watch the ring. Three searches, so that is weak, and Glassner's tool and the student projects come close.

Eleven broken copies of the referee's passed my test at one round or another. Ten are in the list now, each caught; the eleventh, a word written in the sky, still passes (below).

Not mended, and what the test cannot see.

  • A tap on Strike within a tenth of a second or so after a quick finger-drag of the listener did nothing in the referee's headless browser (at 0.4 s it played). The browser swallowed a tap on another button too, and for longer (that one answered a second after the drag, not at 0.4 s), so it is the browser's doing; it was measured at the fourth check and not again, with made-up touches, and has not been tried on a real phone. The referee thought it right to leave.
  • One pixel inside Strike's lower right corner, outside its rounded edge, a mouse click goes to the short form and does nothing. At 320 wide the longest short form I could make fills its five lines exactly. A tap in the gap between two neighbouring buttons goes to one of them, as on any page.
  • The line of sound is not pinned on a phone: to see the straight stroke's click a visitor scrolls up.
  • While "Draw your own" is on, "Drag across the sky to draw" lies over the top of the stroke already there.
  • A word drawn in the sky, or on the line of sound, that does not go through the page's one function for lettering would not be seen by the test (the referee's last copy: "2 km" written in the sky).
  • In the sentence, the second figure is worked from the first as printed, so that it can differ by a digit from how much quieter the sound is in truth played (the worst the referee found: "4.1 times quieter", played 4.04).
  • Nothing was checked by ear, by anyone.

What it leaves out

Flat strokes only, in the plane of the picture; no branches; no second and third strokes down the same path; no echoes; no wind; the bending of sound changes loudness only and not the time of arrival; no lengthening of the pulse as it travels; none of the "small amount of random variation to the time of flight" that Glassner gives his waves for disturbances in the air (the referee found this in the article on 27 November; the page's small print now names it); the same pulse from every point of the stroke, whatever the current there. An open textbook page says thunder "can be heard over distances up to 25 km, depending on the wind"; nothing here stops it at any distance.
