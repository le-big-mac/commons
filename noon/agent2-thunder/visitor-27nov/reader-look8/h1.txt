Why thunder rumbles

Lightning is over in a moment, and its whole length makes its sound at once. The sound from each part reaches you when it has had time to travel, so the stroke is read out to you from its nearest point to its farthest. Turn your sound on, press Strike and watch the ring: it is the sound you are hearing at that moment, and it marks where on the stroke that sound set out.

The stroke is 6.8 km long. The first sound comes 8.8 s after the flash, from 3.0 km away; the last from 5.7 km, so the thunder lasts 7.7 s. It is loudest 13.2 s after the flash, with about 190 times less pressure than the crack that “A bow about you” makes; it is played about 3.7 times quieter (loudness is squeezed: see the foot of the page). The dim part of the stroke is more than half hidden from you by the bending of sound.

Things to try (press Strike after each).

Drag the listener along the ground, or press where you want to stand. Close in, at half a kilometre, the thunder nearly always begins with its loudest part. Far off, at 8 km, it comes later (about three seconds a kilometre), quieter and shorter, and usually with its loudest part somewhere in the middle. The foot of the stroke goes dim: the bending of sound in the air hides it from you.
A bow about you (it is drawn smooth): every part of it is the same distance away, so all of its sound arrives at once. One crack, as loud as this page plays anything. Now tick “crooked”: the crack becomes a bang of about a third of a second to a second, something like a hundred times lower in pressure than the crack, and is played three to four times quieter.
Pointing away: the stroke lies along your line of sight. Its sound is strung out over many seconds and carries about a tenth of the energy of an upright stroke heard from 3 km, where the page opens, since few stretches of it face you. That is a rumble. Untick “crooked” and all that is left is a click from each end.
A straight one (drawn smooth): one crack, as the ring first touches it, and then next to nothing, with a faint click from its far end. From far off the crack is much weaker and the click is not, so that the crack no longer stands alone: from 9 km the click has a third of the pressure of this stroke’s own crack. The rumble of thunder is the crookedness of lightning: tick “crooked” to give it back.
Draw your own, then drag across the sky.

What this is: each point of the stroke gives off one short pulse of pressure, shaped like the letter N and five thousandths of a second long; the pulses travel at 343 metres a second, weaken as one over the distance, lose their high notes to the air as they go, and add up. Sound also bends upward in the air, which hides the lowest part of a far stroke; that part is drawn dim. The model is that of H. S. Ribner and D. Roy (1982) as A. Glassner describes it (2000), and Glassner's article already says that a clap comes from a stretch of channel lying on a circle about the listener. Three things in it are this page's own, matched to nothing that has been checked: how fast the air takes the high notes away, how the hidden part fades, and most of the recipe for its made-up crooked strokes. What this leaves out: the stroke is flat, in the plane of the picture; there are no branches, no echoes from hills, no wind, none of the small random delays that Glassner gives the sound for the unsteadiness of the air, and no second and third strokes down the same path; and here the bending of sound only hides the foot of a stroke, where in Glassner's program it also lengthens the waves and curves their paths. Most of this sound lies below 200 cycles a second, which small loudspeakers hardly give: use headphones if you can. The page squeezes loudness, or the quiet thunder could not be heard beside the loud: a sound a hundred times lower in pressure is played about three times quieter. How it was made and checked.
