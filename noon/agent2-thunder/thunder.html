Why thunder rumbles

Lightning is over in a moment, and its whole length makes its sound at once. The sound from each part reaches you when it has had time to travel, so the stroke is read out to you from its nearest point to its farthest. Press Strike and watch the ring: it is the sound you are hearing at that moment, and it marks where on the stroke that sound set out.

Things to try.

  • Drag the listener along the ground, or press where you want to stand. Close in, at half a kilometre, the thunder begins with its loudest part. Far off it comes later (about three seconds a kilometre), quieter and shorter, and usually with its loudest part somewhere in the middle. The foot of the stroke goes dim: the bending of sound in the air hides it from you.
  • A bow about you (it is drawn smooth): every part of it is the same distance away, so all of its sound arrives at once. One crack, the loudest thing on this page. Now tick "crooked": the crack becomes a bang of half a second or more, about a hundred times lower in pressure (the page plays it about three times quieter).
  • Pointing away: the stroke lies along your line of sight. Its sound is strung out over many seconds and is much the quietest here, about a tenth of the energy of an upright stroke, since few stretches of it face you. That is a rumble. Untick "crooked" and all that is left is a click from each end.
  • A straight one (drawn smooth): one crack, as the ring first touches it, and then nothing. The rumble of thunder is the crookedness of lightning: tick "crooked" to give it back.
  • Draw your own, then drag across the sky.

What this is: each point of the stroke gives off one short pulse of pressure, shaped like the letter N and five thousandths of a second long; the pulses travel at 343 metres a second, weaken as one over the distance, lose their high notes to the air as they go, and add up. Sound also bends upward in the air, which hides the lowest part of a far stroke; that part is drawn dim. All of this is the model of H. S. Ribner and D. Roy (1982) as A. Glassner describes it (2000), and Glassner's article already says that a clap comes from a stretch of channel lying on a circle about the listener. What this leaves out: the stroke is flat, in the plane of the picture; there are no branches, no echoes from hills, no wind, and no second and third strokes down the same path. Most of this sound lies below 200 cycles a second, which small loudspeakers hardly give: use headphones if you can. The page squeezes loudness four to one, or the quiet thunder could not be heard beside the loud: a sound ten decibels weaker is played two and a half decibels quieter. How it was made and checked.
