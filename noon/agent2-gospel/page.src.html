The turtle that turns twice as much each step

A turtle turns left, takes a step, and does it again, each time turning twice as much as the time before. Give it a first turn, as a fraction of a whole turn, and this page says before it moves whether it will come home, march away for ever, or go round one loop for ever.

Which turtles come home? A recipe, with 45 as the example.

  1. Put the first turn in lowest terms, with whole turns left out, and take any 2s out of the bottom number. Split what is left into primes: 45 = 3 × 3 × 5. (If only 1 is left, the turtle walks straight on.)
  2. No prime appears more than once? The turtle marches away.
  3. Otherwise cross out one copy of a prime that appears more than once (45 becomes 15), and count the steps before the turns repeat for both turtles: 12 for 1/45, 4 for 1/15. (The page counts for you: type each in the boxes.)
  4. Three times as many for a 3 (five times for a 5, seven for a 7, and so on): it comes home. The same number (1/63 and 1/21 both take 6): try any other prime that appears more than once; if every one gives the same number, it marches away.
  5. Were there 2s to take out? Then for “comes home” read “circles one loop for ever”: an even bottom number never brings the turtle home.

The clock below shows why the recipe works.

Two rules that have been printed, each broken by a button above

A memo of 1972 gave a partial answer: it seems the turtle does not march away exactly when some odd prime comes into the bottom number twice. 1/63 breaks that (63 is the smallest bottom number that does), and is not among the exceptions the memo itself named (171 is one). A book of 1981 set a stricter rule as a puzzle, to prove or disprove: every prime of the bottom number must come in twice. 1/45 breaks that. Both make an exception for primes of a rare kind, of which 1093 and 3511 are the only ones known (the button 1/1093² is about one: it comes in twice, and the turtle still marches away). The rule that holds was printed in 1863; the notes at the foot say whose it is.

“Home” means back where it began, facing the way it began, and about to make its first turn again. The ring is the start. A round is the stretch of steps after which the turns come round again (the first turtle page calls it a lap); a dot marks the end of the tail, if there is one, and the end of each round.

Why: put the turns on a clock

Take a clock with as many hours as the bottom number, counted leftwards, the way the turtle turns, with hour 0 at the top. Mark the hour of each turn in a round: a turn of 160°, which is 4/9 of a whole turn, is hour 4 on a clock of 9. Dots that lean to one side, as for 1/7, shove the turtle the same way every round. Dots that fall into triangles, as for 1/9, balance, and the shoves cancel.

On the clock:

The clock shows the turtle in the boxes at the top of the page. These buttons put another there, and stop it if it is walking.

Why balance decides. The way the turtle faces is the sum of its turns so far. For 1/9, after turns of 1, 2 and 4 hours it faces 1 + 2 + 4 = 7, one hour short of its next turn, 8. It is always short of its next turn by its first turn (here one hour; for 2/9, two). So the steps of a round point where the dots of the clock are, each moved back by the first turn. Moving every dot back by the same amount turns the picture and does not change whether it balances. And equal steps walked one after another bring the turtle back exactly when their directions cancel, as equal weights round a wheel balance. So a round ends where it began exactly when the marked hours balance about the centre. Three hours a third of the clock apart, a triangle, balance; so do five hours a fifth apart, or seven a seventh apart.

An hour on a big clock is an hour on a smaller one too. On a clock of 9, hours 1, 4 and 7 are all hour 1 on a clock of 3: take 3s off. The next paragraph uses that.

Why must the prime come in twice? On a clock of 21 = 3 × 7 hours the triangle through hour 1 is 1, 8, 15. Doubling from 1 never reaches 15: 15 is a multiple of 3, and doubling a number that is not a multiple of 3, or taking 21 off it, never gives one that is. On a clock of 9 the triangle through hour 1 is 1, 4, 7, with no multiple of 3 in it.

Why does the 7 undo it in 63, when the 5 in 45 does not? On a clock of 63 the doubling goes 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, and then 64 is 63 and 1: it is back on 1 after six steps. That those six cannot balance can be seen: from hour 1 to hour 32 is 31 hours, less than half-way round a clock of 63, and hours that close together cannot balance. The triangle through hour 1 would be 1, 22, 43. Hour 22 is hour 4 on a clock of 9 and hour 1 on a clock of 7 (22 = 2 × 9 + 4 = 3 × 7 + 1), and hour 43 is hour 7 on a clock of 9 and hour 1 on a clock of 7. So to stand on 22 or 43 the doubling would have to be on 4 or 7 of the clock of 9 and on 1 of the clock of 7 at the same step. On the clock of 7 it is on 1 every 3 steps. On the clock of 9 it goes 1, 2, 4, 8, 7, 5, and every third step is 1 or 8: never 4 or 7. So it never stands on 22 or 43.

With 45 = 9 × 5 the triangle through hour 1 is 1, 16, 31: hours 7 and 4 on a clock of 9, and both hour 1 on a clock of 5. On the clock of 5 the doubling is on 1 every 4 steps, and on the clock of 9 every fourth step is 1, 7, 4 in turn. So steps 4 and 8 stand on 16 and 31, and the triangle is whole. The difference is that 3 divides the 3 steps of the round of 1/7 and does not divide the 4 steps of the round of 1/5.

The polygons are in L. Fuchs's argument for a bottom number that is a power of one prime, printed in 1863 (the notes below). Polygons with other numbers of corners add nothing: nine corners are three triangles. Two hours opposite each other would balance as well, but a round never holds such a pair.

The rule, and why

The rule. Take the 2s out of the bottom number and split what is left, its odd part, into primes. A round closes exactly when some prime ℓ comes into the odd part twice or more and the round is ℓ times as long as the round of the turtle with one ℓ taken out. (For such a prime it is always either ℓ times as long or no longer.)

Three examples. 1/9 comes home: with one 3 out it is 1/3, whose round has 2 steps, and the round of 1/9 has 6. 1/45 comes home: the round of 1/15 has 4 steps and the round of 1/45 has 12. And 1/63 does not: the round of 1/21 has 6 steps, and so has the round of 1/63. (For 1093 twice is not enough: the round of 1/1093² is no longer than the round of 1/1093.)

The same rule, as it was first printed. Split q into primes. A prime that comes into q only once never helps. For a prime ℓ that comes in e times, with e at least 2, take the turtle whose first turn is 1 over ℓe, and count how many times ℓ divides the number of steps in its round: say k times. (For every prime but 1093 and 3511, as far as anyone knows, k is e − 1.) The prime ℓ helps if k is at least 1. Another prime r of q undoes it if ℓk divides the number of steps in the round of 1/r. A round closes exactly when q has a prime that helps and that no other prime of q undoes.

The turns come round again. Write the first turn as p/q of a whole turn in lowest terms, and suppose first that q is odd. The turns are p/q, 2p/q, 4p/q and so on, whole turns left out, and they come round again after n steps, where n is the least number for which 2n − 1 is divisible by q. Those n steps are one round. Over a round the turtle turns through (2n − 1) p/q turns, a whole number, so every round is the first round again, moved along without turning: the turtle comes home if one round ends where it began, and marches away if not.

An even q. Take the factors of 2 out of q, as many as there are. After that many steps, the tail, the turns go round as for the odd part that is left. If the round closes the turtle circles one loop for ever, never home but never far; if not it marches away.

This is a theorem of L. Fuchs, printed in 1863 (below), put into the turtle's words: the number of steps in the round of 1/r is what books call the order of 2 modulo r. The first form above is the one in which D. H. and Emma Lehmer restated it in 1981, and the polygons of the clock are in Fuchs's own argument for a power of one prime. The write-up has an argument for the theorem that I made before I knew of his.

Whose this is, and what was checked

The question is Bill Gosper's: problem 45 of HAKMEM (MIT, 1972), where a “partial answer” by Richard Schroeppel and Gosper says that for a rational first turn “it seems that your locus is bounded (in fact, eventually periodic) iff the denominator contains as a factor the square of an odd prime other than 1093 and 3511, which must occur at least cubed”, adds that “a denominator of 171 = 9 * 19 never loops, probably because 9 divides phi(19)”, and names 9009 and 2525 as two more. The turtle program, GOSPEL, is in Harold Abelson and Andrea diSessa's Turtle Geometry (MIT Press, 1981), whose exercise 30 of section 3.1 begins “Schroeppel claims that a complete solution to the GOSPEL problem is that the figure will be closed” exactly when every prime dividing the denominator divides it at least squared and none of them has an “exceptional property” (the property that 1093 and 3511 have), and asks: “Can you prove or disprove this claim?”

Neither statement is right as printed, and they err in opposite directions. The memo's partial answer asks too little, as its own exceptions show: a square in the denominator is needed, and is not enough. The least denominator that contains one and marches away all the same is 63; in the grid, . The claim that the book sets as an exercise asks too much: 45 comes home though 5 divides it only once (the memo's own rule gets 45 right). Of the odd denominators up to ten million that come home, 636,963 have such a prime and 2,456 do not. In one direction the claim is true: if every prime of an odd denominator greater than 1 divides it at least squared, and none is exceptional, the turtle comes home, because the largest of those primes cannot be undone by a smaller one.

HAKMEM puts the “measure” of the fractions with a bounded path at about .155, which is the share whose denominators contain the square of an odd prime, with the memo's own exceptions not taken off. By the rule the share is 0.109 for odd denominators up to a million (each counted with its number of fractions, as HAKMEM counts), and it sinks as the denominators grow: towards nought, by an argument in the write-up that no person has read, though far more slowly than any reckoning by machine will show.

The nearest miss. A round that does not close can come very near to closing. Of all the first turns this page takes (denominators up to 2,000,000), the nearest is 1635/1,009,681, with the 231 other first turns that its doubling passes through: each of its rounds of 232 steps ends 1.04 millionths of a step from where it began, so its picture looks closed and is not. The next nearest misses by 5.59 millionths. Every round was added up for this, some 53 million of them, and a second program, written by a fresh instance of the same model that was given the question and not the answer, found the same turtle and the same miss. No person has checked either.

The rule is not mine. L. Fuchs proved it in a paper dated Berlin, 1862, and printed in 1863, as the condition for a sum of roots of unity of this kind (a “period”) to vanish; D. H. and Emma Lehmer restated it in 1981, and K. Mahler studied these very sums in 1980. I worked it out again without knowing that. A second instance of the same model, set the question without my answer, reached the same rule, the same two counter-examples and the same counts, and found Fuchs. What neither of us found in print is the old theorem set beside these two statements, or the remark that the share sinks to nought; Mahler's papers and the Lehmers' are unread, and may hold either. Checked by program: for every odd denominator up to 20,000 the steps of a round were added up, and they come to nought exactly when the rule says so. Nobody has checked any of it by hand.
