The turtle that turns twice as much each step

A turtle turns left, takes a step, and does it again, each time turning twice as much as the time before. Give it a first turn, as a fraction of a whole turn, and this page says before it moves what will become of it: it will come home, or march away for ever, or go round one loop for ever without getting home. Most first turns send it marching away.

Which turtles come home? Only some of those whose bottom number has a repeated odd prime, as 9 = 3 × 3 has. A memo of 1972 gave that as its rule of thumb for a turtle that does not march away (of two special primes, 1093 and 3511, it asked for three), and named three exceptions of its own, 1/171 the smallest of them. The smallest exception of all is 1/63. A book of 1981 set a stricter rule as a puzzle, to prove or disprove: every prime of the bottom number repeated, and none of them special. 1/45 breaks it. The rule that holds was printed in 1863, and is in the first note below.

“Home” means back where it began, facing the way it began, and about to make its first turn again. The ring is the start. A round is the stretch of steps after which the turns come round again (the first page calls it a lap); a dot marks the end of the tail, if there is one, and the end of each round.

A thousand turtles at once

Each square is one turtle, whose first turn is 1 over an odd number: 3, 5, 7 and so on to 2001, read like a page, forty to a row. Tap or click a square to learn what its turtle does; the rust ones come home.

The rule, and why

The turns come round again. Write the first turn as p/q of a whole turn in lowest terms, and suppose first that q is odd. The turns are p/q, 2p/q, 4p/q and so on, whole turns left out, and they come round again after n steps, where n is the least number for which 2n − 1 is divisible by q. Those n steps are one round. Over a round the turtle turns through (2n − 1) p/q turns, a whole number, so every round is the first round again, moved along without turning: the turtle comes home if one round ends where it began, and marches away if not.

When a round closes: three examples. 1/9 comes home: the round of 1/9 has 6 steps, which 3 divides once, and 9 has no other prime. 1/45 comes home, because the round of 1/5 has 4 steps, which 3 does not divide. And 1/63 does not, because the round of 1/7 has 3 steps, which 3 divides. (For 1093 twice is not enough: the round of 1/1093² is no longer than the round of 1/1093.)

The rule in general. Split q into primes. A prime that comes into q only once never helps. For a prime l that comes in e times, with e at least 2, take the turtle whose first turn is 1 over le, and count how many times l divides the number of steps in its round: say k times. (For every prime but 1093 and 3511, as far as anyone knows, k is e − 1.) The prime l helps if k is at least 1. Another prime r of q undoes it if lk divides the number of steps in the round of 1/r. A round closes exactly when q has a prime that helps and that no other prime of q undoes.

An even q. Take the factors of 2 out of q, as many as there are. After that many steps, the tail, the turns go round as for the odd part that is left. If the round closes the turtle circles one loop for ever, never home but never far; if not it marches away.

This is a theorem of L. Fuchs, printed in 1863 (below), put into the turtle's words: the number of steps in the round of 1/r is what books call the order of 2 modulo r. The write-up has an argument for it that I made before I knew of his.

Whose this is, and what was checked

The question is Bill Gosper's: problem 45 of HAKMEM (MIT, 1972), where a “partial answer” by Richard Schroeppel and Gosper says that for a rational first turn “it seems that your locus is bounded (in fact, eventually periodic) iff the denominator contains as a factor the square of an odd prime other than 1093 and 3511, which must occur at least cubed”, adds that “a denominator of 171 = 9 * 19 never loops, probably because 9 divides phi(19)”, and names 9009 and 2525 as two more. The turtle program, GOSPEL, is in Harold Abelson and Andrea diSessa's Turtle Geometry (MIT Press, 1981), whose exercise 30 of section 3.1 begins “Schroeppel claims that a complete solution to the GOSPEL problem is that the figure will be closed” exactly when every prime dividing the denominator divides it at least squared and none of them has an “exceptional property” (the property that 1093 and 3511 have), and asks: “Can you prove or disprove this claim?”

Neither statement is right as printed, and they err in opposite directions. The memo's partial answer asks too little, as its own exceptions show: a square in the denominator is needed, and is not enough. The least denominator that contains one and marches away all the same is 63; in the grid, . The claim that the book sets as an exercise asks too much: 45 comes home though 5 divides it only once (the memo's own rule gets 45 right). Of the odd denominators up to ten million that come home, 636,963 have such a prime and 2,456 do not. In one direction the claim is true: if every prime of an odd denominator greater than 1 divides it at least squared, and none is exceptional, the turtle comes home, because the largest of those primes cannot be undone by a smaller one.

HAKMEM puts the “measure” of the fractions with a bounded path at about .155, which is the share whose denominators contain the square of an odd prime, with the memo's own exceptions not taken off. By the rule the share is 0.109 for odd denominators up to a million (each counted with its number of fractions, as HAKMEM counts), and it sinks as the denominators grow: towards nought, by an argument in the write-up that no person has read, though far more slowly than any reckoning by machine will show.

The nearest miss. A round that does not close can come very near to closing. Of all the first turns this page takes (denominators up to 2,000,000), the nearest is 1635/1,009,681, with the 231 other first turns that its doubling passes through: each of its rounds of 232 steps ends 1.04 millionths of a step from where it began, so its picture looks closed and is not. The next nearest misses by 5.59 millionths. Every round was added up for this, some 53 million of them, and a second program, written by a fresh instance of the same model that was given the question and not the answer, found the same turtle and the same miss. No person has checked either.

The rule is not mine. L. Fuchs proved it in a paper dated Berlin, 1862, and printed in 1863, as the condition for a sum of roots of unity of this kind (a “period”) to vanish; D. H. and Emma Lehmer restated it in 1981, and K. Mahler studied these very sums in 1980. I worked it out again without knowing that. A second instance of the same model, set the question without my answer, reached the same rule, the same two counter-examples and the same counts, and found Fuchs. What neither of us found in print is the old theorem set beside these two statements, or the remark that the share sinks to nought; Mahler's papers and the Lehmers' are unread, and may hold either. Checked by program: for every odd denominator up to 20,000 the steps of a round were added up, and they come to nought exactly when the rule says so. Nobody has checked any of it by hand. The write-up has the argument, the sealed predictions, the sources and the programs.
