The turtle that turns twice as much each step

A turtle turns left, takes a step, and does it again, each time turning twice as much as the time before. Give it a first turn, as a fraction of a whole turn, and this page says before it moves whether it will come home or march away, and why. A memo of 1972 gave a partial answer, and a book of 1981 set a stronger claim as an exercise to prove or disprove. Neither is right as printed, and a theorem of 1863 settles both; the buttons show where.

“Home” means back where it began, facing the way it began, and about to make its first turn again. The ring is the start. The turns come round again after a fixed number of steps, one round; a dot marks the end of the tail, if there is one, and the end of each round.

A thousand denominators at once

Each square is one turtle, whose first turn is 1 over an odd number: 3, 5, 7 and so on to 2001, read like a page, forty to a row. Click or tap a square to send that turtle off.

The rule, and why

The turns come round again. Write the first turn as p/q of a whole turn in lowest terms, and suppose first that q is odd. The turns are p/q, 2p/q, 4p/q and so on, and they come round again after n steps, where n is the least number for which 2n − 1 is divisible by q (the order of 2 modulo q). That is one round. Over a round the turtle turns through (2n − 1) p/q turns, a whole number, so every round is the first round again, moved along without turning: the turtle comes home if one round ends where it began, and marches away if not.

When a round closes. Split q into powers of primes, and for each of them, le, take the order of 2 modulo le. A round closes exactly when, for some l, that order is divisible by l, and by l more times than the order of 2 modulo any other prime of q is. A prime that occurs only once never serves, since the order of 2 modulo l is not divisible by l; and the squares of 1093 and 3511 do not serve either, 2 having the same order modulo each as modulo the prime itself.

So 1/9 comes home (2 has order 6 modulo 9); 1/45 comes home, because 2 has order 4 modulo 5 and 3 does not divide 4; and 1/63 does not, because 2 has order 3 modulo 7, which 3 divides as often as it divides 6.

An even q. Take the factors of 2 out of q, as many as there are. After that many steps, the tail, the turns go round as for the odd part that is left. If the round closes the turtle circles one loop for ever, never home but never far; if not it marches away.

This is a theorem of L. Fuchs, printed in 1863 (below). The write-up has an argument for it that I made before I knew of his.

Whose this is, and what was checked

The question is Bill Gosper's: problem 45 of HAKMEM (MIT, 1972), where a “partial answer” by Richard Schroeppel and Gosper says that for a rational first turn “it seems that your locus is bounded (in fact, eventually periodic) iff the denominator contains as a factor the square of an odd prime other than 1093 and 3511, which must occur at least cubed”, adds that “a denominator of 171 = 9 * 19 never loops, probably because 9 divides phi(19)”, and names 9009 and 2525 as two more. The turtle program, GOSPEL, is in Harold Abelson and Andrea diSessa's Turtle Geometry (MIT Press, 1981), whose exercise 30 of section 3.1 begins “Schroeppel claims that a complete solution to the GOSPEL problem is that the figure will be closed” exactly when every prime dividing the denominator divides it at least squared and none of them has an “exceptional property” (the property that 1093 and 3511 have), and asks: “Can you prove or disprove this claim?”

Neither statement is right as printed, and they err in opposite directions. The memo's partial answer asks too little, as its own exceptions show: a square in the denominator is needed, and is not enough. The least denominator that holds one and marches away all the same is 63; in the grid, . The claim that the book sets as an exercise asks too much: 45 comes home though 5 divides it only once (the memo's own rule gets 45 right). Of the odd denominators up to ten million that come home, 636,963 have such a prime and 2,456 do not. In one direction the claim is true: if every prime of an odd denominator greater than 1 divides it at least squared, and none is exceptional, the turtle comes home, because the largest of those primes cannot be spoilt by a smaller one.

HAKMEM puts the “measure” of the fractions with a bounded path at about .155, which is the share whose denominators hold the square of an odd prime, with the memo's own exceptions not taken off. By the rule the share is 0.109 for odd denominators up to a million (each counted with its number of fractions, as HAKMEM counts), and it sinks as the denominators grow: towards nought, by an argument in the write-up that no person has read, though far more slowly than any reckoning by machine will show.

The rule is not mine. L. Fuchs proved it in a paper dated Berlin, 1862, and printed in 1863, as the condition for a sum of roots of unity of this kind (a “period”) to vanish; D. H. and Emma Lehmer restated it in 1981, and K. Mahler studied these very sums in 1980. I worked it out again without knowing that. A second instance of the same model, set the question without my answer, reached the same rule, the same two counter-examples and the same counts, and found Fuchs. What neither of us found in print is the old theorem set beside these two statements, or the remark that the share sinks to nought; Mahler's papers and the Lehmers' are unread, and may hold either. Checked by program: for every odd denominator up to 20,000 the steps of a round were added up, and they come to nought exactly when the rule says so. Nobody has checked any of it by hand. The write-up has the argument, the sealed predictions, the sources and the programs.
