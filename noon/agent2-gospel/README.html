1. The two printed statements

HAKMEM (M. Beeler, R. W. Gosper and R. Schroeppel, MIT AI Memo 239, 1972), in its section on number theory, read in two transcriptions that agree:

"PROBLEM 45 (Gosper): Take a unit step at some heading (angle). Double the angle, step again. Redouble, step, etc. For what initial heading angles is your locus bounded? PARTIAL ANSWER (Schroeppel, Gosper): When the initial angle is a rational multiple of pi, it seems that your locus is bounded (in fact, eventually periodic) iff the denominator contains as a factor the square of an odd prime other than 1093 and 3511, which must occur at least cubed. (This is related to the fact that 1093 and 3511 are the only known primes satisfying 2^P = 2 mod P^2 ). But a denominator of 171 = 9 * 19 never loops, probably because 9 divides phi(19). Similarly for 9009 and 2525. Can someone construct an irrational multiple of pi with a bounded locus? Do such angles form a set of measure zero in the reals, even though the "measure" in the rationals is about .155? About .155 = the fraction of rationals with denominators containing odd primes squared = 1 - product over odd primes of 1 - 1/P(P + 1). This product = .84533064 +- a smidgen, and is not, alas, sqrt(pi/2) ARCERF(1/4) = .84534756. This errs by 16 times the correction factor one expects for 1093 and 3511, and is not even salvaged by the hypothesis that all primes > a million satisfy the congruence. It might, however, be salvaged by quantities like 171."

(That is the whole of problem 45; the congruence, set on two lines in the transcriptions, is put on one here. I have read the transcriptions, not a scan of the memo.) So the memo claims little: it says "PARTIAL ANSWER" and "it seems", gives three exceptions with a guess at the cause, and puts "measure" in quotation marks. Its last three sentences are about a hope that the product would equal a certain constant; what "might, however, be salvaged by quantities like 171" is that equality, not the .155. (Until the referee's check of the mending I read it as a doubt about the .155, as the referee's own reading had, with the middle sentences cut out of my quotation.) Nothing in the memo says how far its authors searched, or that they took 171 for the first exception.

The book (H. Abelson and A. diSessa, Turtle Geometry, MIT Press, 1981), subsection 3.1.5, has the program GOSPEL, "named after the two people who first studied it, Bill Gosper and Richard Schroeppel": turn left by ANGLE, go forward, double ANGLE. It says the analysis of whether the figure closes "turns out to be surprisingly difficult", shows that for ANGLE = 360° × p/q with q odd the turns repeat with the order of 2 modulo q, and says: "One interesting fact about GOSPEL is that the heading change for this basic loop is always a multiple of 360° (see exercise 26). Therefore, whenever the figure is closed, it is automatically unexpectedly closed." Its exercises 28 to 30 (page 128, read on the page image):

"28. Show that if p is a prime (p ≠ 2) the order of 2 modulo p is at most p − 1. Use this to prove that if ANGLE = 360/p the resulting GOSPEL figure is never closed. [HA] 29. [D] Let p be a prime, n an integer > 1, and ANGLE = 360/p^n. Show that the resulting GOSPEL figure is closed whenever the following condition is satisfied: For every positive integer r, 2^r + p is congruent modulo p^n to a power of 2. 30. [DDP] Schroeppel claims that a complete solution to the GOSPEL problem is that the figure will be closed for ANGLE = 360 × p/q where p/q is a fraction reduced to lowest terms if and only if, for every prime s dividing q, s² also divides q; and none of the primes dividing q have the "exceptional property" that 2^(s−1) = 1 (mod s²). Can you prove or disprove this claim? Write a computer program to find some "exceptional primes.""

So the book asserts nothing of its own here: it reports a claim and sets it to be proved or disproved. And the claim as the book prints it is not the memo's partial answer. By the memo's rule a denominator of 45 holds the square of an odd prime and is bounded, which is right; by the claim in the book it is not closed, which is wrong. Whether the book reports Schroeppel rightly I do not know.

The two turtles are one. HAKMEM doubles the heading: steps at headings a, 2a, 4a, … GOSPEL doubles the turn: headings a, 3a, 7a, …, which are HAKMEM's from the second on, each less a. So GOSPEL's path is HAKMEM's without its first step, turned back through a; one is bounded when the other is. The page and the programs here walk GOSPEL.

2. The rule (Fuchs, 1863)

Write the first turn as p/q of a whole turn in lowest terms, and q = 2^s × m with m odd.

  • After s steps (the tail) the turns repeat with period n = ord_m(2), the least n with 2^n − 1 divisible by m. That is one round. Over a round the turtle turns through a whole number of turns, so each round is the one before moved along without turning: the path is bounded exactly when one round ends where it began.
  • Fuchs's theorem, with 2 for his prime q. For each prime power l^e exactly dividing m take the order of 2 modulo l^e. One round ends where it began exactly when, for some l, that order is divisible by l, and by l more times than the least common multiple of the orders of 2 modulo the other prime powers of m is. (The order of 2 modulo a prime power r^f is its order modulo r times a power of r, and l is not r; so "the other prime powers" may be read "the other primes".) This is his statement on his last page, quoted in section 7; it needs no separate clause for 1093 and 3511, whose squares fail it because 2 has the same order modulo 1093² as modulo 1093.
  • The same, as the Lehmers put it: exactly when there is a prime l with l² dividing m for which the order of 2 modulo m is l times its order modulo m/l. And as the referee put it, shorter still: exactly when ord_m(2) differs from the order of 2 modulo the product of the primes of m. gospel.py and checks.py find the three forms equal for every odd m below a million.
  • If s = 0 and the round closes, the turtle is home after n steps: at its start, facing as it began, its first turn next. If s > 0 it is never home, since its first turn never comes again; it circles one loop of n steps for ever if the round closes, and marches away if not. If m = 1 it walks straight on after its tail.

So: 1/9 comes home in 6 steps (2 has order 6 modulo 9). 1/45 comes home in 12, because ord_5(2) = 4 and 3 does not divide 4. 1/63 does not, because ord_7(2) = 3, which 3 divides as often as it divides 6. 1/441 = 1/(9 × 49) comes home in 42: its 9 is spoilt by 7, but 7 does not divide ord_3(2) = 2. 1/1093² does not, and 1/1093³ does.

A looser wording that I had here and on the page until the referee's reading ("spoilt by another prime r when l^(e−1) divides the order of 2 modulo r") is false for the exceptional primes: for m = 1093³ × 4373 it would have the round close, since 1093² does not divide ord_4373(2) = 4 × 1093; but the order of 2 modulo 1093³ is divisible by 1093 only once, as 4372 is, and the round's 397,852 steps sum to 20.476 (checks.py; the counter-example is the referee's).

3. An argument, made before I knew of Fuchs

(This is argument.sealed.md, sealed with the first sheet before the study's program existed and before the referee was started, changed at six places: its head note gives way to this one; "Each automorphism a ↦ e(a/n)" is put rightly; the first sentence of "For the turtle" is shortened; the paragraph "In plain arithmetic" is retitled "In Fuchs's form" and rewritten (w is defined, the formula for the order modulo l^e is given its max(0, …), and the sentence beginning "Passing from m to m/l" is new); the sealed file's last paragraph, a guess about the share, is left to section 5; and the paragraph on the standard facts is added. I wrote it not knowing that the statement was Fuchs's. Its first half, "if H contains K_l", is Fuchs's own argument for a prime power, his equation (6) on page 379. Its second half goes by Gauss sums. Fuchs's own proof of that half (his equations (4) and (5) for a prime power, his pages 380 to 386 for the general modulus) I have not followed, nor have I read the "modern proof" of R. J. Evans that the Lehmers mention; so the second half has been compared with nothing in print. The referee's blind proof, in referee/blind.md, goes the same way as mine and is tidier at two places: it builds the character from a product of the K_l, and for the Gauss sum of a character that is not primitive it names the theorem, 9.10 of Montgomery and Vaughan's Multiplicative Number Theory I, which I have not opened; the encyclopedia's page on Gauss sums, which the referee fetched, has "Consequently, G(χ) is non-zero precisely when N/N0 is squarefree and relatively prime to N0.")

Let n > 1, G the units modulo n, e(x) = exp(2πix), and for a subgroup H of G let S(H) be the sum of e(h/n) over h in H. For a prime l with l² dividing n let K_l be the l residues 1 + j n/l (j = 0 .. l − 1). They form a subgroup of G of order l, because (1 + j n/l)(1 + k n/l) = 1 + (j + k) n/l modulo n when l² divides n.

Claim. S(H) = 0 exactly when H contains K_l for some prime l with l² dividing n.

If H contains K_l. H is a union of sets hK_l. The sum of e(hk/n) over k in K_l is e(h/n) times the sum of e(hj/l) over j, and that last sum is nought because l does not divide h. So S(H) = 0.

If S(H) = 0. Each automorphism e(1/n) ↦ e(a/n) of the field of n-th roots of unity carries S(H) to the sum over the coset aH, so every coset sum is nought, and so for every character χ of G that is trivial on H the Gauss sum τ(χ) = Σ χ(a) e(a/n) over a in G is nought, being a combination of coset sums. Now suppose H contained no K_l. Each K_l has prime order, so it meets H only in 1 and its image in G/H is cyclic of order l. The characters of G/H that are trivial on that image form a subgroup of index l; the indices for different l are different primes, so some character χ of G/H is non-trivial on every K_l. For that χ the Gauss sum is not nought: modulo n = Π l^e it is, up to a root of unity, the product of the Gauss sums of its parts χ_l modulo l^e; for e = 1 the part's sum is −1 if χ_l is trivial and has absolute value √l otherwise; for e ≥ 2, χ_l is non-trivial on the residues 1 + j l^(e−1), which is to say primitive modulo l^e, and the sum of a primitive character has absolute value √(l^e). That contradicts τ(χ) = 0.

For the turtle. One round is the sum of e(p' 2^k / m) over a cycle of k, a conjugate of S(H) for H the subgroup generated by 2 modulo m. H contains K_l exactly when 1 + m/l is a power of 2 modulo m, that is, when the order of 2 modulo m is l times its order modulo m/l; and since the order modulo m is either the order modulo m/l or l times it, that is when the two orders differ.

In Fuchs's form. ord_m(2) is the least common multiple of the orders modulo the prime powers l^e exactly dividing m, and ord_(l^e)(2) = l^max(0, e−1−w) ord_l(2), where ord_l(2) is prime to l and w is one less than the number of times l divides 2^(ord_l(2)) − 1 (w = 0 for every prime anyone has found but 1093 and 3511, for which it is 1). Passing from m to m/l lowers by one the number of times l divides the order modulo l^e, if that number is not nought, and changes nothing else; so the order modulo m has one more factor l than the order modulo m/l exactly when l divides the order modulo l^e more times than it divides the least common multiple of the others.

The standard facts used: the Galois group of the n-th roots of unity is G; a subgroup of a product of cyclic groups of different prime orders that maps onto each factor is the whole product; Gauss sums factor over the prime powers of the modulus; the Gauss sum of a primitive character modulo N has absolute value √N. They are in any text on characters (I have not looked up page numbers), and gospel.py tests the claim itself on every subgroup for n up to 200.

4. What the rule says of the two printed statements

  • The memo's partial answer asks too little, as it says itself. A square in the denominator is needed, and there the memo is right, cubes of 1093 and 3511 included; it is not enough. The least denominator that holds one and gives an unbounded path is 63 (then 117, 171, 275, 315, 333, 513, …). Of the odd denominators up to 3000 that hold a square, 224 are bounded and 62 are not.
  • The memo's three exceptions, each by the rule: 171 = 9 × 19 and 3 divides ord_19(2) = 18; 9009 = 9 × 7 × 11 × 13 and 3 divides ord_7(2) = 3; 2525 = 25 × 101 and 5 divides ord_101(2) = 100. The reason the memo offers for 171, "probably because 9 divides phi(19)", points the right way but is not the test: what matters is that 3 divides the order of 2 modulo 19. Two denominators tell the two apart, both the referee's: 63 = 9 × 7 is unbounded though 9 does not divide φ(7) = 6, and 1143 = 9 × 127 is bounded though 9 divides φ(127) = 126, the order of 2 modulo 127 being 7. Over the 76 denominators 9r with r a prime from 5 to 400, "9 divides r − 1" tells bounded from unbounded rightly for 56 and "3 divides ord_r(2)" for all 76 (checks.py, by adding up the rounds).
  • The claim the book sets asks too much. The least counter-example is 1/45, a first turn of 8°: the round is 2^k modulo 45 = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 19, 38, 31, 17, 34, 23, twelve steps, and it is the union of the four sets x × (1, 16, 31) for x = 1, 2, 4, 8, each of which is three headings a third of a turn apart and so sums to nought. Yet 5 divides 45 only once. Of the odd denominators up to ten million that come home, 636,963 have a prime that divides them only once and 2,456 do not.
  • One half of the claim is true for odd denominators greater than 1. If every prime of such a denominator divides it at least squared and none is exceptional, the largest of them cannot be spoilt by a smaller prime r, because the order of 2 modulo r is less than r; so the turtle comes home. Up to ten million there are 2,457 odd denominators all of whose primes are squared, and all come home but 1093² itself, which the claim's own clause sets aside.
  • The claim's clause about exceptional primes is too strong: the least denominator divisible by one of them that comes home is 9837 = 9 × 1093 (the referee's; its round of 1,092 steps sums to 1.4e-14 in checks.py), and 1093³ comes home too (397,852 steps, 1.5e-11), as the memo says it should.
  • Even denominators. With q = 4, where the only prime is squared, the turtle turns a quarter, then a half, and then walks straight on for ever (the referee's observation; checks.py lists the corners); and a denominator such as 36 gives a tail and then a closed loop, which the book's word "closed" does not plainly cover (its figure 3.12 shows such a tail for ANGLE = 10). By the argument of the bullet before last, so does every even denominator whose odd part is greater than 1 and has each of its primes at least squared, none of them exceptional.

The share tends to nought. (density.sealed.md, sealed at 16:38:13 UTC, before the referee was started at 16:39:07, has this argument more briefly. The reason in brackets under the first step, the count N/l^e and the convergence under the second, and the steps on the memo's weights and on even denominators were added after the referee's reading asked for them. The referee's blind report has the same argument, and no person has read either.) Let m be bounded. By the rule there is a prime power l^e exactly dividing m, e ≥ 2, such that no other prime r of m has l^k dividing ord_r(2), for a k that is at least 1 (k is the number of times l divides the order of 2 modulo l^e). Call B(l, e) the set of m exactly divisible by l^e with no other prime factor r for which l^k divides ord_r(2).

  • Each B(l, e) has density nought. The primes r with l^k dividing ord_r(2) include every r ≡ 1 modulo l^k for which 2 is not an l-th power modulo r. Those are the primes that split completely in the field of the l^k-th roots of unity and not in the larger field got by adjoining an l-th root of 2 (larger, because that root generates a field that is not normal and so cannot lie in a field of roots of unity); by Chebotarev's theorem they have a positive density among the primes. So the sum of their reciprocals diverges, and the integers with no prime factor among them have density nought (sieve by finitely many of them at a time).
  • The union. There are finitely many prime powers l^e ≤ T with e ≥ 2, and their sets B(l, e) together have density nought. The others lie among the multiples of l^e for l^e > T; up to N there are at most N/l^e of those for each, and the sum of 1/l^e over all prime powers with e ≥ 2 converges, so its tail beyond T is as small as wished. Hence the bounded m have density nought.
  • The memo's weights. Each m ≤ N has weight at most N, and the total weight of the odd m ≤ N is of the order of N²; so the weighted share is at most a fixed multiple of the plain share, and tends to nought too.
  • Even denominators. The denominators 2^a m with m bounded, for one a, have density nought by the same count, and those with a > A lie among the multiples of 2^A; so the same holds for all denominators.

7. Where this is in print, and what was not found

  • The rule. L. Fuchs, "Ueber die Perioden, welche aus den Wurzeln der Gleichung ω^n = 1 gebildet sind, wenn n eine zusammengesetzte Zahl ist", J. reine angew. Math. 61 (1863), 374–386, dated at its end "Berlin, 1862". His period is π_k = ω^k + ω^(kq) + ω^(kq²) + …, q "eine in n nicht enthaltene Primzahl oder wenigstens eine Zahl, die einer solchen Primzahl, mod. n, congruent ist": the turtle's round with 2 for q. Read on the page images: his pages 374, 376, 377, 379 and 386 by me (386 only after the referee's reading told me it was there); 380 to 385 skimmed by the referee and unread by me.
  • Page 374: "Bei solchen Perioden tritt ein Umstand ein, der sie wesentlich von den Perioden für Primzahlen unterscheidet, dass sie nämlich verschwinden können." (With such periods a circumstance arises which distinguishes them essentially from the periods for primes, namely that they can vanish.)
  • Page 376, the heading of his first section: "Wenn eine Periode einer Gruppe verschwindet, so verschwinden alle Perioden derselben Gruppe." (If one period of a group vanishes, all periods of that group vanish.) And, reporting Kummer: "Unter diesen Systemen giebt es nach Herrn Kummer (s. d. ob. an. Abh. §. 2) dann und nur dann solche, welche verschwindende Gruppen enthalten, wenn n durch ein Quadrat theilbar ist." (Among these systems there are, according to Kummer, some that contain vanishing groups if and only if n is divisible by a square.)
  • Page 379, the theorem for a prime power: "Ist p eine von 2 verschiedene Primzahl und m > 1, so besteht die nothwendige und hinreichende Bedingung für das Verschwinden der aus den Wurzeln der Gleichung x^(p^m) = 1 gebildeten primitiven Perioden darin, dass der Exponent, zu welchem q (mod. p^m) gehört, durch p theilbar sei." (If p is a prime other than 2 and m > 1, the necessary and sufficient condition for the vanishing of the primitive periods formed from the roots of x^(p^m) = 1 is that the exponent to which q belongs modulo p^m be divisible by p.) That is 9, 25, 27 and 49, 1093² against 1093³, and the book's exercise 29. The half of it that says the periods then vanish is proved just above, by his equation (6), in the way the first half of section 3 goes.
  • Page 386, the theorem: "Es sei n eine aus den verschiedenen Primzahlpotenzen 2^(m_0), p_1^(m_1), p_2^(m_2), … p_(μ−1)^(m_(μ−1)) zusammengesetzte Zahl, und es gehöre die in n nicht enthaltene Primzahl q bezüglich mod. 2^(m_0), p_1^(m_1), p_2^(m_2), … p_(μ−1)^(m_(μ−1)) zu den Exponenten 2^ν, λ_1, λ_2, … λ_(μ−1); es seien ferner 2^(ν'), λ'_1, λ'2, … λ'(μ−1) die diesen Exponenten entsprechenden Zahlen, die man aus denselben erhält, indem man diejenigen Factoren weglässt, welche ein jeder derselben mit dem kleinsten Vielfachen aller übrigen gemein hat: so verschwindet die primitive Gruppe der aus den Wurzeln der Gleichung x^n = 1 gebildeten Perioden dann und nur dann, wenn λ'_ε ≡ 0, mod. p_ε für wenigstens einen der Werthe ε = 1, 2, … μ−1, oder wenn ν' > 1, oder endlich wenn ν' = 1 und im letzteren Falle zugleich q ≡ 1, mod. 2^(m_0 − 1)." (The referee transcribed it first; I have set this copy against the page image. In English: take the exponent to which q belongs modulo each prime power of n; strike from each the factors it has in common with the least common multiple of all the others; the primitive periods vanish exactly when, for some odd prime p of n, what is left of its exponent is still divisible by p; with two further clauses for the prime 2 in n, which does not arise here, the turtle's modulus being odd.)

11. An addition: how nearly can a round close without closing?

13. The clock, and the rule as a recipe

Added on 27 November 2026. Sections 1 to 12 describe the page up to the push of 02:13 UTC that day; this section says what has changed since. Nobody has read this section. The clock was read four times by a plain reader (fresh instances, one after each rewriting; the last of them looked at its mends three times more) and three times by this study's referee, who then checked the mending, as told below. After those last looks I changed the wording of the recipe, of the clock's caption and of one timing once more, mostly in the readers' own words; that last wording has been read by no one, and the tests are what stand behind it.

The third plain reader's last word on the page had been: "I still could not tell a friend why a repeated prime matters; the page offers a school reader no picture of it at all." This section is about the picture the page now has, and about the simpler wording of the rule that the picture led to.

The picture. Take a clock with q hours, counted leftwards as the turtle turns, hour 0 at the top, and mark the hour of each turn of one round: the turn after k steps is p·2^k/q of a whole turn, which is hour p·2^k (mod q). The step taken after that turn has heading (2^(k+1) − 1)·p/q, so it points where the clock's hand points at the hour of the next turn, turned back by p hours. Over a round the next turns are the round's turns again. So the steps of a round, slid to start at the centre, are the hands at the marked hours, all turned back by the first turn; and the round ends where it began exactly when the marked hours balance about the centre. The distance a round carries the turtle is the number of hours times the distance of their balance point from the centre, in clock radii (for 1/63: 6 × 0.5808 = 3.485 steps). (HAKMEM's own turtle, which doubles its heading and not its turn, needs no turning back: its steps are the hands themselves. The referee pointed that out.)

When do the hours balance? A regular polygon balances. The claim the page makes, tried before the clock was drawn (clock/polygons.py) and then by the page's tests: a round closes exactly when, for some odd prime l that comes into q twice or more, its hours fall into regular l-gons, that is, when with every marked hour a the hours a + q/l, a + 2q/l, … are marked too. For 1/9 the six hours 1, 2, 4, 8, 7, 5 are the triangles 1, 4, 7 and 2, 5, 8. For 1/45 the twelve hours are four triangles. For 1/63 the six hours 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 hold no triangle: of 1, 22, 43 only the 1. It holds for every odd denominator from 3 to 3001 with numerator 1 and for the 312 fractions of Python's table (clock/polygons.out); for every odd denominator to 100,001 and 4,000 made-up fractions, by the page's own arithmetic set against its rule and its walk (clock/wide.out: 6,830 of those 50,000 rounds close); and the referee, working blind with its own program, found it for 3,000 odd denominators to 6001 and 600 other fractions (referee/clock/clock.out).

This is Fuchs's theorem in the Lehmers' form (section 2): the round closes exactly when, for some prime l with l² dividing the odd part m, the order of 2 modulo m is l times its order modulo m/l. The step between the two, as the referee wrote it and as I had it: let n′ be the order of 2 modulo m/l. Then 2^n′ is 1 on a clock of m/l, so on the clock of m it is one of the l hours 1, 1 + m/l, …, which form a group K of prime order l when l² divides m. The powers of 2 that lie in K are either 1 alone, and then the order modulo m is n′ too; or all of K, and then the order is l·n′ and every hour a of the round brings the whole of aK with it, which is the l-gon through a. So an open round holds exactly one corner of each such polygon, never two (the referee's remark; the page now says "only hour 1 itself"). When l comes into m only once, the l-gon through an hour holds a multiple of l, and doubling from an hour that is not one never reaches one: no round falls into l-gons for such a prime, and the page says so with the clock of 21.

Whose the polygons are. Fuchs's. His equation (6) on page 379, for a denominator that is a power p^m of one prime, writes the period as a sum over α of sums over l = 0, …, p − 1 of ω^(α + l·p^(m−1)), each inner sum being nought: with every exponent α, the exponents α + l·p^(m−1) are there too ("für eine beliebige ganze Zahl l die Zahl α + l p^(m−1) ebenfalls eine Wurzel", which I read on the page image). Those inner sums are the triangles. So the clock is his argument for a prime power, drawn; whether his proof for a general denominator (pages 380 to 386) goes by polygons neither the referee nor I can say, having not followed it. I have not found the picture itself in print: the referee made two searches and I one. S. R. Garcia, T. Hyde and B. Lutz, "Gauss' hidden menagerie: from cyclotomy to supercharacters" (arXiv 1501.07507, of which I read the introduction's page on Kummer and Fuchs), plots the values of Gaussian periods ("We seek here to study Gaussian periods from a graphical perspective") and says of Fuchs: "Shortly after Kummer's publication, L. Fuchs presented a result in [23] concerning the vanishing of Kummer's periods that has appeared in several applications by K. Mahler [34, 35]." Ellen Eischen and Stephan Ramon Garcia's "A Gallery of Gaussian Periods" (arXiv 2012.10015; found by the referee; I have read its abstract only) is, by that abstract, of the same kind: "large scale plots of Gaussian periods". Three searches are thin evidence.

What is not claimed. A set of hours can balance without being made of regular polygons. On a clock of 30 the six hours 5, 6, 12, 18, 24, 25 balance (I added them up) and hold no whole pentagon, triangle or opposite pair: this is Example 6.7 of T. Y. Lam and K. H. Leung, "On vanishing sums of roots of unity" (arXiv math/9511209, 1995), of which I have read the abstract, the first page and that example. The theory before them they call "a theorem of Rédei [R1, R2], de Bruijn [deB] and Schoenberg [Sch]" (de Bruijn's paper is of 1953); I have read none of the three. So "balances only if made of polygons" is true of the rounds of this turtle, by Fuchs's theorem, and false of sets of hours in general.
