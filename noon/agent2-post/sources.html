Sources

Each source was fetched to a folder of its own (kept outside this folder, not republished) and read as text. The quotations below are checked against those texts by checkfacts.py (quotes.json).

  1. Blind play. W. Goddard, "Static Mastermind" (the copy at rosenbaum-games.de, which carries no date; the year 2003 is from its address and from a search tool), read through the introduction, the table and the statements of the theorems. "Static mastermind is like normal mastermind, except that the codebreaker must supply at one go a list of questions (candidate codes), the answers to which must uniquely determine the secret code." "In particular we confirm that g(4, 6) = 6 (the original version)." Of the search behind that: "Even with all this symmetry, the original 6 4 game took several days running on a typical desktop PC." Of the symmetry: "it is well known that there are up to symmetry only 5 codes in the original 64 game; after a code such as 1123 there are 130 codes up to symmetry" (my openings give the same 5 and 130). Its table of g for small boards, as I read it from the page's layout (rows are colours 2 to 6, columns 2, 3 and 4 positions): 2, 2, 3 / 2, 3, 3 / 3, 4, 4 / 4, 4, 5 / 4, 5, 6. And in the text: "We saw earlier that g(3, k) = k for 2 ≤ k ≤ 4."
  2. The page that table's starred entries come from. A. Bogomolny and D. Greenwell, "Invitation to Mastermind" (cut-the-knot.org), read in the parts on the static game. On the problem: "In [1], Chvatal mentions a problem, suggested by Pierre Duchet, of finding the minimum number of guesses the codebreaker needs to make all at once, without waiting for the answers, to determine the code." On the standard game, before Goddard: the work on ordinary play "strongly suggests that the static problem can't be done in five guesses, but no proof of this is known". And a correction: "On Feb 12, 2003, Petr Felzmann pointed out that the entry for 4 colors / 3 pegs must be 3 instead of the original 4. For example, the set of guesses (0, 0, 1), (0, 2, 2), (3, 1, 2) determines the code uniquely." With the colours renamed that is the list my program found (112, 133, 423). Goddard's paper has the entry as 4.
  3. Ordinary play. I did not fetch Knuth's paper (D. Knuth, "The computer as master mind", J. Recreational Math. 9 (1976), as cited in item 4). What I use is one sentence of item 4, and my own run: "Knuth showed that a minimax strategy guarantees guessing the hidden vector in no more than 5 turns". That five cannot be bettered I had from memory; collect.out has it by search.
  4. A paper on variants, read in its abstract and introduction: A. Berger, C. Chute and M. Stone, "Query Complexity of Mastermind Variants", arXiv:1607.04597. It has the game with no answers until the end ("when feedback is not received until all queries have been submitted") and nothing between that and ordinary play.
  5. The rule of the lag. The delay is that of A. Ambainis, S. A. Bloch and D. L. Schweizer, "Delayed Binary Search, or Playing Twenty Questions with a Procrastinator" (SODA 1999; Algorithmica 32 (2002) 641–651). I have not read it: the title and the reference are a search tool's report, and the bibliography site refused my client. The rule as I use it is from a Polish magazine's account of the same problem (Delta, "Informatyczny kącik olimpijski (65): Opóźnione wyszukiwanie", 2013), read in its first page: "odpowiedź na i-te pytanie dostajemy dopiero po zadaniu pytania numer d + i" (the answer to the i-th question arrives only after question number d + i has been asked). Added at 09:58 UTC, after publication: I have now read the account to its end. It gives the answer for twenty questions: with a delay of d, the longest stretch of numbers that k questions settle obeys "ℓd (k) = 1 + ℓd (k − d − 1) + ℓd (k − 1)", which for a delay of 1 gives "ℓ1 (k) = Fk − 1, gdzie Fk jest k-tą liczbą Fibonacciego" (the Fibonacci numbers less one), and it says "Okazuje się, że powyższa strategia jest optymalna." (it turns out that this strategy is the best), sending the reader to the 1999 paper for the proof. Its example of where such a delay arises: teachers who must set next week's homework before this week's is marked.
