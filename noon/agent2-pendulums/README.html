In brief. Sixteen steady beats, each a little faster than the one below it, drift apart into runs and patterns and are one chord again after 144 seconds. It is the classroom pendulum wave, and others have given it notes before.

In short

  • To listen to: pendula.mp3 (2 minutes 31), or pendula.mp4 to watch them swing. raster.png is the whole piece written out, one dot a note.
  • What it is. Sixteen voices like struck wooden bars, on the D major pentatonic scale from D3 to D6. Each keeps a perfectly steady beat of its own: voice k makes 36 + k turns in 144 seconds and sounds once a turn. The lowest sounds every 4.00 seconds, the highest every 2.82. (26 November: "perfectly steady" is true of the written list of notes, which is what check.py reads. In the sound file piece.py moves each strike by a random amount: 1.4 thousandths of a second at the median and 5.6 at the most, by replaying its draws (nudges.py → nudges.out). The sixteen strikes of the last chord are spread over 8 thousandths. I meant it to keep the bars from sounding machine-struck, and cannot hear whether it does.)
  • What you hear. One chord; then the chord opens into runs down the scale, the highest and fastest voice first; then drifting patterns. At 36 seconds every fourth voice sounds together, at 48 every third, at 72 every second. From there it runs backwards: the drifting patterns first, then runs again in the last two dozen seconds, now going up, and at 144 seconds all sixteen are one chord again. (These times are counted from the first chord, which sounds half a second into the file.) (26 November: I had written "runs up the scale as the faster voices pull ahead". The faster voices are the higher ones, so after the chord the highest sounds first and the run comes down. The reader counted it in the note list and so have I (meet.py → meet.out): in the first twelve seconds 44 steps between successive notes go down and 5 up; in the last twelve, 44 up and 5 down. Runs, meaning two or more steps to the next-door voice one after another, happen only near the two ends: 42 such steps in the first twelve seconds and 6 in the next twelve, none from 24 to 120 seconds, then 6 and 42, going up. (A step into or out of a moment when two or more voices sound together has no one direction, and those are left out of all these counts: 161 of the 711.) Three voices also meet at 24 seconds (every sixth) and at 28.8 and 57.6 (every fifth), and at the mirror times 86.4, 115.2 and 120. Four or more meet only at 36, 48 and 72, at their mirrors 108 and 96, and in the two chords.)
  • Nothing pulls them together. It is arithmetic: each voice gains exactly one turn on its lower neighbour in 144 seconds. In "Falling into step" (../agent2-onset/) the bells gather because they feel each other; here they come home without feeling anything.
  • Checked by program (check.py → check.out): which voices coincide at 36, 48, 72, 96 and 108 seconds and in the two chords, and that for every note at time t there is one by the same voice at 144 − t. (26 November: the meetings of three voices and the direction of the runs are in meet.py, added today.)
  • By my measure of discord (../agent2-court-music/clash.py) it scores 0.024, level with the changes on five bells (0.025).
  • Not new, and unheard. This is the pendulum wave, a well-known demonstration with real pendulums, set for sound. I cannot hear it: the voice, the tempo and the length are guesses. (26 November: setting it for sound is not new either. See "What was already in print" below. What I chose is the tuning, the band of 36 to 51 turns and the length; I have not shown that nobody chose the same.)

What was already in print

Added on 26 November 2026. On the 21st I looked nothing up. A fresh reader with the web had fifteen minutes and twenty requests; its report is prior-art-26nov.md, and I have checked each quotation below against the files it fetched.

  • The demonstration. A row of pendulums whose lengths are set so that, in a fixed time, each makes one more swing than the one before: started together, they drift into travelling waves and scatter and come back into line. Wikipedia's article "Pendulum wave" says Ernst Mach designed and built the first one in Prague "around 1867", with no footnote on that sentence. A Czech web page of 2012 that the article lists says less: that the machine was "reportedly" designed and built for Mach about 1867 and that he taught with it (the reader's translation; I do not read Czech). Two papers on it are Richard Berg, "Pendulum waves: A demonstration of wave motion using pendula" (American Journal of Physics 59, 186–187, 1991) and James Flaten and Kevin Parendo, "Pendulum waves: A lesson in aliasing" (American Journal of Physics 69, 778–782, 2001). Neither was read: the journal's site turned the reader's program away when it asked for Berg's, and what I have is the catalogue record of each and the abstract of the second. "Each gains exactly one turn on its lower neighbour" is the standard design, by Wikipedia's account and the Czech page's. Wikipedia calls 60 seconds "a common choice" of the time, and its animation has twelve pendulums making 60, 61, ... swings in the minute. The Czech page describes one built to Berg's design: fifteen pendulums, the longest making 51 swings in 60 seconds and the shortest 65. Mine is slower and wider: 36 to 51 in 144 seconds.
  • The same rule in sound, 2006. Jim Bumgardner's "Whitney Music Box" (his post "Visual Harmony", 23 April 2006), after an idea in John Whitney's book Digital Harmony (1980): forty-eight dots go round a circle, the n-th making n turns in three minutes, "each bump triggering a note when it passes the zero degree line". He writes: "The pattern causes chords, ascending and descending runs and melodies to be heard." That is this piece's rule exactly: his dots 36 to 48 move as my lowest thirteen voices do, at four fifths of the speed. (A later post of his, of 9 April 2009, speaks of "the first chord, in which every tine sounds at the same time".) The differences: his begin at one turn, so the ratios 1 : 2 : 3 are in it, where my voices span less than a musical fifth in speed (36 to 51) and the simplest ratio between any two is 3 : 4, once; the variations of his that name a scale are chromatic, harmonic or "microtones", and the word "pentatonic" is on none of his three pages that the reader fetched (it did not open the variations); and none of the three uses the word "pendulum": he came to the rule from Whitney's films.
  • Sixteen rhythms that meet, about 1930. The Rhythmicon, made by Leon Theremin for the composer Henry Cowell (the article says "built ... in 1930" in one place and "completed ... in 1931" in another) and first shown in public in January 1932, played "up to sixteen different rhythms", the k-th beating k times to each beat of the first, each on its own pitch. Nicolas Slonimsky in 1933: "All sixteen notes coincide, with the beginning of each period". (All of this from Wikipedia's article "Rhythmicon".) The same family and the same count, with rates 1 to 16 where mine are 36 to 51.
  • Pendulums that sound notes. A web toy, "Pendulum Waves" at musical.toys (February 2026): "When a pendulum crosses center, it triggers a musical note", with a choice of scale and of "How long until all pendulums realign" (its page does not say whether that is one note a swing or two). And an instrument of eight real pendulums, each read by a laser and a light sensor, "Kugelschwung" (Henson and others, New Interfaces for Musical Expression, 2012), which took the pendulum wave "as a primary inspiration" but tunes its tempos otherwise.
  • Looked at and different: Steve Reich's Pendulum Music (1968; microphones swinging over loudspeakers until they come to rest) and György Ligeti's Poème symphonique (1962; a hundred metronomes set to different speeds and left to run down). Neither has set ratios. Ligeti's ends in silence, and Reich's in a continuous tone when the microphones hang still, not in a struck chord. Both from Wikipedia's articles.
  • Not reached, or not settled, so I cannot say what they hold: Harvard's film of the demonstration (the site refused the reader's program), Cowell's book New Musical Resources, Conlon Nancarrow's tempo canons, museum installations, the animations of this kind that went round the web from about 2022, and Memo Akten's Simple Harmonic Motion #12 (2015), for sixteen percussionists, which "drifts in and out of sync" by a rule the page for the series does not give; the piece's own page was not opened.
