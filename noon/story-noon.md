# The Tercentenary Clock

*a short story (agent2), from eight of my fifty words: detective, misdirection, tercentenary, noon,
anteater, reticence, confections, unhurt*

The town of Varrow had been founded, by its own account, three hundred years ago at noon, and it
meant to mark the hour exactly. For this it had the clock: a brass automaton in the square, built
for the bicentenary, which every day at twelve sent a procession of twelve founders out of one door
and in at another. For the tercentenary the council had paid a confectioner to add a thirteenth
figure, the founders' anteater. The founders were said to have arrived with one, and nobody now
alive could say why.

At a quarter to eleven on the day, the anteater was gone from its bracket.

Inspector Odile Marsh was in the square already, being off duty and fond of clocks. The mayor
found her beside the lemonade.

"It is sugar," he said. "Spun sugar over a wire frame, gilded. It took him four months. It cannot
be replaced by noon and the bishop is here."

"Who has been inside the clock?"

"No one. The case is locked. I have the key." He showed her: on a chain, around his neck, warm.

Marsh looked at the clock for some time. The case was sound. The glass was unbroken. The bracket,
when the mayor let her in, was bare and a little sticky. On the floor of the mechanism, among the
weights, lay a single gilded claw.

She interviewed three people, because three people wanted to be interviewed.

The confectioner, Pell, wept, and said he had enemies in the trade. The clock-winder, a girl of
sixteen named Bette, said that she had wound the clock at seven as always and that the anteater
had been there then, and that she had not touched it, and she said this twice. The bishop said
that an anteater had no place among founders and he was not sorry, and then, seeing her notebook,
said that he was of course sorry.

"The bishop, then," said the mayor, at twenty past eleven.

"The bishop wants me to look at the bishop," said Marsh. "People who have done a thing are not
usually so pleased to be suspected of it. He is enjoying this. It is the first time anyone has
listened to his views on the anteater."

"Pell's enemies."

"Pell's enemies would have smashed it where the town could see. Somebody took great care that
there should be nothing to see at all." She turned the claw over in her handkerchief. "Bette. You
said you didn't touch it. I believe you. What did you do at seven, exactly?"

The girl's reticence lasted about as long as it takes to count to five.

"I wound it. And I — it was cold in the case, I'd thought it'd be cold today, with the wind. And
I've always, on cold days, I light the little spirit lamp under the going-train, so the oil runs.
My grandfather did. Only today wasn't cold."

Marsh looked up. From the lamp bracket a warm-air channel ran up the inside of the case; the
bicentenary clockmaker had cut it to keep the founders' pivots from seizing. It ended in a small
brass grille. Directly over the grille, added four months ago by a man who knew sugar and did not
know clocks, was the thirteenth bracket. The lamp had been burning under it since seven.

"It didn't go anywhere," Marsh said. "It's all still here. It's on the weights, and down the back
of the third founder, and I should think a fair amount is in the oil."

The mayor sat down on the step of the clock.

"Nobody stole it," he said.

"Nobody stole it. You had a locked case and a missing thing, and so you sent for a detective, and
a detective looks for a hand. That was the misdirection, and nobody even intended it." She gave
the claw to Pell, who held it as one holds a tooth. "Bette is unhurt, the clock is unhurt, and you
have forty minutes."

"To do what? There is no anteater."

Marsh considered the square: the bunting, the bishop, the lemonade, the four hundred people who
had come to see a thing happen at noon.

"What did the founders' anteater do, in the story?"

"Nobody knows. It is only said that they arrived with one."

"Then at noon," said Marsh, "twelve founders will come out of the door, and after them an empty
bracket. And you will tell the town that this is the most accurate thing the clock has ever
shown them: that there was an anteater, and that it is not known what became of it."

At noon the founders came out. The thirteenth bracket followed them round, gleaming slightly. The
bishop was seen to nod. Bette, who had been told she would wind the clock for the next fifty years
on the single condition that she never again improve it, counted the strokes under her breath, and
got twelve.
