0. In short

  • The frame of the study is in print: Cohen, Darbois-Texier, Dupeux, Brunel, Quéré and Clanet, "The aerodynamic wall" (Proc. R. Soc. A 470, 2014) names the two governing parameters, the parabola and "Tartaglia" regimes, the logarithms and the best elevation falling below 45°. La Rocca and Riggi (Physics Education, 2009) is titled "were the Medievals right after all?".
  • Galileo himself (1638, Fourth Day) said a gun's shot exceeds its terminal speed and that this makes "the beginning of the parabola flatter and less curved than the end". Robins (1742) gave the musket and 24-pound comparison with measured ranges, and the vertex nearer the fall.
  • The history is wrong in one place: Tartaglia said the path "never shows any perfectly straight part" in the Nova scientia itself, not first in 1546. The front-page "Before Galileo, a cannonball was drawn…" is too broad.
  • The README's numbers are right for its model; my own integrator reproduces them to the digits printed. Not found in print: the area measure, the ten-degree "keeping its line" share, and the apex-fraction values.
  • "As the logarithm of the speed" is loose: the apex gain per decade of speed falls 0.09, 0.05, 0.03.

1. Claims, written before any search (file step1-claims.txt)

(a) Physics - P1. In the model, at a given elevation, launch speed over terminal velocity decides the shape. Presented as a headline "in this model"; the last section says nothing is new. - P2. Below a ratio of about 0.3 the 45° range is within 7% of vacuum and the apex within 0.01 of half way. Stone (ratio 0.32): 236 m, 0.928, apex 0.508, lands 47.0°, best 44°. Computed. - P3. Musket ball (18 mm lead, 450 m/s, terminal 68 m/s, ratio 6.61): 1,056 m against 20.6 km (0.051), lands 80.8° at 0.95 terminal, apex 0.690. Computed. - P4. 24-pound shot (10.9 kg, 450 m/s, ratio 2.87): 3,778 m, 0.183, lands 73.0°, apex 0.632. Computed. - P5. Shuttlecock (60 m/s, terminal 6.8 m/s, ratio 8.82): 11.6 m, 0.032, apex 0.707, lands 82.6°. Computed, as a point. - P6. Area under the path scaled to unit range: 0.167 parabola, 0.5 straight-out-straight-down; stone 0.172, shot 0.278, musket 0.334 ("exactly half way"), shuttlecock 0.350. Own measure; not said new. - P7. Steep fall only at high elevation and at the end: steeper than 80° for the last 6% of the drop; chord 59°; lands 69° at best elevation, 32° at 10°, 14° at 5° and faster than terminal. Computed. - P8. Apex fraction grows "only as the logarithm of the speed": 0.690, 0.807, 0.857, 0.887 at ratios 6.6, 100, 1,000, 10,000; plus the range-fraction and impact-angle rows. Computed; no formula. - P9. Share of range before the direction drops ten degrees: 0.15 parabola, 0.16, 0.36, 0.48, 0.51; in metres the reverse (506 m against 3,096 m). Computed. - P10. "J9 is a theorem": drag opposing velocity only reduces sideways speed, so descent is steeper than ascent at every height and the apex is past half way. No credit. - P12. Three-part figure: apex at (S + r)/(S + 2.414 r). Referee's geometry. - P13. Sphere drag: 0.47; about double near Mach 1; falls above a Reynolds number of a few hundred thousand; effects on range. Remembered.

2. What is in print

P1: FOUND. Sure. - Galileo 1638, Two New Sciences, Fourth Day (Crew and de Salvio 1914, pp. 255–257; dl/06): "this terminal or final speed [terminata velocita] is the maximum which such a heavy body can naturally acquire in falling through the air. This speed I estimate to be much smaller than that impressed upon the ball by the burning powder." Mortars "using small charges and imparting no supernatural momentum… follow their prescribed paths very exactly." - Robins 1742 (1805 edition, p. 145, Prop. VI; dl/20): "The Track described by the Flight of Shot or Shells is neither a Parabola, nor nearly a Parabola, unless they are projected with small Velocities." - Cohen et al. 2014, §4c (dl/09): "The whole system is thus governed by only two parameters, namely the initial angle θ0 and the ratio U∞/U0, between the terminal and the initial velocities." Same drag law and same definition of terminal velocity as the README. - Their abstract: "if the initial velocity is smaller than the terminal velocity of free fall, we observe the classical Galilean parabola: if it is larger, the projectile decelerates with an asymmetric trajectory first drawn by Tartaglia, which ends with a nearly vertical fall, as if a wall impeded the movement."

P2: FOUND IN PART. Fairly sure. - Cohen et al. show a parabola-like path at U0/U∞ = 0.31 (fig. 9a) and say the parabolic range "only fits the data obtained in the low velocity limit". - The 7% and the 0.508 are not printed; they are plain numerics of the same equation.

P3, P4: FOUND IN PART, and older than the README knows. Sure of the quotations. - Robins p. 145, musket ball of ¾ inch at "near 1700 feet in 1″": parabolic range "about 17 miles. Now all the practical writers assure us, that this range is really short of half a mile." He cites Mersenne: "less than 400 fathom, or 800 yards". - Robins p. 146, 24-pound shot at 1,650 ft/s: "it will come out to be about 16 miles, which is between five and six times its real quantity; for the practical writers all agree in making it less than three miles… flies not, at 45°, to the fifth part of the distance". He gives du Metz's measured 2,250 French fathoms, about 4,385 m. - The README's 0.183 for the shot sits inside Robins's "not the fifth part". - Its musket figure does not match the record. Robins's real range is under 805 m; the README model gives 1,056 m, and 1,164 m for Robins's own ball by my integration. I would believe the measurements: the constant drag coefficient is too low for a supersonic ball. - Cohen et al. give a cannonball case from Hélie (Gâvre, 1830–1864): 15.1 kg at 485 m/s, U0/U∞ = 2.05, "The range is maximized and equal to 5690 m for an angle θ = 37.5°". The README's model gives that ball 4,501 m at 33° by my integration. - The README's exact figures (1,056 m, 80.8°, 0.690) are for round inputs from memory and are not in print.

P5: FOUND IN PART. Sure. - Cohen et al. table 1: shuttlecock terminal velocity 6.7 m/s measured in a vertical wind tunnel, fastest hit 117 m/s, ratio 17.5. Their fig. 5b is a filmed shuttlecock path. - They integrate the same equation against nine filmed shuttlecock paths (fig. 7): "Without any adjustable parameter, the comparison with the experimental data is convincing". - The README's 60 m/s case and its numbers are not in the paper.

P6: NOT FOUND. Weakly sure. - One catalogue query found no area-under-path measure. The search was thin. - The neighbouring idea, a number for the asymmetry of old drawings, is in Stewart, "On the trajectories of projectiles depicted in early ballistic woodcuts" (Eur. J. Phys. 33, 2012). Abstract only: "the initial launch angle resulting in the greatest forward horizontal skew of the trajectory is found… Limiting behaviour in the shape of the trajectory in the strong damping limit is also considered and compared to peculiar straight-lined triangular trajectory woodcuts". - My integrator gives 0.1725, 0.2783, 0.3342, 0.3503, and only 0.476 at a ratio of 10,000.

P7: FOUND IN PART. Sure of the substance. - Cohen et al. §4b: "As soon as the drag appears, there is thus a vertical asymptote on the trajectory. This vertical asymptote is reached within the characteristic distance L." - They also note: "the asymmetry increases with both the hit velocity and initial angle". - Halley 1695, quoted in Walley 2018 (dl/11), gives the gunners' experience: "when bombs are discharged with great elevations of the mortar, they fall too perpendicular". - The 6%, the 59° chord and the low-elevation angles were not found.

P8: FOUND IN PART, and the README's wording is loose. Sure. - Cohen et al. give the logarithms for range, wall and height: "x0 ∼ L cos θ0 ln[(U0/U∞)² sin θ0]"; wall at xw/(L cos θ0) = 1 + ln(U0/U∞) (4.10); arc length to the top s0 = ½ L ln[1 + 2(U0/U∞)² sin θ0]; range (4.14) x0 = ½ L cos θ0 ln[1 + 4(U0/U∞)² sin θ0]. - They say (4.14) can be off by 25% at very high speed. Against the README's range fractions it is 20 to 25% low for ratios of 1 to 12, so there is no contradiction. - The apex fraction itself is not given. It follows in two lines: both distances grow as the logarithm, so their ratio tends to 1 as one over the logarithm. - My integration: 0.7237, 0.8066, 0.8571, 0.8874 at ratios 12, 100, 1,000, 10,000. The gain per decade falls 0.09, 0.05, 0.03; 1/(1 − apex) is close to 0.8 ln(ratio) + 1.5. - Chudinov (arXiv 2103.11111, 2021) has a closed formula for "xa - the abscissa of the trajectory apex". I did not evaluate it.

P9: NOT FOUND as a number. Fairly sure. - The substance is Galileo's sentence, Fourth Day, p. 256: "The enormous momentum [impeto] of these violent shots may cause some deformation of the trajectory, making the beginning of the parabola flatter and less curved than the end". - Cohen et al. §4c(ii): "The initial part of the trajectory is thus a straight line along which the particle decelerates over the characteristic distance L."

P10: FOUND. Sure. - Cohen et al. §4g: "θ slowly decreases with increasing velocity: It reaches 30° for (U0/U∞)² = 14 and 20° for (U0/U∞)² = 1700." My integration gives 31.4° and 19.5°; the README's row (31 at ratio 4, 20 at ratio 40) agrees. - Their closed form (4.16) is θ = arctan √{X/[(1+X) ln(1+X)]} with X = (U0/U∞)². The root sign is lost in my text extraction; I restored it by differentiating (4.14). It gives 44.3, 32.3, 26.9, 25.4 against the README's 44, 33, 28 (27), 26. - Hélie, as quoted there: "there is an angle which gives the highest range. Experiments show that the angle is always smaller than 45°". - Walley 2018: "Tartaglia's (unnamed) friend was correct: in a resistive medium (which, of course, air is), the angle for maximum range is less than 45°". - Also Chudinov, "An Optimal Angle of Launching a Point Mass in a Medium with Quadratic Drag Force" (arXiv physics/0506201, 2005), and Erlichson (Am. J. Phys. 1983, abstract only).

P11: FOUND IN PART. Sure of the fact, less of the README's one-line proof. - Robins p. 146–147 states it as observation: "obviously descending through a curve which is shorter, and less inclined to the horizon, than that in which they ascended; also the highest point of their flight, or the vertex of the curve, is much nearer to the place, where they fall on the ground, than to that from whence they were at first discharged." - A 2020 paper, "The English Galileo and His Vision of Projectile Motion under Air Resistance" (abstract only), proves it: "the flight path of a resisted projectile is implemented by a strictly concave function for which the derivative is also strictly concave." - The README's reason is one step short. Slower sideways speed alone does not give a steeper descent, because the vertical speed is lower too. The working fact is d²y/dx² = −g/vx², so the path bends ever more sharply. I derived this; I did not find it quoted.

P12: NOT FOUND, elementary. I checked the geometry by coordinates; it holds for S ≥ r.

P13: FOUND IN PART. Sure. - Robins p. 145 found the transonic rise by experiment: "the resistance of this bullet, when it first issues from the piece, amounts to 120 times its gravity". The README's constant coefficient gives 55 times for that ball, so Robins's figure is about 2.2 times the model's, which is the README's "about twice". - Cohen et al. §2: "CD ≈ 0.4 − 0.5… for the range: 10³ < Re < 2·10⁵" and "The drag crisis experienced by the sphere once the boundary layer becomes turbulent (around Re ≈ 3·10⁵)". Their table gives about 0.2 to 0.25 for large balls.

H1: FOUND, with a correction of scope. Sure. - Cohen et al. §1: "According to Tartaglia, the trajectory was composed of two main phases, a straight violent motion at the exit of the canon… and a final vertical path called natural… The two limits were connected via a circular path." - Valleriani 2013 (dl/23) calls it a "trajectory made up of two straight lines joined up by an arc of circumference". - The scheme is older than Tartaglia. Wikipedia, "Albert of Saxony" (dl/29; a weak source, citing McCloskey 1990): "Initial stage. Motion is in a straight line… Intermediate stage. Path begins to deviate downwards from straight line as part of a great circle… Last stage. Gravity alone draws projectile downwards vertically". - It lasted well after Galileo. Walley shows three-part, labelled figures from Collado (1592) and Sturmy (1669): "The violent motion… The mixt or crooked motion… The naturall motion".

H2: FOUND. Sure. - Tartaglia, Book II, Proposition VIII, in Valleriani's translation: "the one [equally heavy body] that accomplishes its transit at an elevation of 45 degrees above the horizon produces its effect farther away". - Cohen et al. call this "Tartaglia's law".

H3: CONTRADICTED IN PART. Sure of the quotation; one caveat on the edition. - The admission is in the Nova scientia itself, Book II, Supposition II (Valleriani's translation, dl/28): "Indeed, the transit, that is, the violent motion of an equally heavy body that does not follow the perpendicular to the horizon, never shows any perfectly straight part because of the gravity in that body which continuously pulls it toward the center of the Earth. Nevertheless, that part [of the transit] that is not perceived as being curved is assumed to be straight, and that part that is evidently curved is assumed to be part of the circumference of a circle, as this [assumption] does not influence the argument." - Valleriani: "Tartaglia was fully aware that he was forcing the trajectory he could observe into the formal instruments at his disposal." - Walley: "It is also clear from the frontispiece to his book (figure 13) that Tartaglia was aware that the trajectory of projectiles fired upwards from a mortar was curved along its entire length. Indeed the curve shown looks qualitatively like a parabola." - So he never held the three-part figure as literal, even in the first book. I believe the edition over the README's memory. - Caveat: Valleriani's text is the 1558 printing of the 1550 second edition. I did not check the sentence against the 1537 printing. - I did not obtain the Quesiti. Walley dates it 1538; Valleriani's bibliography says 1546, which matches the README.

H4: FOUND, but incomplete. Sure. - Galileo did not claim the parabola for fast shot in air (quotations under P1 and P9). - He also wrote (Third Day opening, dl/06): "It has been observed that missiles and projectiles describe a curved path of some sort; however no one has pointed out the fact that this path is a parabola."

H5: sources exist. Gunners' books drew the three-part figure (Walley's figures); Robins cites the "practical writers" for ranges; Hélie for the best angle.

H6: FOUND as an idea. Sure. - La Rocca and Riggi 2009, abstract (dl/02): "trajectories quite similar to those depicted before the Galilean era may be obtained with a realistic choice of quantities involved." - Walley 2018, abstract: "air resistance makes the trajectory both impossible to calculate and qualitatively similar to theories based on Aristotle's writings." - Darbois Texier's thesis (2013, dl/22): "Lorsque la vitesse de tir du projectile est inférieure à sa vitesse terminale, la trajectoire est parabolique. Dans le cas inverse, la trajectoire est similaire à l'esquisse de Tartaglia". - Cohen et al.: "We will thus refer to the trajectories obtained in this large velocity domain as Tartaglias."

4. Sentences that say more than the sources or the numbers allow

  • Front page, "Before Galileo, a cannonball was drawn flying straight…". Tartaglia's own frontispiece shows a curve, his text says no part is perfectly straight, Galileo says a curved path had been observed, and the figure outlived Galileo. Fair: "From Tartaglia (1537) until long after Galileo, gunnery books often drew a shot's path as a straight line, an arc and a straight drop, though Tartaglia wrote that no part of it is truly straight."
  • Front page, "In air the truth lies between that and his parabola". It reads as the study's finding. Fair: "In air a fast shot's path lies between the two, as Galileo allowed and as Cohen and others showed in 2014; for a model musket ball, by a measure of my own, about half way."
  • Line 22, "exactly half way". The 0.334 against 0.333 is an accident of round inputs from memory in a model that overstates a real musket ball's range by 30% or more. Fair: "about half way (0.33 between 0.17 and 0.5), in a model that sends the ball farther than muskets were reported to shoot."
  • Line 28, "only as the logarithm of the speed". Fair: "The gap between the top and the far end closes only as one over the logarithm of the speed: 0.31, 0.19, 0.14, 0.11 at 6.6, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 terminal velocities."
  • Lines 154–155, "So the best-known author of the three-part figure did not hold to it". Fair: "Tartaglia said in the same book that the straight parts were a simplification."
  • Line 35, "Tartaglia gave 45". True; add that a friend of his thought 45° too high (Walley) and that gunners' trials later put it lower (Hélie).
  • Lines 155–156, "I know nothing here about what working gunners drew or believed". No longer fair to leave: Walley's figures and Robins's citations are a few minutes away.
  • Lines 139–140, "so the descent is steeper". The "so" does not follow as written (see P11).

5. Terms

  • "Elevation" is fine. "Impact angle" / "lands at": the field says angle of fall or angle of descent.
  • "Top" / "apex fraction": vertex, summit or culminating point; Stewart calls the asymmetry skew.
  • "Best elevation": angle of maximum range, optimal launch angle; Cohen et al.'s name for the 45° rule is "Tartaglia's law".
  • The subject is external (exterior) ballistics; the model is the point-mass trajectory with Newton or quadratic drag.
  • Speed squared over g times L: Cohen et al. call L = U∞²/g the aerodynamic length; the steep fall is the aerodynamic wall; the fast regime is a Tartaglia trajectory. A reader needs these words to find the literature.
  • "Drag on large fast spheres, the other way" is the drag crisis. "Drag near the speed of sound" is the transonic drag rise (wave drag).
  • The three parts have names: violent, mixed (or crooked) and natural motion. Tartaglia's word for the path of violent motion is transit.
  • The folder is named "impetus" but the README never says what impetus theory was (Buridan; Albert of Saxony's three stages).
