A gasket with numbers missing

The smaller a circle, the bigger its number.

What is going on

Three circles that touch one another leave a gap, and exactly one circle fits the gap touching all three. Fill every gap, then every new gap, and so on for ever: that is an Apollonian gasket. A circle's number here is one over its radius, so small circles have big numbers; mathematicians call it the curvature. If the first four numbers are whole, every number in the gasket is.

A gasket is named here by its four largest circles, as (−4, 5, 20, 21). The number with the minus is the circle round all the rest.

Not every whole number can turn up. Divide a number by 24 and look at what is left over. In (−4, 5, 20, 21) and in (−3, 5, 8, 8), the two gaskets of the picture's button, only numbers that leave 0, 5, 8, 12, 20 or 21 are ever a circle's number. 36 leaves 12, so it passes that test. In (−4, 5, 20, 21) it is there; in (−3, 5, 8, 8) it never comes.

The guess, first made in 2003 and revised in 2011, was that every number that passes the test turns up, once the numbers are large enough. In 2023 four mathematicians (Haag, Kertzer, Rickards and Stange; their paper is named at the foot of the page) showed that the guess is false: in many gaskets a whole family of numbers that pass the test never appears. Here such a family is said to be ruled out. In (−3, 5, 8, 8) it is the squares, and the numbers six times a square; in (−4, 5, 20, 21) it is the numbers twice a square and three times a square.

The numbers that never come

Any gasket

The theorem and the table of families are from S. Haag, C. Kertzer, J. Rickards and K. E. Stange, The local-global conjecture for Apollonian circle packings is false (Annals of Mathematics 200, 2024; arXiv 2307.02749). The conjecture they overturned was made by Graham, Lagarias, Mallows, Wilks and Yan (2003) and by Fuchs and Sanden (2011). The six lists in the second picture were computed here, and are the same, number for number, as the lists the authors keep with their programs, in a place their paper names. Nothing on this page is new mathematics.

What is certain and what is not: that a family never appears is the paper's theorem. That the blue numbers come to an end is not known: it is the paper's guess, and the lists only show that nothing else is missing up to a hundred million (the authors looked at least a hundred times further, by their paper as it stands in the arXiv archive). In the third panel, the kind of gasket and its families are worked out from the four numbers by the paper's rules, except the families of fourth powers, which the page finds by looking among the circles; the list of missing numbers comes from going through the circles.
