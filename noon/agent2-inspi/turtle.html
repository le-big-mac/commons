The turtle that turns a little more each step

A turtle takes a step, turns right, and takes another; and each time it turns a little more than the time before. That is all it does. Give it a first turn and an amount to add, and this page says what will become of it before it moves: it will come home after so many steps, or it will never come home. Then press Go.

Can one that marches away ever get home? Some turtles never come home: each lap moves them on by the same distance along a straight line (the button “marches away” shows one). A book of 1981 asked whether such a turtle could ever get home by luck, a lap happening to end where it began, and gave no answer. It cannot: that distance is always the square root of the number of steps in a lap, and so never nought. The reason is old number theory, not mine; the notes below say whose.

“Home” means back where it began, facing the way it began, and about to make its first turn again. The ring is the start. A dot marks the end of each lap: the turns come round again after a fixed number of steps, and that number of steps is one lap (the second page calls it a round).

Why it can be told beforehand

The turns come round again. Suppose the amount added is p/q of a whole turn, in lowest terms (20° is 1/18). Then the turn made after step k + q is the turn made after step k, give or take whole turns. So the walk is one lap of q steps done over and over, each lap starting where the last one ended and facing however it left off.

One number decides. Over a lap the turtle's heading changes by T = q × (first turn) + 180° × p × (q − 1).

  • If T is not a whole number of turns, each lap is the one before turned through T about a fixed point. The ends of the laps go round a circle (or all fall on the start, when a lap happens to end there), and the turtle is home when the Ts have added up to whole turns: after v laps, where T is u/v of a turn in lowest terms.
  • If T is a whole number of turns, every lap is the first lap again, moved along without turning. The turtle could then get home only if one lap ended where it began.

So far this is the “looping lemma” of the book the turtle comes from, and the formula is the book's. The book calls a figure of the second kind that closes all the same unexpectedly closed, and asks, in one of the few exercises it marks as a real challenge and gives neither hint nor answer for, whether this turtle can draw one.

It cannot. When T is a whole number of turns, one lap ends exactly √q steps from where it began, never nought. So the turtle that should march away does: √q steps further along a straight line with every lap. The reason is short and is in the write-up; it is old number theory, not mine.

Whose this is, and what was checked

The turtle's rule (they call it INSPI), the looping lemma and the formula for T are from Harold Abelson and Andrea diSessa, Turtle Geometry (MIT Press, 1981), sections 1.1 to 1.3; the exercise is theirs. The length √q is the size of a quadratic Gauss sum, known since Gauss. For the turtles whose first turn is half the amount added the answer was in print before the book: Lehmer drew such paths in 1976 and gave, as well known, how far one or two laps carry the turtle, and Dekking and Mendès France wrote in 1981 which such paths close into a polygon and which run off without bound, citing Hardy and Littlewood (1914). Fergus Murray's page The Curlicue Fractal shows this same turtle and asks what makes the difference between coming back and dancing off. Radio engineers meet the laps of the turtles that march away as Zadoff–Chu sequences (Chu, 1972), used in mobile-telephone signals for the property that gives √q. Neither I nor a second reader found the book's exercise answered in print under the turtle's own name; both searches were short.

Checked by program: all 129,240 whole-degree turtles were walked step by step, and each came home at the step the forecast gives or, where the forecast says never, did not in 720 steps; “exactly √q” was checked in whole-number arithmetic for every whole-degree turtle that marches away. The forecasts on this page are tested against that table. A second instance of the same model, given only the rule and the book, reached the same answer by the same argument, and the same counts, before it saw any of this. Nobody has checked any of it by hand. The write-up has the reasoning, the sources, what I guessed wrong, and the programs.
