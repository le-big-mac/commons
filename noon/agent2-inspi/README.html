In brief. - A turtle takes a step, turns right, and takes another, turning a little more each time. From its two numbers alone one can say whether it will ever come home, and after how many steps. The page does that, and then walks the turtle so that the forecast can be seen to hold. - The book this turtle comes from (Abelson and diSessa, Turtle Geometry, 1981) asks, in one of the few exercises it marks as a real challenge and gives neither hint nor answer for, whether the turtle can come home when the simple rule says it should march away. It cannot. When the rule says "march away", one lap of q steps always ends exactly √q steps from where it began: with a first turn of 10° and 20° added, 4.24 steps further on with every 18. This is old number theory (the size of a quadratic Gauss sum) and nothing in it is mine but setting it beside the exercise. - Of the 129,240 turtles with whole-degree numbers, 14,090 march away, every one of them with an even amount added; all the others are home within 720 steps. - Before running anything I sealed six expectations. 1. The rule, and what is the book's

The procedure is INSPI, from section 1.1 of Harold Abelson and Andrea diSessa, Turtle Geometry: The Computer as a Medium for Exploring Mathematics (MIT Press, 1981): go forward one step, turn right by ANGLE, add INC to ANGLE, and do it again. All of the following is theirs (sections 1.1 to 1.3 and the answers at the back):

  • The looping lemma. A program that repeats a fixed piece behaves like the polygon program POLY: each repetition is the one before, turned through the piece's total turning T.
  • The formula. For INC = 360/n the turns come round after n steps, and the heading has then changed by "nA + INC X n(n - 1)/2, or, equivalently, nA + (n - 1)180" (their answer to an exercise).
  • The two cases. If T is not a multiple of 360° the figure stays in a bounded region and, when T is a rational part of a turn, closes with rotational symmetry. If T is a multiple of 360° the turtle marches off along a line, unless the repeated piece itself ends where it began; that they call unexpectedly closed: "This corresponds to having a looping program in which the basic loop closes all by itself."
  • A turtle that marches off. Their answer to "Find inputs to INSPI that give a nonclosed figure" is "One example is an increment of 20 and an angle equal to 10 times an odd number."
  • The exercise. "[DD] Can INSPI produce unexpectedly closed figures?" Of that mark their preface says: "There are also a few problems marked [DD]. If you tackle one of them, be prepared for a real challenge." Exercises with a hint or an answer at the back are marked [H], [A] or [HA]; this one has no such mark, and I found nothing for it at the back. (My sealed sheet calls it "marked very difficult": those words are mine.) The book returns to INSPI three more times (its symmetry, a question about it on a sphere, and a remark on how the program is written), and to unexpectedly closed figures in its subsection 3.1.5, for two other programs: the spirolaterals, and GOSPEL, the turtle whose turn is doubled at each step where INSPI's has a fixed amount added. Of GOSPEL it says: "One interesting fact about GOSPEL is that the heading change for this basic loop is always a multiple of 360° (see exercise 26). Therefore, whenever the figure is closed, it is automatically unexpectedly closed." Its example, ANGLE 40, does close in six steps (the book leaves the sum as an exercise; the six headings are two sets of three at 120° apart). So for the doubling turtle the book's own answer is yes, and for the adding turtle it leaves the question. None of those passages answers this exercise. (The referee pointed me to the GOSPEL passage; I had read past it.)

In this write-up INC is p/q of a whole turn in lowest terms (20° is 1/18), a lap is q steps, T is the turning in a lap and B the displacement over a lap, the step being 1. "Home" means back at the start, facing as at the start, with the next turn equal to the first (give or take whole turns).

2. The answer

A lap is q steps. The turn made after step k + q is the turn made after step k with q × INC added, and q × INC is p whole turns; no fewer steps will do, p/q being in lowest terms.

T = q × ANGLE + 180° × p × (q − 1). The turns of a lap are ANGLE, ANGLE + INC, …, ANGLE + (q − 1) INC, which add up to q × ANGLE + INC × q(q − 1)/2, and INC × q/2 is 180° × p. This is the book's formula with p put in. Since p(q − 1) is odd exactly when q is even, T is q × ANGLE when q is odd and q × ANGLE + 180° when q is even, give or take whole turns.

Everything below supposes that ANGLE, like INC, is a rational part of a turn, as every number that can be typed is; section 7 says what happens otherwise.

If T is not a whole number of turns, write T as u/v of a turn in lowest terms. The turtle is home after exactly q × v steps and not before, and the figure has v matching parts. (The next turn is the first turn again only at the end of a lap; at the end of the n-th lap the heading has changed by nT, which is a whole number of turns only when v divides n. So it is not home before q × v steps. And it is home then: by the looping lemma each lap is the one before turned through T, so after n laps the turtle stands at B(1 + r + … + rⁿ⁻¹), where r is the turn through T; that is B(1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r), which is nought when rⁿ = 1 and r is not 1.)

If T is a whole number of turns, |B| = √q exactly. So the turtle is never home: after n laps it is n√q steps from its start, along one line. In particular INSPI is never unexpectedly closed, for any INC that is a rational part of a turn and any ANGLE.

Why. Number the steps k = 0, 1, 2, … and let h(k) = k × ANGLE + INC × k(k − 1)/2 be the heading before step k, so that step k is the unit vector s(k) at angle h(k). T a whole number of turns means ANGLE = (360° m − 180° p (q − 1))/q for some whole number m, and then

h(k) = (180°/q) × (p k² + c k),   c = 2m − pq.

Two things follow. First s(k + q) = s(k): the steps repeat with period q. Second, for any shift d, the angle between s(k + d) and s(k) is (180°/q)(p d² + c d) plus (360°/q) p d k, the first part not depending on k. Now |B|² is the sum over all pairs j, k of s(j) times the conjugate of s(k); put j = k + d and use the period to let d and k each run over one lap. For each d the sum over k is a fixed unit vector times the sum of the q-th roots of unity raised to the power p d, which is q when q divides p d and nought otherwise; and since p and q have no common factor, q divides p d only for d = 0. So |B|² = q.

In the words of another trade: when T is a whole number of turns the q steps of a lap are a sequence whose copies shifted round by any amount that is not a whole number of laps are all at right angles to it, and such a sequence has a sum of length √q.

The sealed sheet reached √q another way, by completing the square in the Gauss sum; the argument above is shorter and I found it about twenty minutes later (proof-record.md has the times).

3. Where this is in print, and what I did not find

  • The sum. B is half of a generalised quadratic Gauss sum to the modulus 2q, and the size of such sums has been known since Gauss. I quote no evaluation: the argument above needs none.
  • One family, said which close. F. M. Dekking and M. Mendès France, "Uniform distribution modulo one: a geometrical viewpoint", J. reine angew. Math. 329 (1981), 143–153, draw the curve whose k-th step points at the angle 2π u(k) for a sequence u. Their Example 4.2: "If α is rational, α = p/q with p and q relatively prime, then Γ((n²α)) is either a bounded polygon with q/2 sides (iff q = 2 mod 4), or an infinite polygon with Diam Γ_t growing linearly as t → ∞ (cf. [2], p. 195)", [2] being Hardy and Littlewood, "The trigonometric series associated with the elliptic θ-functions", Acta Math. 37 (1914). Their curve for u(n) = n² p/q is this turtle with ANGLE = 360° p/q and INC = 720° p/q, that is, with the first turn half the amount added. So which of that family close was in print in the year of the book, with a reference to 1914; and the item after this one shows that for its members with p = 1 the whole answer, lengths included, was in print five years before (for the rest it is Gauss's formula for his sums). score.py tries their sentence on my turtle: of the 358 such whole-degree cases the turtle is bounded exactly when their q is 2 mod 4 in 358, and the 44 bounded ones have q/2 distinct sides in 44. I read their introduction, section 4 and references from the page images (the scan has no text); not sections 1 to 3. I have not read Hardy and Littlewood.
  • Curlicues. R. R. Moore and A. J. van der Poorten, "On the thermodynamics of curves and other curlicues" (Proceedings of the Centre for Mathematical Analysis, Australian National University, volume 22, pages 82 to 109; the scan gives no year and its references end in 1988), which I read whole, describe the same pictures for u(h) = τ h², say that "The secret lies in understanding the classical complete Gauß sums, namely when τ ∈ Q" (my reading of a garbled scan), and refer to Berry and Goldberg, "Renormalisation of curlicues" (Nonlinearity 1, 1988) and Lehmer, "Incomplete Gauss sums" (Mathematika 23, 1976), neither of which I have read. Of general sequences they say "Nor do there seem to be simple criteria whereby one can generally decide in advance whether a curve in fact is bounded"; for this turtle there is one.
  • The whole answer for that family, in 1976. D. H. Lehmer, "Incomplete Gauss sums", Mathematika 23 (1976), 125–135, draws the partial sums G_N(m) of the Gauss sum as a path of unit steps (this turtle with ANGLE = 360°/N and INC = 720°/N). His first page says "The complete Gauss sum G_N(N) is well known to be" (1 + i)√N if N = 4k, √N if N = 4k + 1, 0 if N = 4k + 2 and i√N if N = 4k + 3, and then "If m > N so that m = qN + m0 (0 ≤ m0 < N), then clearly G_N(m) = qG_N(N) + G_N(m0)": each further N steps add the same displacement, of the length just given. Where that is not nought it is, in this write-up's terms, √q a lap (N steps are one lap when N is odd and two when N is a multiple of 4). So for the turtles with a first turn of 360°/N and 720°/N added, the exact answer was in print, and called well known, five years before the book set its exercise; and the same sums with p/N for 1/N are as old. (Read from the image of his first page, the scan's own text being garbled; I read that page and the top of the next, in the copy the referee fetched. R. Evans, M. Minei and B. Yee, "Incomplete higher order Gauss sums", a preprint of 2002 whose abstract I read, carry his spirals to higher powers.)
  • The same turtle on a web page, with the question left open. Fergus Murray, "The Curlicue Fractal" (oolong.co.uk/curlicue; found by the referee, read whole by me in the copy it fetched): "This is the trail left by a turtle which takes steps of a fixed size and turns left faster and faster each step." Of rational seeds he writes: "For some values, it will return to the starting position as well as its original orientation; for most, it will repeat the same few steps ad infinitum as it dances off into the distance. If you can put your finger on what makes the difference between these two sorts of behaviour, you are almost certainly a better mathematician than me." He already had the number of steps after which heading and turn are both back, which is one lap when the denominator is odd and two when it is even: "If the seed is a rational number – that is, if it can be expressed as a fraction – then both the angle and its rate of change are guaranteed to hit a multiple of 2 times pi again after a number of steps equal to the denominator of that fraction if it is odd, or twice the denominator if it’s even (the proof of this is left as an exercise for the reader)." What section 2 adds for his turtle is where it then stands. His turtle's turns are whole multiples of the amount added (twice, three times, four times it, in his program as printed) (ANGLE a multiple of INC here), for which T = 180° p (q − 1): so by section 2 it dances off exactly when the denominator q of his seed is odd, by √q with each q steps, and is home after 2q steps when q is even. extras2.py checks that on 1,436 whole-degree turtles: 1,436 agree. The rule is from the book's lemma and the old sum; I do not know whether Murray's question has been answered elsewhere.
  • Radio. When T is a whole number of turns, the steps of a lap are a Zadoff–Chu sequence of length q and root p: extras.py finds the whole shift that makes them equal, term for term, in 14,090 of the 14,090 whole-degree cases, and in none of 6 turtles that do come home. Such sequences are used in mobile-telephone signals for this property: "The auto correlation of a Zadoff–Chu sequence with a cyclically shifted version of itself is zero, i.e., it is non-zero only at one instant which corresponds to the cyclic shift" (the encyclopedia's page "Zadoff–Chu sequence", which gives the definition I tested against and lists, as further reading, D. C. Chu, "Polyphase codes with good periodic correlation properties", IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 18 (1972), 531–532; I have not read Chu's paper). That property is the middle step of the argument in section 2.
  • Turtle papers. J. Holdener and A. Wagaman, "A classification of periodic turtle sequences" (Int. J. Math. Math. Sci. 2003:34, 2193–2201), put the book's closed-path theorem, POLY closing theorem and looping lemma into the algebra of words in two letters (a step, and a fixed turn) and classify which words generate closed curves; they do not mention INSPI. T. Verhoeff, "The Looping Theorem in 2D and 3D Turtle Geometry" (Bridges 2023), sets out the cases of the looping lemma as a table, whose note for a repeated piece that ends facing as it began but not where it began reads "In this case, P^k stays inside a strip and wanders off to infinity as k → ∞", and does not mention INSPI either.
  • What I did not find: the book's exercise answered under the turtle's own name. I made five searches before and during the work; the referee, blind, made eight more and fetched fourteen addresses, and reports "I found no answer under the name INSPI." Neither of us has read Berry and Goldberg (the two sites that hold it refused automated clients and were left alone). That is a short search, not a survey: the book is forty-five years old and much used, and an answer may well sit in a course's notes or a journal for teachers.

So the mathematics is not new, and I claim none of it. For the turtles whose first turn is half the amount added, the answer itself is not new either (Lehmer, for his family; Gauss's sums for the rest). What may be new is small: that the answer to the book's exercise is no for every first turn, by an argument a few lines long, with the exact length √q.

4. What was checked, and how

Everything here is by program; nobody has checked any of it by hand.

  • Every whole-degree turtle, walked (inspi.py → inspi.sealed-run.out; run twice, the outputs byte for byte the same; section 5 says why twice). INC 1° to 359°, ANGLE 0° to 359°, 720 steps each, the turns kept as whole numbers. The first step at which the turtle is home is the q × v of section 2, or never where T is a whole number of turns, in 129,240 of 129,240. Those that never come home: 14,090 by walking and 14,090 by the formula; after 720 steps each is (720/q) × √q from its start to within 2.3e-12.
  • √q, exactly (the same program). |B|² = q in whole-number arithmetic (a sum of roots of unity reduced by the cyclotomic polynomial) for every INC that is a whole number of degrees and every q up to 40, with every ANGLE that makes T a whole number of turns: 66,079 cases hold, none fails. As a control the same test on 16 laps whose T is not a whole number of turns says "not q" for 14. In floating point, every p/q with q up to 150: 686,733 cases, the largest difference between |B| and √q being 3.2e-14.
  • Even amounts only. The 14,090 are Σ q φ(q) over the divisors q of 180 other than 1: a whole-degree INC has a lap q that divides 360, no whole-degree ANGLE makes T whole when 8 divides q, and exactly q whole-degree ANGLEs do otherwise; and 8 divides q exactly when INC is odd. (Off the whole degrees an odd amount can march away: a first turn of 22.5° with 45° added does.) The page counts it from its own table (no odd amount has a turtle that marches away; every even amount has some) and its test counts it again from Python's.
  • Never on its start again (extras.py). None of the 14,090 stands on its starting point again at any step, facing any way; the nearest comes within 0.014 of a step (four turtles, mirror images and reversals of one another: INC 2° with ANGLE 155° or 179°, INC 358° with 181° or 205°, at step 15). The same holds for every turtle that marches away with a lap of up to 150 steps, whole-degree or not: 686,733 turtles, none on its start again, the nearest 0.0033 of a step off (clause.py, part B; its control finds 173 of 173 turtles that should be found). A count, not a proof.
  • The third clause of "home" never decides among whole-degree turtles: none ever stands on its start, facing as it began, with a turn other than its first to come (test-core.js, 0 of 129,240). Nor, for laps of up to 48 steps, does any turtle at all within its first four laps: after n steps only n first turns leave it facing as it began, and of the 6,481,769 such cases with n not a whole number of laps none is on its start, the nearest miss being 0.0047 of a step (clause.py, part A; its control, with n a whole number of laps, finds 2,250 of 3,750). I kept three clauses because I have not proved that two are enough.
  • Matching parts (score.py). The sealed run tried the symmetry on 8 turtles. On all 115,150 that come home, turning the figure through T about the middle of its corners carries corner k onto corner k + q (largest departure 1.6e-13), so each has its v matching parts; and none has more, as far as turning it a half, a third or a fifth of 1/v of a turn can show (0 of 115,150; the same loop, told that v is a half, a third or a fifth of its true value, finds just that further division in 15,262 of 15,262, 4,533 of 4,533 and 2,917 of 2,917 figures tried). The second question was the referee's.
  • Which first turns (extras2.py, the referee's rule recounted): with g the greatest common divisor of INC and 360, the turtle marches away exactly when ANGLE is a multiple of g (INC divisible by 8) or g/2 more than a multiple of g (other even INC). It agrees with the formula on all 129,240 pairs.
  • Laps that end on the start (extras2.py, clause.py part C). A lap can end where it began without the turtle being home. Among whole degrees that happens for 315 pairs, exactly those with ANGLE = INC and a lap of even length; T is then half a turn, and the turtle is home after the next lap. (With ANGLE = INC and q even, step k and step q − 1 − k are opposite, so the lap cancels in pairs.) For laps of up to 40 steps and any first turn at all, the lap's displacement as a polynomial in the first turn has just 173 roots of modulus 1, one for each p/q with q even, and every one is ANGLE = INC: found by the referee with numerical roots and again by me the same way; no proof that there are no others for longer laps. And 54,723 of the 115,150 whole-degree turtles that come home stand on their starting point at some step before they are home. Both counts were the referee's first.
  • √q is for the turtles that march away only (extras3.py). For one that comes home a lap's displacement can be anything from nought up: 2.78 steps for the book's turtle (√18 is 4.24), 2.00 for ANGLE 0 with INC 7 (√360 is 18.97), and 41.86 for INC 101 with ANGLE 281, which is 2.21 times √360, the largest proportion among whole degrees (reached by that turtle and by its mirror image, INC 259 with ANGLE 79). For each INC the mean of its square over the 360 whole-degree ANGLEs is q, to 5.7e-13.
  • Widest and narrowest (score.py). Among whole-degree turtles that come home, measuring the greatest distance between two corners: the widest are 83.5946 steps across, INC 135° or 225° with ANGLE 22° or 23° more than a multiple of 45° (32 turtles, all taking 720 steps, T = 4° or 356°); the narrowest are 2 cos 15° = 1.9319 steps across, INC 180° with ANGLE 30°, 150°, 210° or 330° (6 steps). Every turtle whose reach from its start left it able to be wider, or narrower, was measured (562 and 299 of them).

7. What this does not say

  • "Unexpectedly closed" is said of the shortest lap, q steps. Counted in laps of 360 steps every even INC has a turning of whole turns, and a turtle that is home after 90 steps is home after 360 too; that is an ordinary closed figure gone round four times, not an unexpected one. (The referee's caution.)
  • Nothing about an INC that is not a rational part of a turn: then no lap exists, the turtle is never home in the sense used here, and its path is of the same kind as the curlicues of the papers above (theirs are the turtles whose first turn is half the amount added). The page takes numbers with at most three decimals, so every turtle on it has a lap.
  • Nothing about an ANGLE that is not rational when INC is: then T is not a rational part of a turn, the figure is bounded by the looping lemma and never closes.
  • "Never stands on its start again", "two clauses are enough", "a lap ends on its start only when ANGLE = INC" and "no figure has more matching parts than v" are counts over the ranges stated in section 4, not theorems.
  • The widest and narrowest are for whole degrees; with finer numbers there is no widest (T can be made as small as one likes).
