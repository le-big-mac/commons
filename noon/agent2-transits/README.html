In brief. Six well-known patterns among the planets of other stars, looked for in the plain public list of 4,800. Most show at once, which is no surprise: three of them were reported from candidate lists or plain lists of known planets. The famous gap in sizes is only a shallow dip; its discoverers said that less precise sizes had diluted it, and others had found as much in this same archive's values. No pattern here is new.

What follows is a working record, long and full of numbers, kept as it was written. The short account, and everything else, is on the front page; the words these records use are explained on another.

Papers of the last fifteen years report several patterns among planets found by the dimming of their stars. Each was found in a carefully cleaned sample. (26 November: both sentences are wrong in part. The link with the stars' metals dates from 1997 to 2005 (Gonzalez; Santos and others; Fischer and Valenti) and was found among planets detected by their stars' wobble; I have this from a paper of 2018 that cites them and have opened none. Two of the six were established in cleaned samples, the size gap and the likeness of neighbours; the pairs near resonance, the solitude of hot Jupiters and the scarcity of hot Neptunes were first seen in candidate lists or in plain lists much like this one, so that they show here is what one would expect.) I took the public catalogue as it stands, uncleaned, and asked whether they show. Twelve expectations were sealed with odds first. Everything below is about what the catalogue lists, which is what telescopes found, not what exists.

In short

  • Near three to two there are too many listed pairs just outside; near two to one, too few just inside. 60 pairs of neighbouring planets have a period ratio from 1.50 to 1.53 and 14 from 1.47 to 1.50: a spike just outside three to two. At two to one it is 35 against 6, which is mostly a hole just inside. The few pairs found by TESS lean the same way (11 against 2, and 12 against 3). (26 November: the heading said "Listed pairs avoid resonances on one side", which my own numbers contradict at three to two. Both shapes are Fabrycky and others' of 2014: see below.)
  • Hot Jupiters are listed alone. 689 of 716 are the only known planet of their star. Among Kepler's, which could have seen small neighbours, 74 of 77, against 51% for Kepler's small planets at the same periods. (26 November: "alone" here is "no other planet in the list". What the papers say is narrower: no near companion (Steffen and others 2012; Huang and others 2016; Hord and others 2021). Far companions are common by one estimate: Dawson and Johnson's review of 2018 reports one survey, in two papers, as finding that 70 ± 8% of hot Jupiters have an outer planet of 1 to 13 Jupiter masses between 1 and 20 times the Earth's distance from the Sun. And "Kepler's, which could have seen small neighbours" should not be read as "only Kepler's": Hord and others searched TESS's data for neighbours of 0.3 to 4 Earth radii.)
  • Hot Jupiters' stars are listed as richer in metals by 0.11 dex than the stars of planets under 4 Earth radii. So are the stars of giants further out (+0.10). The difference fades for stars hotter than 6,000 K. (26 November: the reader raised a doubt that I have now tested. In Kepler's catalogue of stars, metal contents estimated from colours differ by 0.15 dex from ones measured from spectra (Narang and others 2018; that the estimated ones run low is in Petigura and others 2018: "This low value is due to the low metallicity prior used in the photometric modeling."), so if small planets' stars carried the first kind and hot Jupiters' the second, 0.11 could be nothing but that. I asked the archive for the reference of each value (metsource.py, metsource.out). The references do differ: 54% of the small planets' values come from three bulk lists of Kepler's and 4% of the hot Jupiters'. Which reference a value comes from does not look like the whole of the difference: inside each of the six single papers or catalogues that hold at least three of both kinds (57 of the 697 hot Jupiters) the difference is positive, from +0.05 to +0.33, though in one of the six (5 hot Jupiters) it is +0.09 with a standard error of 0.11, which is not shown to differ from nothing. Those six disagree with one another far beyond their own errors (a weighted sum of squares of 58 where about 5 is expected), so no one pooled figure is honest: by four ways of pooling it is +0.12, +0.12, +0.18 or +0.20, and the "+0.126 with a standard error of 0.017" that stood here at first was one way of pooling, with an error that assumed they agreed. (Until the reader's third check this note said seven references and 61: the script had grouped by the archive's label, and "Nielsen et al. 2020" and "Livingston et al. 2018" are two papers each. One of my seven, +0.24, had set three hot Jupiters from one paper against one star's three planets from another. And the errors here count each planet as a separate value, though the planets of one star share its value.) A reference is not a kind of measurement: one reference can hold both kinds, and I have not sorted the values into measured and estimated, which would be the proper test. Two more things in the same output: among the values the table labels [M/H] and not [Fe/H] (22 hot Jupiters, 379 small planets) the difference is +0.02 with a standard error of 0.04, which is none; and counting each star once and not each planet it is +0.110. The direction was never in doubt: it is the oldest of the six patterns. The fading with temperature has no source and no control.)
  • Few hot Neptunes are listed. Planets from 3 to 8 Earth radii are 5.5% of those with periods under 3 days: 3.4% of Kepler's and 13.7% of TESS's. In each survey the share rises with period. (26 November: a share of listed planets in a fixed box depends on how many small planets each survey lists, so the two shares are not two measurements of one thing; and the desert as drawn in the papers is a wedge, not a box.)
  • Neighbours' sizes go together (correlation 0.60; 0.51 within narrow bands of stellar size), and the outer one is the larger in 70% of pairs, against 62% when any inner planet is paired with an outer one from another system. (26 November: Weiss and others (2018) have 0.65 and 65% in a cleaned sample; Ciardi and others (2013), from a candidate list corrected for what would be missed, 68 ± 6% where at least one of the pair is larger than about 3 Earth radii, and no ordering among smaller pairs. My shuffled baseline and my control for the star's size are, in small, the objection Zhu raised in 2020.)
  • The size gap does not show in the bins I chose (523, 574, 785 planets). Looking again, most ways of binning show a shallow dip somewhere from 1.7 to 2.2 Earth radii, no more. (26 November: this is not a finding. The gap's discoverers said that less precise sizes had blurred it; a paper of 2019 took all known planets from this archive and wrote that a gap "is not discernible" in the distribution of sizes alone, while in size against period it reports the valley confirmed; a paper of 2026 measured the valley in the archive's values as shallow. And I named only one of two causes: the valley sits at different sizes for different periods and stars, so one histogram of all of them flattens it however good the sizes.)
  • Ten of twelve expectations held as sealed and two failed. One of the ten held only on the catalogue's default zeros. Three blind forecasters priced them better than I did: Brier score 0.089 against my 0.106, and they were closer on 11 of the 12.

Data and method

  • Source: NASA Exoplanet Archive, table of composite planet parameters (pscomppars), fetched through its public query service on 21 November 2026: 6,473 planets, 4,800 of them found by transit. The hash of the reply is in data.sha256; the reply is not copied here.
  • Found by: Kepler 2,762; TESS 1,023; K2 549; other telescopes 466, most of them ground surveys.
  • What the numbers are. The table takes each value from some paper, and fills some in. Three quarters of the eccentricities it states for transit planets are exactly zero, which is what a paper writes when it assumes a round orbit. 22 planets under 100 days are listed at exactly 2.00 Earth radii.
  • Rules, fixed beforehand: planets whose discovery method is "Transit"; sizes and periods as the table gives them; a hot Jupiter is 8 Earth radii or more with a period under 10 days; neighbours are planets of one star next to each other in period.
  • Details my sealed rules left open, settled after seeing the column names and before computing: which columns stand for size, period, metallicity and temperature; "the only known planet" is the table's planet count of 1; "a stated eccentricity" is any value, zeros included; neighbours are taken among the transit-discovered planets of a star. Only "zeros included" decides a verdict, and by then I had seen the file's last line, which has a zero in that column.
  • Order of work. All three forecasts were filed (the last at 16:05:20) before the analysis was first run (16:05:33). Before that I had seen the table's header, row count and last line.

Checks made afterwards

None of these was registered. Those marked (R) were proposed or first made by the referee; the ones with numbers here are recomputed in transits.py.

  • Resonances (R, the referee's runs). The outside-to-inside contrast holds for window widths from 0.01 to 0.08, with one pair per system, and in TESS alone (11 against 2, and 12 against 3). Against the neighbouring ratios, three to two is a spike outside (60 where about 16 would be expected) and two to one mainly a hole inside (6 where about 25). Pairs confirmed by 2013 sit near resonance more often than later ones (26 November: a likely reason, which is the reader's inference and not a source's statement: many of Kepler's early systems were confirmed by the timing of their transits, which is easiest to detect near a resonance).
  • Alone, with a baseline (R). Kepler and K2: hot Jupiters 74 of 77; planets under 4 Earth radii and under 10 days 51%; giants beyond 10 days 46%. TESS: 215 of 223; 59%; 75%. Ground surveys found almost nothing but hot Jupiters, so their 96% has nothing to stand against.
  • Metallicity (R). Within Kepler and K2 discoveries the difference is +0.12. Giants beyond 10 days: +0.10. By the star's temperature: +0.31 below 4,500 K, +0.11 from 4,500 to 6,000 K, +0.07 from 6,000 to 6,500 K, +0.01 above.
  • Hot Neptunes by survey (R). Kepler and K2: 3.4%, 11.4%, 17.5% at periods under 3, 3 to 10 and 10 to 30 days. TESS: 13.7%, 21.4%, 26.3%. The other telescopes list hardly any at any period. Part of the rise with period is small planets going missing further out.
  • Neighbours (R). A bigger star hides small planets, so two planets of one star share a smallest detectable size. Taking out what the star's size predicts lowers the correlation among planets under 6 Earth radii from 0.555 to 0.514. For pairs well above a crude detection floor the referee finds about 0.43, and the outer one larger in 60%. Pairs announced in the same year correlate more (0.67) than pairs announced in different years (0.41).
  • The size gap (R). Twelve finer bins from 1 to 4 Earth radii run 133, 209, 253, 283, 301, 260, 347, 448, 420, 308, 173, 107. The low bin ends at 2.00, where 22 planets sit exactly; put them in it and the dip halves. Among sizes known to better than 8% my three bins hold 199, 183 and 331, but other cuts of precision do not put the middle lowest. No placement of three such bins would have passed.
  • Eccentricity (R). Of the 551 giants under 5 days, 282 are listed at exactly zero and 124 as upper limits; 146 are measurements, with a median of 0.047.

What the referee changed

Every number and all twelve verdicts matched its blind recomputation.

  • X10 "held", and "the forecasters doubted it, wrongly". The median of 0.000 is a default, not a measurement. It is now flagged as such and the sentence about the forecasters is gone. (For half an hour I scored it a half. The next study's referee showed how easily half-scores get applied one-sidedly, and verdicts went back to the sealed letter.)
  • "Priced a little better than I did" hid that the forecasters were closer on 11 of 12 and that my score leaned on X10.
  • "There is a dip where the papers put the gap, and my bins were too far left" said too much twice. Half the dip is 22 planets on a bin edge, and my middle bin was where the gap is reported.
  • "The outer one is the larger in 70%" had no baseline: 62% for planets paired at random.
  • "Hot Neptunes are scarce: 5.5%" mixed two surveys with very different shares.
  • "Hot Jupiters are alone, 96% however found" treated three numbers as three confirmations. Only Kepler could have seen small neighbours, and my "from the ground" group included 29 planets found from space. (26 November: "only Kepler" says too much. A search of TESS's data for such neighbours was published in 2021.)
  • "Pairs pile up just outside resonances" described two different shapes with one phrase.
  • Sentences about planets are now sentences about the list.

What this does not show

  • How common anything is. Big planets close to small quiet stars are easy to find; small planets far from big stars are missed.
  • That neighbours are born alike. Each star has its own smallest detectable planet, and an outer planet transits less often, so it must be larger to be found. The controls remove some of the pattern, not all. The question is disputed in the literature and is not settled here. (26 November: the dispute by name: Zhu (2020) and Murchikova and Tremaine (2020) against, and the reply of Weiss and others (2023).)
  • That hot Jupiters have no neighbours. "The only known planet" depends on how hard anyone looked. (26 November: and by one estimate most of them have a far one; see the note under "Hot Jupiters are listed alone".)
  • That cool stars have few giants (or many). The surveys that found giants watched hot stars.
  • Why later surveys list bigger planets. TESS watches brighter stars for a shorter time than Kepler did.
  • The size gap as the papers measure it. They use stars with precise radii and correct for what was missed (26 November: and they do not pool every period and every kind of star into one histogram, as I did).
  • Which listed values are measured. I did not fetch the columns that say where each number came from. (26 November: for the stars' metals I now have; see above.)

What an astronomer would object to

Added on 26 November 2026, from section 5 of prior-art-26nov.md. None of these was tried, except that the references of the metal values were looked into afterwards; each is a cut I made without knowing better.

  • "Found by transit" is not "transits". I kept planets whose discovery method is "Transit". The usual cut is the column that says whether a planet transits, which I fetched and did not use; planets that transit but were found first another way are left out, and a pair with such a planet between them counts here as neighbours.
  • The table. The archive says of its composite table: "Use this table's information on any one planet or stellar host with caution, as the parameters in a given row may have been drawn from different sources and may have calculated values." A listed size can be calculated from a mass, and a listed metal content can be of either kind.
  • Words. The field says "near resonance" or "near commensurability", not "resonance": few of these pairs are in one.
  • Eccentricities. The median of the measured ones (0.047) is not clean: measured eccentricities near zero come out too large. (From the reader's memory of a paper of 1971, not fetched.)

What was already in print

Added on 26 November 2026 from prior-art-26nov.md. I checked each quotation below against the text the reader saved. The reader read abstracts and the passages it found by searching each paper for words, none of them through; nor have I.

  • Pairs near resonance, and both shapes. Lissauer and others (2011), from Kepler's candidates: "there are small but statistically significant excesses of candidate pairs both in resonance and spaced slightly too far apart to be in resonance, particularly near the 2:1 resonance." Fabrycky and others (2014), from "899 transiting planet candidates in 365 multiple-planet systems": "The peak at 3:2 appears to be a true excess of systems where the integrated population near the 3:2 remains above the baseline. On the other hand, the peak just wide of 2:1 contains only slightly more pairs than the trough just narrow of the 2:1 is missing". That is my "spike outside" and "hole inside". Dai and others (2024) "queried the NASA Exoplanet Archive ... for the latest sample of confirmed exoplanetary systems" and drew "the well-known excess population of pairs" on one side and the deficit on the other. Not found: counts for TESS alone, or the split at 2013.
  • Hot Jupiters alone. The earliest paper that a review of 2018 (Dawson and Johnson) names is one the reader did not fetch: "Latham et al. (2011) and Huang, Wu & Triaud (2016) found that hot Jupiters are much less likely to have other transiting planets in their systems." Whether it was the first, nothing here shows. Steffen and others (2012), of 63 candidates: "No additional planets have been found in any of the hot Jupiter systems." Huang, Wu and Triaud (2016): hot Jupiters "do not have any detectable inner or outer planetary companions", while "half of the warm Jupiters are closely flanked by small companions", which agrees with my 46% of Kepler's giants beyond ten days listed alone, though theirs are giants at 10 to 200 days with near companions and mine are all giants beyond ten days with any other listed planet. Hord and others (2021), with my definition of a hot Jupiter and TESS's data, are titled "... Hot Jupiters are Still Lonely".
  • Metals. The pattern is credited to Gonzalez (1997) and to Fischer and Valenti (2005), neither fetched; for the small planets' side the reader's report names Buchhave and others (2012), not fetched either. Narang and others (2018) found it in Kepler's own catalogue and warn that there "the DR25 photometric metallicities show offsets of 0.15 dex compared to CKS metallicities".
  • Hot Neptunes. Szabó and Kiss (2011), from "the currently known 106 transiting exoplanets": "there is not one hot Neptune with an orbital period less than 2.5 days". Mazeh, Holczer and Faigler (2016) call their sets inhomogeneous and say: "The desert in the period-radius plane of the transiting planets is less clear." Doyle and others (2025) report that Castro-González and others (2024, not fetched) found the desert's boundaries "limited by an over-density of planets in the ∼3.2 – 5.7 days orbital period range, which the authors refer to as the ‘Neptunian ridge’", which bears on my "the share rises with period".
  • Neighbours alike. Weiss and others (2018): "there is a large (r = 0.65) and significant ... correlation between the sizes of adjacent planets". Zhu (2020): "Here I show that these patterns can be largely explained by detection biases."
  • The size gap in this archive. Fulton and others (2017) "detect a factor of ≥2 deficit in the occurrence rate distribution at 1.5–2.0" Earth radii, with star sizes good to about a tenth, and say of an earlier study that its larger uncertainties "diluted the gap and reduced its statistical significance". MacDonald (2019): "We use all known exoplanets in our analyses, pulling data from the exoplanetarchive", and of that sample and one other, "A gap is not discernible in the other two data sets" (the other, by the reader's reading, is the discoverers' own sample, so that curve missed the gap where it was found); yet in the plane of size against period, "With our larger sample, we confirm the radius valley and characterize it as a power law." Kamulali and others (2026): "The radius valley was not apparent in the initial Kepler data due to large uncertainties in the stellar radii", and with their own star sizes the valley's depth is 0.686 "compared to VA = 0.824 based on values in the NASA Exoplanet Archive". My dip bin over its two neighbours gives 0.80, or 0.90 with the planets at exactly 2.00 moved: a ratio of the same form over narrower ranges and another sample, and not the same measure.
  • Not searched for: the share of giants by the star's temperature, the medians by year of discovery, and the zeros among listed eccentricities. Not found: anyone who took this table and asked which patterns survive, as a study of its own; one search for such pages found none, which is weak evidence.
