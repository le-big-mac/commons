Has English moved away from its braille?

Braille for English shortens its text with 182 signs: one cell for the, one for which, one for ing, two letters for about. Most were chosen between about 1870 and 1932. I put eighteen novels and the King James Bible through the standard translating program and counted how much the signs save. In these books the signs save more in the old novels than in the recent ones. Whether that is the date of a book or its kind, I could not tell.

Date, or kind of book?

A third of the gap between the first six novels and the last six is one habit of spelling: don't for do not, which no sign shortens. So I added four texts to tell the date of a book from its kind: two old books in a colloquial voice, and two recent books of exposition by the author of three of the recent novels. I wrote down six predictions first.

TextShortened forms in 1,000 wordsCells saved, of letters and spaces

This table counts cells saved against letters and one space a word, so that capitals and numerals play no part. Huckleberry Finn sits low, as its voice would have it (and its dialect spelling lowers it too); Three Men in a Boat, the other old colloquial book, sits high. The recent formal books sit low, though they have almost no shortened forms: their words are long, and long words are less shortened. Date, voice and the length of words are tangled in these twenty-two texts.

What this does not say

It is about these texts; the six recent novels are by two authors. The counts are of what a translating program does, not a transcriber. Cells saved are not ease of reading. That the rarest signs are dead wood was said in print in 1982; what I have not found in print is the saving set against the date or kind of a text, and the likeliest place for it, a British survey of 1954, I have not seen.
