In short - Most of the README's counts were published in 1986 by the agency that owned this table (Helmbold and Khan, CAA-TP-86-2), which the README never names: attackers win 61%, three to one wins 74%, "neither necessary nor sufficient", and a "World War II anomaly". - The headline split (71% against 51%) I did not find stated. 1-2. Claims and verdicts

  1. Bigger side won 60.2%; 70.9% world wars, 50.6% others (AUC 0.677, 0.500). FOUND IN PART, and contradicted as read. - Helmbold and Khan 1986, summary: "Force ratio is an unsatisfactory and inadequate predictor of victory in battle." Non-WWII subset (435 battles), p. 4-18: "one could do almost as well simply by using the average proportion of attacker victories as by using the fitted probability." §4-4, "THE WORLD WAR II ANOMALY" (in ADV, not force ratio): "the World War II data subset is quite different from the other data subsets"; leading hypothesis "that the WWII data are flawed". - Against: Rotte and Schmidt 2002 (IZA DP 491; same table, 625 rows; probit with leadership, morale, surprise), Table 3: marginal effect of force ratio 0.1277 (1600-1849), 0.0322 (1850-1929), 0.1029 (1930-1973); "numerical superiority has been a crucial quantitative determinant of battlefield success throughout history." Yigit 2000 (thesis, same 660 rows, war by war): "the general trend remains applicable for the overall analysis of the campaigns". - Lawrence 2019 (blog): 1600-1900, "The pattern here is not particularly clear"; division level 1904-1991, "The data shows a very clear pattern related to force ratios." - My counts (README's figures reproduced). Size: world-war rows 71, 75, 68, 70% in four bands of men engaged; others 45, 55, 49%. Composition: the attacker is outnumbered in 37 of 285 world-war rows, 132 of 318 others; were attackers' wins unrelated to numbers the bigger side would still win 63% against 52%, half the gap; the rest rests on those 37 rows (13 won). Leadership: the compilers credit one side with better leadership in 188 of 283 other rows, 42 of 304 world-war rows; where neither is credited, the bigger side won 56 of 87 other rows (64%; AUC 0.71). - I believe the counts, not the reading. The 60.2% itself: found nowhere. Fairly sure.
  2. Three to one, 83/108 = 77%. FOUND. Helmbold and Khan p. 3-20: "although the attacker wins about 74 percent of the battles in which the force ratio is at least 3, he also wins about 62 percent of the battles regardless"; Table 3-7: 78 won, 23 lost, 5 drawn of 106 (77% of decisive); "A 3 to 1 force ratio is neither necessary nor sufficient to assure a victory in a battle." Sure.
  3. Outnumbered attackers 58%, by period. FOUND IN PART. Same page: "still close to 50 percent even for FR values between 1/3 and 2/3". Lawrence 2019: under one to one, 54% (1600-1900); Arab-Israeli 76%, "16 were conducted by the Israelis".
  4. Attackers 64.7%. FOUND. "scoring the attacker as the victor in 61 percent of the battles" (368 of 598 with 34 draws: 65.2% of decisive); constant "at about 61 percent over the entire time period 1600-1979". Selection: Lawrence 2019, "the attacker choosing to attack because they have advantages".
  5. Prepared defences. NOT FOUND; Helmbold and Khan list "Fortifications significantly raise defender's P(WIN)?" as untested.
  6. Loser's fraction larger, 79.5%; defender's 65.2%. FOUND IN PART. Dupuy's verity (Institute post, 2016): "Casualty rates of winners are lower than those of losers." Helmbold and Khan: fractional exchange ratio "is less than unity in about two-thirds of the battles" (the 65.2%). 79.5% not found.
  7. Casualty fractions. FOUND IN PART, another measure. Yigit: "Casualty rates have declined generally over the past four centuries". Flat until 1939: not found.
  8. Surprise. FOUND IN PART (Dupuy, quoted 2016: "a factor ranging between 1.5 and 2.5"); percentages not found.
  9. r = +0.15. FOUND IN PART: verity "There is no direct relationship between force ratios and casualty rates." Number not found. 10, 11. Not searched.
