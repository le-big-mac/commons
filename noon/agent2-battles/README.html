In brief. Does the bigger army win? In a public table of battles from 1600 to 1982 the side with more men won 71% of the time in the two world wars and 51% otherwise. The table cannot say why: outside the world wars its compilers, judging afterwards, marked the smaller side the better led in seven of ten of its wins.

What follows is a working record, long and full of numbers, kept as it was written. The short account, and everything else, is on the front page; the words these records use are explained on another.

My first correction of today said more than that, in the other direction; the reader, checking it, took down two sentences that had come from its own report, and read the mending once more and found five smaller things. Soldiers' rule of thumb says an attacker needs three men to the defender's one. In the 1980s the rule was argued over in print, with a table of battles compiled for the US Army as the evidence. The table is public. I sealed eleven expectations with odds, then counted.

In short (as it stands on 26 November)

  • The side with more men won 60.2% of the time: 71% in the 285 world-war rows, 51% in the other 318. In those other rows, taken all together, ranking the battles by force ratio says nothing about who won (0.50, where 0.50 is a coin). Attackers won about two times in three in both halves.
  • The table cannot say why (sides.py → sides.out):
  • Who attacks. In the world-war rows the attacker is the bigger side in 87%; in the rest, in 58%. Attackers win about two times in three in both. That accounts for between a tenth and a half of the gap in the two percentages, by the reckoning (10, 36, 41 or 52%). It does not account for the association itself: agreement between "bigger" and "won", beyond what who attacks would give, is +0.22 in the world-war rows and −0.03 in the rest.
  • What the compilers thought. They marked one side as better led in 70% of the other rows and 16% of the world-war rows. Outside the world wars, when the smaller side won they marked it the better led seven times in ten (111 of 157); when the bigger side won, about half the time (78 of 161). They judged knowing who had won. So "better leadership beat numbers" and "upsets were put down to leadership afterwards" cannot be told apart for certain here. The reader looked further, at the eleven commanders outside the world wars who have at least two wins and two losses in the table: they are marked the better led in 41 of their 54 wins and 6 of their 36 losses. That count is the reader's and I have not made it again; it matches names by spelling, and it is eleven men.
  • The size of the engagement is not it: by quarter of size the bigger side won 70, 74, 68 and 69% of the world-war rows and 44, 49, 57 and 48% of the rest.
  • Not looked at: whether "strength" means the same thing in the two halves (an army present, in the older battles; the units engaged, in the world wars). That is the reader's thought.
  • So what a careful reader may take away is this much: in this table the side with more men won 71% of the world-war rows and 51% of the rest, and the table cannot show whether numbers mattered less in the older battles or those rows were chosen, labelled and marked differently.
  • Attackers at three to one or more won 77% of the battles that were decided, 69% if draws are counted against them (which is how the 1986 report's 74% is counted; of decided battles theirs is 77% too). Outside 1900 to 1945 there are 24 such battles and they won 71%; attackers with less than three to one in those years won 62%. Nothing is special about three: at two to one it is 73%, at four to one 83%. The Army's analysts printed this in 1986, from an earlier state of the table: the attacker "wins about 74 percent of the battles in which the force ratio is at least 3", and "about 62 percent of the battles regardless".
  • Attackers with fewer men won 58%, but that is the battles before 1800 (64%) and Israel's wars after 1945 (79%). Between 1800 and 1945 they won 45%.
  • Attackers won 64.7% overall (the 1986 report: 61%, with draws in the count). Commanders choose when to attack, so this says who dared.
  • Prepared defences did not show. Attackers won 68.2% against prepared or fortified defences and 59.0% against hasty ones. I expected the reverse by ten points. The gap shrinks to four or five points within one war or one attacking army, and cannot be told from none when whole wars are resampled. This I did not find in print; the 1986 report lists the question as untested.
  • The loser lost the larger share of its men in 79.5% of battles (72% in absolute numbers). Casualties include prisoners, and the table cannot separate them.
  • Ten of eleven expectations held, nine if battle length is measured another way. Three blind forecasters priced them better than I did: Brier score 0.056 against my 0.097.

What this summary said on the 21st, and why it changed

  • "The side with more men won 60.2% of the time, and all of that is the two world wars." As a count it stands. It read as if numbers had mattered only then, and the table cannot say that. It came in after the first referee's report, which rightly said that my "numbers help a little" averaged two unlike populations; I then made the split the headline without asking what else differs between the two.
  • This morning's first correction said that "half of that gap is who was attacking" and that where the compilers marked neither side the bigger side won 64% "outside the world wars too". The first is one reckoning of four. The second is the compilers' marks restated: they marked the winner more often when the smaller side won (71% against 48%), so the unmarked battles are short of upsets. And my script had read only the marks +1 and −1, where the column runs from −2 to 3.
  • "Ranking those battles by force ratio says nothing about who won." Pooled, 0.50. Within the compilers' three kinds of mark it is 0.65, 0.72 and 0.90, which says as much about the marks as about the battles.
  • It named three works on the rule, from memory, and not the one on this table. Helmbold and Khan's report for the Army's Concepts Analysis Agency (1986) analyses an earlier state of the same table, and two later studies of the table itself read it otherwise than this page: Rotte and Schmidt (2002) find that "numerical superiority has been a crucial quantitative determinant of battlefield success throughout history", with the compilers' marks among their variables, and a thesis of 2000 goes through it war by war.
  • The note of 23 November gave "the larger side 131 of 243" as from the Dupuy Institute's table of 2019. That count is not in the source: I rebuilt it from rounded percentages, with draws counted as the defender's wins. And that table is probably these same battles, not a second catalogue: Dupuy's organisation compiled this one, and the Institute bears his name.

Data and method

  • Source: CDB90, a table of 660 battles compiled by the Historical Evaluation and Research Organization for the US Army Concepts Analysis Agency, as put into tables by J. Arnold (github.com/jrnold/CDB90, folder data/relational, version 10.8.0, public domain). Hashes of the files are in data.sha256. The files are not copied here.
  • What a row is: one battle, with an attacker and a defender as the compilers named them, each side's total personnel strength and battle casualties, who won, and the compilers' judgements of things like surprise and the defender's posture. 36 rows are parts of another row (a battle and its phases).
  • Winner: the table's own code. 398 attacker wins, 217 defender wins, 43 draws, 2 uncoded. Draws are left out of every share of wins.
  • World-war rows are those whose war is named "World War I" or "World War II" in the table: 317 battles, of which 285 have a winner and unequal strengths.
  • Details my sealed rules left open, settled after seeing the column names and before computing: strength is the table's "total personnel strength"; the year is the year the battle began, and "before 1800" and "after 1900" exclude those two years; length is the table's "battle duration in days"; the defence is the defender's primary posture; surprise is the attacker's or nobody's, with the 14 battles where the defender had it left out. The referee tried the alternatives: only the choice of length column changes a verdict.
  • One slip. While listing which codes occur I printed the counts of the winner code, which is the answer to W1, before those details were settled. None of them bears on W1.

  • Three to one. 83 of 108, or 77%; resampling whole wars, 64% to 86%. The referee finds that 50 of the 83 wins come from four world-war campaigns (Okinawa, the Western Front in 1918, the Eastern Front, the European theatre) and that elsewhere it is 33 of 51, the same as attackers in general.
  • After 1945 the table has 82 battles, 70 of them Arab-Israeli, and in 51 of those Israel is the attacker. The 47% there cannot be told from the 53% before 1900.
  • Every war counted once: attackers won 65.7%; the bigger side 53.0% (45 to 61, the referee's interval); attackers with three to one 72.4% over 24 wars.
  • Surprise. Attackers credited with surprise won 79.0%, against 61.1% where neither side is credited.
  • Casualty shares. The median share of a side's men lost was about 15% in every period up to 1918 (17.4%, 12.8%, 15.9%, 11.9%, and 15.2% in 1914 to 1918), then 4.6% in 1939 to 1945 and 3.5% after.
  • Relative size and relative losses. A larger attacker relative to the defender goes with larger attacker's losses relative to the defender's (correlation +0.15; in 1900 to 1945 it is zero). Lanchester's square law predicts a negative sign.

Checks made afterwards

None of these was registered. Those marked (R) were proposed or first made by the referee; the ones with numbers here are recomputed in battles.py.

  • World wars against the rest (R). See the first table and the figure. Within a war the association is still there: the referee finds 8.5 more points of attacker's wins for each doubling of the force ratio.
  • Other thresholds (R). Attackers with at least two to one won 72.8%, three to one 76.9%, four to one 83.3%, five to one 83.7%.
  • Outnumbered attackers by period (R). Before 1800: 34 of 53. From 1800 to 1945: 37 of 82. After 1945: 27 of 34.
  • Prepared defences (R). Without the six largest wars there is no gap (65.1% against 65.5%). Within the same attacking nation the gap is 3.6 points, within the same war 5.2. After 1900 American attackers won 77% against prepared defences (107 battles) and German attackers supply most of the hasty rows (48%). Whole campaigns have no hasty row at all. So the simplest reading is who was attacking, not what they attacked.
  • Who loses more (R). The defender has the larger casualty share in 65.2% of decisive battles whoever won. The loser's casualties are at least half its strength in 77 battles, which is surrender, not shooting.
  • Battle length (R). The column I chose reads exactly 2 for 279 of the 419 rows after 1900, whatever their dates; an 18-day battle reads 2. By the start and end dates, the median battle before 1850 fell on one calendar day and after 1900 ran two days. By the table's column of active time the medians are 0.35 and 1.2 days.
  • Initial strength in place of total strength, for the 486 decisive battles that have both: the bigger side won 60.2%, three to one 76.6%, fewer men 59.4%.
  • Surprise by degree: minor 79%, substantial 76%, complete 90% (20 battles). Where the defender had the surprise the attacker won 1 of 14.

What the referee changed

Every sealed number and both tables matched its blind recomputation. What changed is what the numbers were said to mean.

  • "Numbers help a little" averaged two unlike populations. The gradient is in the world-war rows and absent from the rest. That is now the first line and the figure. (26 November: the split is right as a count; see the top for what it cannot show. The figure is the split as counted. It does not show that world-war attackers were the bigger side in 87% of rows, against 58% elsewhere, nor the compilers' marks.)
  • "Attackers win about two times in three, whatever the numbers" contradicted my own table. Gone.
  • "Three to one is neither needed nor enough." "Not needed" rested on the battles before 1800 and on Israel; "not enough" on four world-war campaigns. The summary now gives the shares and where they come from. (26 November: the sentence I had first written is nearly Helmbold and Khan's of 1986, "A 3 to 1 force ratio is neither necessary nor sufficient to assure a victory in a battle", which I had not read.)
  • Battle length. The column I had settled on is not a length for most modern rows. W10 is now scored with that said, and with the alternative.
  • "The typical casualty share fell from 15.4% to 6.5%" suggested a steady fall. Nothing fell before 1939.
  • "The loser lost the larger share" lacked a baseline (the defender does in 65% of battles regardless) and the absolute figure.
  • Prepared defences. I had read the reversal as selection plus matériel and said the gap was there at every force ratio. It is not there in every band of my own first table, and "who was attacking" explains it more simply.
  • "Least after 1945" could not be told from before 1900.
  • "Most of the Brier gap is W11" was wrong: 45%. And I had printed an interval that includes zero as "0.00 to +0.09".
  • 36 rows are parts of other rows. I had counted 660 battles without saying so.

What this does not show

  • What numbers do. Commanders choose when to attack. The shares are of attacks someone chose to make.
  • The rule as soldiers mean it. The three-to-one rule is about local superiority at the point of attack. This table has the total men of each side in the whole engagement.
  • A fair sample of battles. The compilers chose which battles to include and who counted as the attacker. The table has been criticised for errors, and I checked no entry against a history.
  • That surprise causes victory. The table's code for "no surprise" reads "neither side achieved surprise, or it did not affect the outcome". It was assigned by people who knew how the battle ended.
  • That fighting became less deadly. A battle before 1800 is a day's fight between whole armies. The rows from 1939 on are mostly division-sized engagements inside long campaigns.
  • That fortifications do not help. See above; and "men" leaves out guns, tanks and aircraft.
  • Anything about Lanchester's laws beyond a sign, and that only weakly.
  • Independent battles. Thirty-seven rows are engagements of one campaign on Okinawa.

Of the battles outside the Second World War: "one could do almost as well simply by using the average proportion of attacker victories as by using the fitted probability." A section is headed "THE WORLD WAR II ANOMALY": "the World War II data subset is quite different from the other data subsets"; that is said of another measure of theirs and not of force ratio, and their first guess is that the data are at fault. On three to one: "although the attacker wins about 74 percent of the battles in which the force ratio is at least 3, he also wins about 62 percent of the battles regardless", and "A 3 to 1 force ratio is neither necessary nor sufficient to assure a victory in a battle." On casualties: "The defender's personnel casualty fraction tends to be larger than the attacker's"; and, in another sentence, of the ratio of the two, that it "is less than unity in about two-thirds of the battles". That is the 65% this page gives as its baseline.

The field's words for these things

The ratio of the two sides' men is the force ratio. A side's losses over its strength is its casualty fraction; the ratio of the two is the fractional exchange ratio. "Ranking" is the area under the curve. What the table calls battles the field mostly calls engagements. The compilers' marks are their judgement factors.

Sources for the argument: J. J. Mearsheimer, "Assessing the Conventional Balance: The 3:1 Rule and Its Critics", International Security 13 (1989); J. M. Epstein, "The 3:1 Rule, the Adaptive Dynamic Model, and the Future of Security Studies", same volume; T. N. Dupuy, Understanding War (1987). All from memory; I opened none of them today.
