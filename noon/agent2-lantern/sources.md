# What I read, where it is, and what I looked for

Noon (agent2), 25 November 2026. Fetched files are kept outside this folder (`~/lantern-data/`), each source in a folder of
its own; `quotes.py` looks for every quotation of `quotes.json` in the fetched text. Thirty-four quotations, thirty-four found
(`quotes.out`). The scanned texts are a machine's reading of old print: where it misread a word the checker is given the
words as scanned, and the README quotes them as printed.

1. **Thomas Young, A Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy and the Mechanical Arts, vol. 1 (London: J. Johnson, 1807).**
   Internet Archive, item `lecturescourseof01younrich`: the text (`_djvu.txt`) and one page image, leaf 847, which is Plate
   XXVIII (`https://archive.org/download/lecturescourseof01younrich/page/n847_w1400.jpg`, served at 2340 × 3086 pixels;
   sha256 in `expectations.md`). The plate is lettered "Pub. by J. Johnson, London 1 July 1806" and "Joseph Skelton sc."
   - The caption of Fig. 401, among the explanations of the plates: "An arrangement proposed for a phantasmagoria."; "when
     the box is drawn back on its wheels, the rod I K lowers the point K, and by means of the rod K L adjusts the slider in
     such a manner, that the image is always distinctly painted on the screen G."; "The rod K N must be equal to I K, and
     the point I must be twice the focal length of the lens F, before the object, L being immediately under the focus of
     the lens."; "When the box advances towards the screen, in order that the images may be diminished and appear to
     vanish, the support of the lens F suffers the screen M to fall and intercept a part of the light."; "The screen M may
     have a triangular opening, so as to uncover the middle of the lens only, or the light may be intercepted in any other
     manner."
   - Lecture XXXV, p. 418: "the distance of the image from the principal focus nearest to it being always inversely as the
     distance of the object from the principal focus on the opposite side."
   - Lecture XXXVI, p. 426: "By removing the lantern to different distances, and altering at the same time more or less
     the position of the lens, the image may be made to increase or diminish, and to become more or less distinct at
     pleasure"; "In reality, however, these figures become much brighter as they are rendered smaller".
   I read the lecture's two paragraphs on the lantern and the phantasmagoria, the caption, and the sentences round p. 418;
   not the rest of the volume, and not volume 2 (where Young's mathematical elements are).

2. **David Brewster, Letters on Natural Magic (1832; the text is Project Gutenberg's of the seventh edition, 1856),
   Letter IV.** Internet Archive, item `lettersonnatural51645gut`. Read: the letter's pages on the magic lantern and the
   phantasmagoria, from the lantern's improvements to the "catadioptrical phantasmagoria".
   - "An exhibition depending on these principles was brought out by M. Philipstall in 1802, under the name of the
     _Phantasmagoria_"
   - "The variation of the distance of the lens D from the sliders in EF was not exactly adapted to the motion of the
     lantern to and from the screen, so that the outline of the figures was not equally distinct during their variations of
     magnitude."
   - "Dr. Thomas Young suggested the arrangement shown in Fig. 6 for exhibiting the phantasmagoria."; "so that the image
     is always in the conjugate focus of D, and therefore distinctly painted on the screen."; "The rod KN must be equal in
     length to IK, and the point I must be twice the focal length of the lens D before the object, L being immediately
     under the focus of the lens."
   - Of the shade: "This method, however, has many disadvantages".
   - "The method recommended by Dr. Young for pulling out and pushing in the object lens D, according as the lantern
     approaches to or recedes from the screen, is very ingenious and effective. It is, however, clumsy in itself, and the
     connexion of the levers with the screen, and their interposition between it and the lantern, must interfere with the
     operations of the exhibitor."
   - "It is, besides, suited only to short distances between the screen and the lantern; for when that distance is
     considerable, as it must sometimes require to be, the levers KL, KI, KT, would bend by the least strain, and become
     unfitted for their purpose." (His figure, like Young's, letters the third rod KN; "KT" is the book's.)
   - "For these reasons the mechanism which adjusts the lens D should be moved by the axle of the front wheels, the tube
     which contains the lens should be kept at its greatest distance from EF by a slender spring, and should be pressed to
     its proper distance by the action of a spiral cam suited to the optical relation between the two conjugate focal
     distances of the lens."
   Brewster does not say that he saw the show himself.

3. **Miguel Herrero Herrero, "The last Fantascope?", The Magic Lantern (the Magic Lantern Society's journal), one page**
   (`https://www.magiclantern.org.uk/the-magic-lantern/pdfs/4011018a.pdf`). Of the large lanterns used for the
   phantasmagoria: "Some have an autofocus mechanism built into the wheels." Nothing on how.

4. **"Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage", Wikipedia** (plain text by its interface, 25 November 2026): "The Peaucellier–Lipkin
   linkage (or Peaucellier–Lipkin cell, or Peaucellier–Lipkin inversor), invented in 1864, was the first true planar
   straight-line mechanism"; and, of its bars, "Since OA and AD are both fixed lengths, then the product of OB and OD is a
   constant". An encyclopaedia, not Peaucellier's letter, which I have not read.

5. **R. J. Noye, "Phantasmagoria", a page of his notes on the lantern in South Australia**
   (`https://noye.agsa.sa.gov.au/Lantern/Phantas.htm`). "By the time photography was invented, in 1839, the phantasmagoria
   was well-known and a description and illustration of the apparatus was published in the Encyclopedia Britannica"; and of
   that illustration: "A combination of frames, sliders, rods, levers and other contraptions caused the lens to slide
   backwards and forwards to give the correct focus, while a mask with a triangular cutout was allowed to rise and fall in
   front of the lens to give a constant intensity of light as the lantern was moved." By that description it is Young's
   arrangement, shade and all. The page does not name him, takes the drawing for apparatus in use, and says nothing of whether the focus is
   exact. I have not seen the encyclopaedia itself. (Written before the addition; I have now: source 7.)

6. **Joseph Becker, United States patent 1,103,343, "Method of Adjusting and Regulating Automatically-Focused Enlarging or
   Copying Cameras", patented 14 July 1914** on an application filed 9 February 1910, with priority from an application of
   10 July 1905 (his patent 1,103,342). Google Patents' page (`https://patents.google.com/patent/US1103343/en`), whose text
   is a machine's reading of the print. **The referee found this in its blind step; I had not.** I then fetched the page
   and read the description through its statement of the linkage, its theory and its account of what is old.
   - "upon the application of Joseph Becker, of Washington, District of Columbia, for an improvement in Methods of
     Adjusting and Regulating Automatically-Focused Enlarging or Copying Cameras"
   - "my invention consists in connecting the three frames by rods which are pivoted to the frames and to each other in
     such manner as to keep the image always in coincidence with conjugate foci of the lens"
   - "by my linkage, comprising two equal links 20 and 21 and a shorter link 22."
   - Of the three pivots' axes: "may lie in any plane parallel with the direction of motion of carriages 13 and 14."
   - Of the links' lengths: "may have any values such that the difference of their squares shall be equal to the square of
     the focal length of the lens 9 used"
   - "The lens 9 may be either simple or compound."
   - "Equation (4) is referred to by French authors as Newton's formula"
   - Of two further forms of the linkage, with more links: "are both old as elements in straight line or so-called parallel
     motion mechanism."; of one of them, "comprises six of the seven links used in Peaucellier's beam"; and of a form whose
     second half ties the three axes together, that they "will remain in perfect alinement without a slide way", "so that
     no useless side pressures are put on the slides".
   - Of the short link made adjustable: "it is no longer necessary to measure the distance B to D, as proposed above".
   The claims, as the page gives them, are of a method of adjusting; I have not read his earlier patent 1,103,342.
   The words "Young", "lantern" and "phantasmagoria" do not occur in the page's text.

## What I searched for

Eleven searches of the web. A search is a machine's report of what it found, and I say so where I lean on one.

- For how the lanterns of the phantasmagoria were focused: four searches (Robertson's fantascope and its focusing; the
  English phantasmagoria lanterns; the same in French; "autofocus" built into the wheels). They gave Herrero's page and
  general histories; none describes a mechanism.
- For the texts: two (Brewster's letter; Young's lectures and their plates).
- For focusing linkages in enlargers: one. It named a paper in Photogrammetric Engineering, March 1955, pp. 127-131, and
  summarised it as listing "Pythagorean right-angle, Carpentier, and Peaucellier" inversors for automatic focusing. The
  file was no longer at its address, and I have not read it. So that enlargers were focused by such linkages in the
  twentieth century I have only by the search's summary.
- For whether anyone has said that Young's arrangement is exact, or is an inversor, or was the first of its kind: three,
  one of them the thorough kind. Nothing.
- For what nineteenth-century books made of it: one. It gave Noye's page.

The referee, in its blind step, made seven searches of its own (`referee/blind-reading.md`, 7) and found Becker's patent,
which mine had not. Neither of us found anyone who sets it beside Young's figure.

Not read, and likely to speak of Young's proposal: the printed histories of the magic lantern.
