Dr Young's rods

In the phantasmagoria of the 1790s a ghost grew as the magic lantern rolled back from the screen and shrank as it rolled up to it. The lens had to be moved at every step, or the ghost went soft. In 1806 Thomas Young drew three rods that were to do it of themselves. On paper they do it exactly.

Behind the screen

Why it works

A lens paints a sharp image when the slider's distance from one principal focus, times the screen's distance from the other, is the square of the focal length. Young says as much in his lectures. He puts I two focal lengths before the slider and L under the lens's focus, so that IL and LN are those two distances. And because IK and KN are equal, the three pivots I, L, N being in one line, IL × LN is always IK² − KL², however the rods lean: that is a theorem about a point inside a circle. Cut KL so that IK² − KL² is the focal length squared, and the ghost is sharp at every size.

Young's caption does not say how long KL must be, nor that I, L and N must lie in one line, nor that N must be at the screen. Try the faults above. The same theorem is in the linkage that Peaucellier published in 1864, whose second half keeps the three points in line of itself; and the same three rods, with their conditions written out, are described in a patent of 1914 for photographic enlargers.

Young's caption was reprinted in a dozen books. The seventh and eighth editions of the Encyclopaedia Britannica (title pages of 1842, and of 1853 to 1860) give it without his name and with one letter changed: "the rods KN and KL must be equal", where Young has KN and IK. Rods made so keep IL × IN the same, not IL × LN. Choose them above: the ghost is nearly sharp when it is large and soft when it is small.

That is on paper. In wood and brass the short rod KL stands nearly upright when the ghost is small, and then it presses the lens's slider on its bar far harder than it pushes it along: with long rods and an ordinary slide it would stick. Young's own drawing has short rods.
