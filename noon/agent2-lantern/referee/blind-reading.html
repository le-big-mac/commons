BLIND READING: Young's rods

Clock: began 14:33 UTC, ended 14:43; inside the 30 minutes. The web took about 7 minutes of that (roughly 14:35 to 14:42). Assumed throughout for numbers: f = 150 mm, lens f/4, picture on the slide 75 mm wide, IK = KN = 1550 mm, sizes m = 2 to 20.

1. The arrangement and the claim - A lantern in a box H on wheels faces a fixed screen G; the slide E is fixed in the box and the lens F rides on a slide of its own. - Three rods meet at a joint K above: IK to a pivot I on an arm of the box, KN to a pivot N on the screen, KL to a pivot L on the lens slide. - As the box is drawn back K sinks and KL pushes the lens toward the slide; as it advances the lens is drawn out. - Claim: "the image is always distinctly painted on the screen", at every distance. - Conditions he gives: KN = IK; I twice the focal length before the object; L immediately under the focus of the lens. - A shade M, let fall by the lens support as the box advances, is to dim the shrinking image.

2. Is it true? Exactly, in paraxial optics, given six conditions, three of them unstated. Proof: - Let I, L, N lie on one line, with IK = KN = a and KL = b. The circle about K of radius a passes through I and N. - The power of L with respect to that circle gives LI · LN = a² − b², whatever the position of K (L lies between I and N, so b < a). - Put I at 2f before the object, L under the screen-side focus, N in the screen plane. With u, v the object and image distances, LI = u − f and LN = v − f. - So the rods enforce (u − f)(v − f) = a² − b². Newton's relation, which Young gives on p. 418, is (u − f)(v − f) = f². - The two agree for every position if and only if a² − b² = f². I checked this numerically: the worst lens error over m = 1.02 to 20 is 6e-11 mm (faults.py).

Conditions: - C1, IK = KN: stated by Young and by Brewster. - C2, I at 2f before the object: stated by both. - C3, L under the focus: stated by both; neither says it is the focus on the screen side. - C4, N in the plane of the screen: stated by neither; only drawn. - C5, I, L, N in one straight line parallel to the travel of the box and of the lens slide: stated by neither. - C6, IK² − KL² = f²: stated by neither. It is implied if the thing is assembled in focus once with C1 to C5 kept, because KL is then whatever joins K to L. Miss it and the image is in focus nowhere.

Further points: - Brewster repeats Young's three conditions nearly word for word and adds none. He writes "KT" for KN. - The length IK is free and sets the range: m + 1/m ≤ 2·IK/f. - At m = 1 the linkage is at a dead point (KL upright) and cannot pass through it. - For a thick lens "2f" must read "the distance between the two focal points". - More generally the three pivots may all be shifted together: p, p − f, p − 2f from object, lens and screen, for any p. - Lens aberrations are outside the claim.

5. Brewster's objections and his cam (weigh.py) - Bending, at short throws: a deal lath 30 × 12 mm on edge, 1.55 m long, sags 0.5 mm under its own weight and shortens 0.0005 mm; its Euler load is 177 N against a few newtons of slide friction. The objection is empty here. - Bending, at long throws: at 3.1 m (throw 6.3 m) the lath sags 8.6 mm, shortens 0.06 mm, Euler load 44 N: still passable. At 6.2 m (throw 12.5 m) it sags 138 mm, shortens 8 mm, Euler load 11 N: it fails without trussing. A 6 mm iron rod fails already at 3.1 m (sag 212 mm). - So "bend by the least strain" holds only beyond about 6 m of throw. - Short distances only: true, but for another reason first. The throw is at most about 2·IK, and K stands nearly IK above the pivots when the lantern is close: 1.5 m for a 3.3 m throw, 3.1 m for 6.3 m. - Clumsy: fair. As drawn the rods cross the light cone and the space between lantern and screen; they would have to be set to one side. - Cam, travel: m = 2 to 20 needs 2632 mm, which is 5.6 turns of a 150 mm wheel. A cam on the axle itself serves less than one turn (471 mm), so it needs about 6:1 gearing or a scroll. - Cam, lift: 67.5 mm in all, of which the last 7.5 mm serves m = 10 to 20. - Cam, errors: profile error passes one for one, with no multiplication. Wheel slip of 0.5% of the travel (13 mm) gives a lens error of 4.4 mm at m = 2 (blur 1/137), 0.55 mm at m = 5 (1/680), 0.13 mm at m = 10. Slip accumulates until the cam is re-zeroed. - What the cam cannot do: know where the screen is (it reckons from the wheels; the rods are tied to the screen); be made without computing and cutting a curve; serve a new lens without a new cam. - What the rods cannot do: long throws and low rooms; leave the stage clear; follow any law but a constant product (a cam could take the lens's real behaviour, or drive the shade); escape the multiplication of their errors at small sizes. - Neither passes m = 1.

6. The shade - Screen brightness goes as 1/v² (evenly lit slide) or 1/m² (all the light through the lens). From m = 20 to m = 2 that is 49 or 100 times. - For constant brightness the open part of the lens must fall to 27% at m = 10, 8% at m = 5 and 2% (or 1%) at m = 2; less still for "fainter". - A shade at the lens dims the whole picture evenly, so the place is right. Stopping down also sharpens the small images, where the rods are worst: 2% of the area cuts blur about 7 times. - The law is the difficulty. The opening must go as (1 + f/x)², x being the lens's distance beyond focus: steep in the first 7.5 mm of travel and flat after. A plain fall with a triangular notch does not give that; only a cut profile would. - If the condensers bring the lamp's image to the lens, as his Fig. 400 caption says ("focus is near the magnifying lens"), the light fills only the middle of the lens. A notch that uncovers "the middle of the lens only" then dims little until it bites the flame's image, and then unevenly. - Verdict: rough dimming, yes; a true law, no. Brewster's "many disadvantages" is unargued.

Findings: - I found nothing that discusses whether Young's arrangement is exact, and nothing that ties it to a later mechanism. - Closest later mechanism: J. Becker, US patent 1,103,343 (granted 1914, filed 1910, priority 1905), automatically focused enlarging cameras, https://patents.google.com/patent/US1103343. As the fetch tool reported it: "two equal links 20 and 21 and a shorter link 22"; "the difference of their squares shall be equal to the square of the focal length"; pivot axes in a plane parallel with the motion; it cites Newton's Opticks; it says the linkage "comprises six of the seven links used in Peaucellier's beam"; it names no Young. That is Young's arrangement with C5 and C6 supplied. I read the tool's summary, not the patent text. - Related Becker patents turned up but were not opened: US895968, US1103342. - A 1955 Photogrammetric Engineering paper on a rack-and-pinion inversor (search snippet only; the address gave 404): the inversor "as an autofocus", listing "Pythagorean right-angle, Carpentier, Paucellier" kinds. https://asprs.org/wp-content/uploads/pers/1955journal/mar/1955_mar_127-131.pdf - Peaucellier 1864, Lipkin 1871: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peaucellier%E2%80%93Lipkin_linkage - My own reading, not found in print: Young's three rods are half a Peaucellier cell with the pole L inside, the slides doing the work of the other half, 57 years earlier.
