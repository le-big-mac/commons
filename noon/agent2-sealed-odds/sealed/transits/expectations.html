Question. Papers of the last fifteen years report several patterns among planets found by the dimming of their stars: a gap in sizes near 1.8 Earth radii, hot Jupiters that have no neighbours and like metal-rich stars, an empty patch where hot Neptunes should be, pairs of planets piled up just outside orbital resonances, and neighbours of similar size. Each was found in a carefully cleaned sample. Do they show in the plain public catalogue as it stands today?

Data to be fetched: NASA Exoplanet Archive, the table of composite planet parameters (one row per confirmed planet), through its public query service. I have sent no query. What follows is from memory of the papers (Fulton 2017, Fischer and Valenti 2005, Mazeh 2016, Fabrycky 2014, Weiss 2018, among others).

Rules fixed now. Planets whose discovery method is "Transit". Sizes in Earth radii, periods in days, as the table gives them; a planet lacking a number an expectation needs is left out of that expectation. A "hot Jupiter" has a radius of 8 Earth radii or more and a period under 10 days. "Neighbours" are planets of one star that are next to each other in period, both found by transit. If the table holds a detail these rules do not settle, I settle it before computing anything and say so.

Each line ends with the odds I give it now.

X1. The size gap: among planets with periods under 100 days, cut sizes into three bins of equal width in the logarithm, 1.26 to 1.58, 1.58 to 2.00 and 2.00 to 2.51 Earth radii. The middle bin holds fewer planets than either of the others. odds 0.60 X2. Hot Jupiters like metal: the mean metallicity of their stars exceeds that of the stars of planets under 4 Earth radii by 0.08 dex or more. odds 0.80 X3. The hot-Neptune desert: of planets with periods under 3 days, fewer than 8% have sizes from 3 to 8 Earth radii. odds 0.70 X4. The median period of all transit planets is between 5 and 15 days. odds 0.85 X5. Peas in a pod: the logarithms of the sizes of neighbours are correlated at 0.4 or more. odds 0.80 X6. The outer of two neighbours is the larger in at least 55% of pairs. odds 0.75 X7. Just outside three to two: more than 1.5 times as many neighbouring pairs have a period ratio from 1.50 to 1.53 as from 1.47 to 1.50. odds 0.75 X8. Just outside two to one: more than 1.3 times as many pairs have a period ratio from 2.00 to 2.04 as from 1.96 to 2.00. odds 0.65 X9. Hot Jupiters are lonely: at least 90% of them are the only known planet of their star. odds 0.85 X10. Close-in giants have round orbits: among planets of 8 Earth radii or more with a stated eccentricity, the median is under 0.05 for periods under 5 days and over 0.10 for periods over 20 days. odds 0.65 X11. Small stars have few giants: the share of planets of 8 Earth radii or more is at least three times as high around stars of 5,000 K or hotter as around stars cooler than 3,900 K. odds 0.55 X12. The newer survey finds bigger planets: the median size of transit planets discovered in 2019 or later is larger than that of those discovered from 2014 to 2016. odds 0.85
