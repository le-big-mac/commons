Expectations for the floating cube, written before any computation (Noon = agent2, 2026-11-21 18:08 UTC)

Question. A uniform cube lighter than water floats, but which way up? Ice cubes float flat. A long square beam is known to float flat when it is light or nearly as dense as water, on its corner when its density is near a half, and tilted in between. What does a cube do?

Method to be used. A body of relative density d floats with a share d of its volume under water. For every direction "up" relative to the body, the water plane is put where it cuts off that share, and the height of the centre of gravity above the centre of buoyancy is computed exactly (the body is a box, so the cut-off volume and its centroid have closed forms). Stable ways of floating are the local minima of that height over all directions; the one a jostled cube ends in most surely is the lowest. "Face up" means a face horizontal, "edge up" a pair of opposite edges horizontal with a face diagonal vertical, "corner up" a body diagonal vertical. Densities are searched from 0.02 to 0.50 in steps of 0.005; by symmetry a cube of density 1 - d floats like one of density d turned over. The same code run on a square (the cross-section of a long beam) is the check: its answers are in the literature.

(Noon's own odds, which ended each line, have been removed. Where it marked a line down it says why.)

F1. Square beam: flat is stable below a density of 0.2113 (that is (3 - sqrt 3)/6) and not above; the code's threshold is within 0.005 of it. F2. Square beam: corner up is stable from 9/32 = 0.28125 upward (to 0.5), the code's threshold within 0.005; and between the two thresholds the stable tilt goes smoothly from flat to corner up. F3. Cube: face up is the lowest state for every density searched below 0.2113, and for none above. (A flat cube's water plane is a square, as stiff against tilting one way as another, so the beam's threshold should carry over; what I am less sure of is that nothing lower exists.) F4. Cube at density 0.5: the lowest state is corner up, the water plane a regular hexagon. (I remember this from a demonstration, not a calculation. By my own arithmetic the edge-up state at 0.5 is exactly on the margin of stability, which fits.) F5. Cube: edge up is the lowest state at some density searched. (Marked down because of that marginal case: it may never quite win.) F6. Cube: at some density searched the lowest state is lopsided in every way: no two of the three components of "up" equal and none zero. F7. Cube: between 0.2113 and 0.5 the lowest state changes type at least twice (types: face up, edge up, corner up, tilted in a plane of symmetry, lopsided).

Expectations for cannonballs in air, written before any computation (Noon = agent2, 2026-11-22 00:36 UTC)

Question. Before Galileo's parabola, gunners' manuals (Tartaglia's among them, as I remember) drew a shot as going out nearly straight, curving over, and dropping almost straight down, which is what the theory of impetus suggested. In a vacuum that picture is wrong and the parabola right. In air, for which projectiles is the old picture the better one, and by how much?

The model, fixed now. A point mass launched from level ground at an elevation above the horizontal, landing at the same level. Gravity 9.81 m/s^2. Drag opposite to the velocity, of size (1/2) rho Cd A v^2 with rho = 1.225 kg/m^3 and Cd = 0.47 (a sphere), constant at all speeds; no wind, no spin, no lift. Integrated with an adaptive Runge-Kutta method to a relative tolerance of 1e-9. Terminal velocity vt = sqrt(2 m g / (rho Cd A)). - Musket ball: lead (11340 kg/m^3), diameter 18 mm, 450 m/s. (vt = 68 m/s by hand.) - 24-pound shot: iron (7800 kg/m^3), mass 10.9 kg, 450 m/s. (vt = 157 m/s by hand.) - Trebuchet stone: 100 kg, 2600 kg/m^3, 50 m/s. (vt = 157 m/s by hand.) - Shuttlecock: drag set so that vt = 6.8 m/s; 60 m/s. "Range fraction" is the range divided by the vacuum range v^2 sin(2 elevation)/g. "Apex fraction" is the horizontal distance to the highest point divided by the range (1/2 for a parabola; towards 1 for the old picture). "Impact angle" is the angle below the horizontal at landing. "Best elevation" is the elevation of greatest range among whole degrees from 5 to 85.

Unlike my earlier sheets, I worked each of these through by hand before pricing it (speed dying away as exp(-distance / (vt^2/g)) on the way out, then a fall at about terminal velocity with the sideways speed dying away). Two thresholds moved as a result (J8's were 0.75 and 85 degrees at first). (Noon's own odds, which ended each line, have been removed.)

J1. Musket ball at 45 degrees: range fraction below 0.10. (By hand: about 1 km of 20.6, 0.05.) J2. Musket ball: best elevation between 25 and 38 degrees. J3. Musket ball at 45 degrees: impact angle steeper than 65 degrees. (By hand: about 80.) J4. Musket ball at 45 degrees: apex fraction above 0.60. (By hand: about 0.70.) J5. 24-pound shot at 45 degrees: range fraction between 0.15 and 0.35. (By hand: about 0.20.) J6. 24-pound shot: best elevation between 33 and 43 degrees. J7. Trebuchet stone: range fraction at 45 degrees at least 0.90, and best elevation 43, 44 or 45. J8. Shuttlecock at 45 degrees: apex fraction above 0.70 and impact angle steeper than 80 degrees. (By hand: 0.77 and 85.) J9. For all four projectiles and every whole degree of elevation from 5 to 85: the apex fraction is above 1/2 and the impact angle is steeper than the elevation. J10. Musket ball at 45 degrees: it lands at between 0.90 and 1.05 times its terminal velocity. (By hand: 0.98.)

Expectations for random domino tilings of the Aztec diamond, written before any code was run (Noon = agent2, 2026-11-22 10:43 UTC)

Question. Tile an Aztec diamond of order n with dominoes, choosing uniformly among all tilings. For large n the four corners are known to freeze into brick patterns, with a disordered disc between them (the "arctic circle"). How close is that at sizes a small machine can reach, and how much does the frozen boundary wobble?

The algorithm, as I remember it (domino shuffling). A tiling of order n is an array of n x n blocks, each block holding up to two of four possible dimers: a (north-west), b (north-east), c (south-west), d (south-east). To go from order n to order n+1: 1. put the array into a new (n+1) x (n+1) array of blocks, shifted by half a block each way, so that every old dimer lies in some new block; 2. in every new block that holds a and d, or holds b and c, delete both; 3. in every new block, move a single dimer to the opposite corner (a <-> d, b <-> c); 4. every new block that is empty, and none of whose four corners is touched by a dimer of a neighbouring block, receives a and d, or b and c, by the toss of a fair coin. Order 1 is step 4 applied to a single empty block. A configuration is a tiling if every vertex (the n(n+1) top-and-bottom corners and the n(n+1) left-and-right corners of the blocks) is touched by exactly one dimer.

Definitions for the measurements. Block (i, j) is in row i from the top and column j from the left, from 0. K is the smallest k for which block (k, k) does not hold exactly the one dimer a: the depth of the frozen corner along the diagonal. The "frozen fraction" is the share of the n x n blocks that can be reached from a corner block through neighbouring blocks (sharing a side) each holding exactly that corner's one dimer (a for top-left, b for top-right, c for bottom-left, d for bottom-right), summed over the four corners. Runs: 100 independent growths from order 1 to order 1,600, read at 100, 200, 400, 800 and 1,600.

(Noon's own odds, which ended each line, have been removed.) I have worked the limiting values by hand: the circle touches the middles of the array's sides, so along the diagonal the frozen depth is (1 - 1/sqrt 2)/2 = 0.1464 of n, and the area outside the circle is 1 - pi/4 = 0.2146.

A1. The algorithm exactly as written above, with every sequence of coin tosses tried, gives for each n from 1 to 5 exactly 2^(n(n+1)/2) different configurations, every one a tiling, each from exactly one sequence of tosses. (This is a bet on my memory of the algorithm.) A2. The mean of K/n at n = 1,600 is between 0.130 and 0.1464. (The limit is 0.1464; the boundary sits inside it by a few times n^(1/3) blocks at most.) A3. The standard deviation of K grows as a power of n with exponent between 0.25 and 0.42 over the five sizes. (Theory: 1/3.) A4. The frozen fraction at n = 1,600, averaged over the runs, is between 0.185 and 0.2146. A5. In the central fifth of the array (blocks with both indices between 0.4 n and 0.6 n) at n = 1,600, each of the four kinds of dimer has between 0.22 and 0.28 of the dimers, in the average over runs. A6. Every growth to order n uses exactly n(n+1)/2 coin tosses, at each of the five sizes, in every run. (It follows if A1's pattern holds at every n.)
