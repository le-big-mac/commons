Rhymes that came apart

In 1712 Pope could write

Here thou, great ANNA! whom three realms obey,
Dost sometimes counsel take — and sometimes Tea.

and it rhymed. Words move; rhymes written to ring true are left standing as they were. This page takes rhymes by thirty-two English poets, from Thomas More (born 1478) to Rupert Brooke (born 1887), each marked by hand as a rhyme by the makers of a research corpus, and asks two pronouncing dictionaries of today, one American and one British, whether the two words still end alike.

What "still rhymes" means here. Two words rhyme if, in one dictionary, some listed pronunciation of each has the same last vowel and the same sounds after it. That is the lenient end of what counts as rhyme; the stricter measure in the first figure asks for the same sounds from the stressed vowel on. The dictionaries are the Carnegie Mellon Pronouncing Dictionary (American) and BEEP (British); what they record is careful speech of the late twentieth century, which is neither everyone's speech now nor the poets'. A rhyme that fails here may have been true when it was written (obey : tea), or a licence even then (love : move has been one for four hundred years), or a rhyme for the eye; the page cannot tell which, and says only that it does not ring now. A rhyme that passes here may not have rung then.

Where it is thin. Only the last word of a line is looked at, so Byron's and Prior's rhymes on two words are judged on the second alone. All pairs in a group of rhyming lines are counted, so one odd word in a long stanza counts several times. Words with two pronunciations (wind) pass if either fits. Four poets (Spenser, Sidney, Drayton, Lovelace) are in old spelling, and only their rhymes whose words a modern dictionary knows as spelt are judged. The families were drawn up after the scores were seen. The verses are as the corpus has them, some from poor scans.

The rhymes and their marking are the Chicago Rhyming Poetry Corpus, begun by Morgan Sonderegger and extended by Sravana Reddy. That poets' rhymes record their speech is the subject of H. C. Wyld's Studies in English Rhymes from Surrey to Pope (1923), from which several of the families here are taken; that most rhymes of about 1900 are exact by a modern dictionary, and how many, is in Sonderegger's paper of 2011. Nothing on this page is new to those who study this; the counting, poet by poet and family by family, was done here.
