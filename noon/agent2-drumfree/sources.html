What I read, and when

Both things below were read on 22 November 2026, after my second study of the drumskin was scored and before this study's sheet was sealed at 23:58 UTC. They are why this study exists, and the sealed lines N1, N2, N4, N9 and N10 were written with them in front of me.

  1. A paper, read through as text extracted from its PDF: G. Sathej and R. Adhikari, "The eigenspectra of Indian musical drums", arXiv:0809.1320v1, 8 September 2008 (https://arxiv.org/abs/0809.1320). A fuller account of it is in ../agent2-drum12/sources.md. What this study takes from it, in its words where quoted: - Its Table I gives experimental ratios for nine modes of the dayan, the right drum of the tabla, with the second taken as 2: 1.03, 2.00, 3.00, 3.00, 4.00, 4.00, 5.04, 5.03, 5.08. The paper attaches the last three to (0,3), (4,1) and (2,2). Its text speaks of "Raman's experimental values" for concentric loading, and its introduction says Raman and coworkers "obtained the eigenmodes and eigenvalues of the mridangam". So the table is headed with one drum and credited, as far as I can tell, to experiments on another. I have not read Raman's papers. - "The sihai is approximately eight times as dense as the leather and covers approximately a quarter of the area of the membrane. The sihai is applied in layers". - "There is, indeed, a wide variation of σ and k in the dayan from different instrument makers, typically in the range 2.5 < σ < 4 and 0.45 < k < 0.55", σ squared being the ratio of the two densities and k the patch's radius as a share of the skin's. Squared, that is 6.25 to 16 times the skin. The sentence gives no source. It is about the tabla; the paper says the mridangam often uses "a temporary loading of flour paste". - The 1954 model of Ramakrishna and Sondhi, as the paper's Table I uses it: "σ = 3.125 and k = 0.4", which is 9.77 times the skin over 0.4 of the radius. I have not read the 1954 paper. - The paper's own model, a smooth step, at its reported best: σ = 2.57, k = 0.492, ξ = 0.091. - On what its model leaves out: "we have completely neglected the role of the enclosed air inside the drums. The principal effect of this is to raise the pitch of those modes which, during vibration, appreciably change the volume of the enclosed air. Modes with nodal circles are strongly affected, the greatest being the fundamental." And on damping: "the radiation damping of the fundamental, which is in the far field is an acoustic monopole, is quite different from that of the first overtone, which in the far field is an acoustic dipole. We have seen no numerical study of the radiation damping problem for the Indian drums".

The PDF and its text are kept in my home folder and are not copied here; checkfacts.py reads the text to check the quoted phrases, and that one check will not run elsewhere.

Yonder's measurement of real strokes, ../agent2-drum/listening-notes-agent3/real-drum/README.md (23:44 UTC, 22 November): 6,977 single strokes of South Indian drums from the Mridangam Stroke Dataset (Anantapadmanabhan, Bellur and Murthy, Zenodo record 4068196). The passage this study leans on, word for word: "Call the note of a tuning, in the octave from 110 to 220 Hz, T. dhin, cha and num ring whole multiples of T from the second up [...] At T itself there is next to nothing from 20 ms on. There is a partial at 1.09 to 1.12 T that follows the tuning: it is all of bheem, most of thom in E, and 13% of the first quarter second of dhin, where it dies within a few hundredths of a second." Yonder reads the partial as the head's lowest: "Noon's model has its lowest partial at 1 with 11% of the weight, lasting; the real head, on these recordings, has it a tenth sharp and brief." (My first version of this file said Yonder did not say which mode it is. The referee corrected that.) Two things in the same note bear on that reading. The partial fills bheem and most of thom in one tuning, and the note calls thom "the bass stroke", so it may belong to the other head or to the air inside. And T is nominal: "The tuning notes are the dataset's folder names put in the octave from 110 to 220 Hz." The mridangam is not the drum of the paper above. I have not downloaded the recordings or rerun the measurement.
