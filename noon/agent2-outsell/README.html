In brief. Two rival products, one a little better, and buyers who copy earlier buyers. How often is the better one ahead after a given number of sales? Worked out exactly. How much turns on the very first sales depends on a weight the model leaves open.

What follows is a working record, long and full of numbers, kept as it was written. The short account, and everything else, is on the front page; the words these records use are explained on another.

Two rival products, one of them better. Buyers come one at a time, and each is swayed by what earlier buyers bought. How often is the better product the one that is ahead? I wrote down twelve expectations with odds, all worked by hand, and then computed the answer exactly: every way the sales can go, added up, with no sampling. There is a page to play with, play.html.

In short

  • If nobody copies, a small edge is soon enough. A product that buyers prefer by 11 to 10 is ahead after a thousand sales with chance 0.934, and 723 sales are enough for nine times in ten.
  • If buyers copy in proportion to sales, the same edge is ahead with chance 0.651 after a thousand sales, 0.744 after a hundred thousand and 0.785 after a million. Nine times in ten takes 9.8 thousand million sales. That is when a product's first sale doubles its weight (a = 1, below), and the next line is what that choice is worth.
  • How much the first sales count decides it. If each product starts with the weight of ten sales (a = 10), the same product is ahead with chance 0.800 after a thousand sales and needs 8,621 for nine times in ten. With the weight of a tenth of a sale it is 0.551, and a number of sales with 69 digits.
  • There is an exact formula for copying in proportion. After 2m − 1 sales the better product is ahead with the chance that B′ < B^(1/q), where B and B′ are independent Beta(a, m) variables and q is the edge. It agrees with the step-by-step computation to ten decimal places in 30 cases. I do not know whether it is in print. (26 November: the first of its two steps, Rubin's clocks, is in print; the second is a one-line check. The reader did not see the formula in the texts it obtained, read none of them through, made no search for it as such, and judged it immediate.)
  • From a thousand sales up, copying lowered the better product's chance in every case I computed. Below that it can raise it a little: by up to 0.02 in the first few sales, and from a hundred sales to 680 only at even numbers, where it breaks a tie, and by 0.00002 at most.
  • If buyers copy more than in proportion, and a = 1, the matter is settled early and for good. With the 11 to 10 edge the better product ends ahead with chance 0.570 (g = 1.5) or 0.545 (g = 2) however long the market runs, and nine times in ten takes buyers who prefer it by about 2 to 1 or 3 to 1. With a = 10 it is neither: 0.716 after ten thousand sales at g = 1.5, and still rising. (26 November: "neither" was wrong. It settles there too, later and higher: 0.722 after a hundred thousand sales, 0.725 in the end. The limits are now computed directly, and for g = 2 there is a series: closed.py → closed.out.)
  • With a = 1 the early leader mostly stays the leader. Copying in proportion, the product ahead after 100 sales is the one ahead after 10,000 with chance 0.904, and where the worse product led after 100 the better was ahead after 10,000 with chance 0.240. With a = 10 those are 0.763 and 0.709. With a = 100 the first is 0.697, which is what it is with no copying at all.
  • Twelve sealed lines, twelve held. From the scorecard: marked worked, 10 lines, 10 held; marked worked roughly, 2 lines, 2 held. My odds averaged 0.765, which was too low, and the twelve lines are four facts.
  • A model, not a market. Two products, buyers who see every earlier sale, a preference that never changes, and nothing else: no price, no stock, no advertising, no third product. Nothing here is evidence about real markets.

What this summary said on the 22nd, and why it changed

  • It named no paper. The model with copying in proportion is that of Jiang, Sun, Figueiredo, Ribeiro and Towsley (2014), with their r for my q and their starting count for my a. They ask how long the contest lasts and not who leads after a given number of sales, and state, from a theorem of Janson (2004), that the better product takes the lead for good in the end. This page's question is the other one, and its numbers were not found in print. (Whether they allow a starting count below one, as I do, the reader did not check.)
  • "How much the first sales count decides it." How much they count is the weight a, which the model leaves open. After about a thousand sales the better product is ahead with chance 0.907 when a = 100, against 0.934 with no copying at all and 0.651 when a = 1.
  • "I do not know whether it is in print", of the formula: see the note in place.
  • "With a = 10 it is neither." It was slow, not different in kind.
  • The note of 23 November quoted Jiang and others (2016) as saying that with different fitnesses "the fittest agent is bound to win". The sentence begins "if β ≤ 1", that is, when copying is in proportion or less; and it goes on: "If β > 1, then there is a nonzero probability that the less fit wins".

The model

  • Two products, A the better. When A has sold nA and B has sold nB, the next buyer takes A with probability q (a + nA)^g / (q (a + nA)^g + (a + nB)^g).
  • q, the edge, is the odds with which a buyer who knows nothing of earlier sales prefers A. "11 to 10" is q = 1.1.
  • g, the strength of imitation. 0 is none: every buyer judges alone. 1 is in proportion to sales so far. Above 1 is more than in proportion.
  • a, the weight of a product that has sold nothing. With a = 1 the first sale doubles a product's weight. With a = 10 each product starts as if ten had already bought it. a = 1 unless said, and the figure is drawn for a = 1.
  • P after N sales is the chance that A has sold more than B, plus half the chance of a tie.
  • Exact computation (urn.py): the probability of every number of A's sales after n sales is carried forward one sale at a time. Nothing is sampled except where a check says so.

Why, and the formula

  • Two clocks. Let A's k-th sale wait an exponential time with rate q (a + k − 1)^g and B's k-th with rate (a + k − 1)^g, all independent. Whichever clock rings first is the next sale, with the model's probability, so the order of sales is the model's. A is ahead after 2m − 1 sales exactly when A's first m sales take less time than B's first m. I remember this construction as Rubin's; sources.md.
  • Copying in proportion. A sum of exponentials with rates a, a + 1, …, a + m − 1 is distributed as minus the logarithm of a Beta(a, m) variable. So A's time is −ln(B)/q and B's is −ln(B′), and A is ahead when B′ < B^(1/q). For a = 1 that is line R4: P = m ∫ (1 − u^q)^m (1 − u)^(m−1) du over 0 to 1. For an even number of sales the same argument gives the mean of two such chances, with m and m + 1 sales. (26 November: that sentence can be read two ways and only one is right. After 2m sales, P is the mean of the chance that A's (m + 1)-th sale comes before B's m-th and the chance that A's m-th comes before B's (m + 1)-th. It is not the mean of the odd formula at m and at m + 1, though the two agree to six places at a thousand sales: closed.out. The construction is Rubin's, printed in the appendix of Davis (1990). That the sum of waits is minus the logarithm of a Beta(a, m) variable follows, for whole-number a, from Rényi's representation of 1953, which I know only as a paper of 2017 states it; for any a it is a one-line check, since both have the Laplace transform Π (a + k)/(a + k + s) over k from 0 to m − 1.)
  • What it says. When the sales are many compared with a, P is close to the chance that G′ < G^(1/q) (a + N/2)^(1 − 1/q), with G and G′ independent Gamma(a) variables. After 999 sales that gives 0.5512, 0.6506, 0.7975 and 0.8863 for a = 0.1, 1, 10 and 100, where the exact values are 0.5512, 0.6507, 0.7997 and 0.9069 (after3-C.out). (26 November: "exact" there is the Beta formula's own value. A sum made one sale at a time agrees with it to six places: closed.out.) G and G′ are the luck of the early sales, and they never wash out. The better product's advantage grows only as the power 1 − 1/q of the sales: for 11 to 10, the eleventh root. (26 November: this is the known long-run law. The two products grow as independent Yule processes with Gamma-distributed limits, and the weaker one's count as the power 1/q of the stronger's: Janson, for urns of this kind; the same power is the "fitness" exponent of Bianconi and Barabási, 2001. My step from that law to the chance of being ahead after N sales is a heuristic that the numbers bear out, not a proof.)
  • What a does. Two things. A larger a makes G and G′ less scattered in proportion to their size. And since their size is a, it divides the number of sales that count by a: in terms of G/a the condition has (N/2a) where it had (N/2).
  • Less than in proportion. The share settles at x with x/(1 − x) = q^(1/(1 − g)): the edge is magnified, and so is the scatter. Below g = 1/2 the scatter falls as it would with no copying, only larger; above it, more slowly. After a thousand sales the better product's lead over a half, divided by the scatter, is 1.51, 1.44, 1.12 and 0.66 at g = 0, 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 (after3-CDEF.out): the scatter gains more than the lead does. That is computed, not derived, except that for g below 1/2 and many sales the ratio tends to √(1 − 2g)/(1 − g) times its value with no copying, which is less than 1. (26 November: the limiting share is in print for a market model that contains this one, in Maldonado and others; a change of behaviour at g = 1/2 is in print for equal products. The factor was not found.)
  • More than in proportion. Each product's clock would ring infinitely often in a finite time. The one whose time is shorter takes the larger part of the sales for good. With a = 1 the first wait carries 75%, 83% and 92% of the scatter (variance) of that time at g = 1.25, 1.5 and 2, so the first sale nearly settles it. With a = 10 the first wait carries 14%, 18% and 26% (after3-CDEF.out). (26 November: there is a closed form, the reader's: the share is a^(−2g)/ζ(2g, a), which gives 74.5, 83.2 and 92.4%, and 13.9, 18.1 and 25.9%. That one side takes all but finitely many sales when copying is more than in proportion is Rubin's theorem, in the same appendix.)

Checks made afterwards

None was registered.

  • The ruler, by sampling (after-1.out). 400,000 sampled markets at q = 1.1 and N = 1,000 gave 0.8707, 0.6511 and 0.5679 at g = 0.5, 1 and 1.5, each with a standard error of 0.0005 to 0.0008; the exact values are 0.8701, 0.6507 and 0.5676. The exact probabilities sum to 1 within 4 parts in ten thousand million million.
  • Ties and odd numbers. At g = 1 and q = 1.1, P is 0.65066, 0.65069 and 0.65071 after 999, 1,000 and 1,001 sales. The tables of "sales needed" first gave the least odd number; seven entries are one lower when even numbers are allowed, and the tables now have the least of all (after3-CDEF.out).
  • The formula at large numbers (after-3.out, after2.out). Two evaluations of it, one with 70 digits and one in ordinary arithmetic, agree to six decimal places at a million, a thousand million and a million million sales. For any a it agrees with the step-by-step computation within 7 parts in a million million, in 30 cases with m up to 5,000 and a from 0.3 to 30.
  • Does copying ever help? (after-2.out, after-0.out, after3-B.out) In a table of six edges from 1.02 to 2, nine strengths from 0 to 2 and a = 1, P fell at every step of g at 100, 1,000 and 10,000 sales, and rose from g = 0 to 0.25 at 10 sales. On a wider net (q = 1.02, 1.1, 2; a = 1, 10, 100; seven strengths from 0.01 to 1; every number of sales up to 2,000): copying raises P at some number of sales up to 680, in none of the 63 cases at 1,000 or more, and from 100 sales up only at even numbers and by 0.00002 at most. In the first 30 sales with a = 1 the largest rise is 0.011, 0.017 and 0.019 at g = 0.5, 1 and 1.5 for q = 2, and 0.022 at g = 1 for q = 5.
  • A strict reading of "ahead" (after3-B.out; the referee's). If a tie counts for nothing, copying raises the chance that the better product has sold more after 100 sales when the edge is small, because it breaks ties: at q = 1.02, from 0.4997 with none to 0.5160 at g = 1, the ties falling from 0.079 to 0.010. The worse product's chance rises too, from 0.421 to 0.474.
  • Less than in proportion, checked (after-v.out). At g = 0.25 the standard deviation of the better product's share, times the square root of the sales, is 0.694, 0.702 and 0.705 after a thousand, ten thousand and a hundred thousand sales; the account above gives 0.706 for large numbers. At g = 0.5 it keeps growing: 1.29, 1.50, 1.68. At g = 0.75 the scatter itself falls only as the fourth root of the sales: 0.112, 0.065, 0.037. The mean share creeps towards where it settles: at g = 0.75, 0.574, 0.584 and 0.588 on the way to 0.594.
  • Another form of copying (after-45678.out, after3-CDEF.out). Let a share s of buyers copy an earlier buyer picked at random, with one imaginary earlier buyer of each product among those they pick from, and the rest judge alone at 11 to 10. After 1,000 sales P is 0.934, 0.911, 0.860, 0.720, 0.572 and 0.520 for s = 0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 0.9, and it never rises with s at 100, 1,000 or 10,000 sales. Without the imaginary buyers it is 0.704 and 0.557 at s = 0.5 and 0.75. This is a weak second witness: these copiers carry no preference of their own, so copying here can only add scatter.
  • The limits for g above 1, by another route (after-9.out; a = 1). Sampling the two clocks' total times 400,000 times gives 0.6189, 0.5702 and 0.5451 for g = 1.25, 1.5 and 2 (standard error 0.0008). The step-by-step values at a hundred thousand sales are 0.6107, 0.5701 and 0.5451. (26 November: computed directly the limits are 0.61788, 0.57036 and 0.54505; the sampled 0.6189 is 1.3 standard errors above its limit.)
  • The early lead, by sampling (after-r.out). In 200,000 sampled markets at g = 1 and a = 1 the leader after 100 sales was the leader after 10,000 in 0.9045 of them (exact: 0.9040), and where the worse product led after 100 the better was ahead after 10,000 in 0.2386 (exact: 0.2401; standard error 0.0015).
  • What I looked up (sources.md; I read no paper). A search tool reported a 2016 paper on this model, "nonlinear Pólya urn processes with fitness", about the time of the last tie and the number of ties, and said of its own results that none "gives a closed-form expression for P(fitter colour is ahead after n draws)". It reported the construction as "Rubin's exponential embedding".

What the referee changed

Its own computation gave every number it recomputed, and all twelve verdicts are right by the letter. Its blind answer for the sales needed at 11 to 10 was 9,820,848,444. Twelve readings did not stand.

  • Three lines of "In short" were true only of a = 1 and did not say so: "the better product ends ahead with chance 0.570 or 0.545, however long the market runs", "the early leader mostly stays the leader", and the 0.904 and 0.240 under it. With a = 10 they are 0.716 and rising, 0.763 and 0.709. They are marked now, with the other values beside them, and the figure's caption names a.
  • "Copying of this kind never raised the better product's chance from 100 sales up, at any edge or strength I computed." True of my table, and false just off it: weaker copying, or a heavier start, raises P at even numbers of sales up to several hundred, and a strict reading of "ahead" breaks it by more. It now says a thousand, with what happens below.
  • "A larger a makes G and G′ less scattered, which is all that a does." Wrong by my own formula: a also divides the number of sales that count.
  • The tables of sales needed gave the least odd number. Seven entries were one too high.
  • "In the first 28 sales a little copying raises it, by 0.006 at most" was the figure for two strengths at a = 1. Stronger copying raises it by up to 0.02 in the first few sales.
  • "More than 2 × 10^15" was where ordinary arithmetic stopped, and understated the answer by 9 and by 53 powers of ten.
  • The other form of copying had two imaginary buyers that the README did not mention, and can only add scatter. Both are said now.
  • "For many sales" in the approximation should be "many compared with a", and the approximation had not been checked in numbers. It has now.
  • "Depends mostly on the first few waits" and "takes all but a few of the sales" are a = 1 statements, and the second is loose even there. Numbers are given instead.
  • The scorecard's count of facts left R8, R10 and R11 unplaced, and did not say how little R11's holding means or that the odds were too low.
  • "Everything after the first table was computed after the sheet was scored" overlooked that most of the first table was too.
  • "2 to 1 (g = 1.5)" is "about": at exactly 2 to 1, P is 0.898.

Did the list help this time? (checklist.md) It was the fourth use. The referee found three of its answers untrue as written: that only one summary figure hung on a = 1 (four did), that the account under "Why" was written before the computation (only its a = 1 part was), and that nothing was on a grid (the "never" claim sat on nine strengths and one a, and the search for sales needed on odd numbers). The seven pieces before this lost 15, 11, 9, 8, 10, 10 and 14 readings. This one lost twelve.

What this does not show

  • Anything about real markets. No data were used. The experiment I had in mind had many songs and real listeners; this has two products and a formula.
  • More than two products, buyers who see only some earlier sales, tastes that differ between buyers, or an edge that changes as a product sells.
  • Which a is right. The answers run from "soon" to "never in practice" as a goes from 100 to 0.1, and the model does not say what a is.
  • That copying never helps the better product. It lowered P in every case computed from a thousand sales up, with half a tie counted; that is a table, not a theorem.
  • That copying is bad for buyers. Only who is ahead is counted, not what anyone gains.
  • That the formula is new.

What was already in print

Added 26 November. A fresh reader with the web had 25 minutes and 35 requests; its report is prior-art-26nov.md. I have checked each quotation here against the text it fetched. The reader read no paper through, and several it wanted it could not obtain (below).

  • This model, another question. Jiang, Sun, Figueiredo, Ribeiro and Towsley, "Duration and Intensity of Cumulative Advantage Competitions" (arXiv, 2014; that is the title on the copy the reader has): "the less fit agent has a non-negligible probability of taking an early lead which can last for a very long time due to the CA effect, but it will ultimately surrender the lead to the fitter agent and never lead again, a phenomenon that we call 'delusion of the weakest'". Its Theorem 2 bounds the chance that the last tie comes after time t by powers of t whose exponents "depend critically on x0", my a. The same authors in 2016, for copying of any strength β: "if β ≤ 1 then the fittest agent is bound to win the competition (i.e., take the lead forever). If β > 1, then there is a nonzero probability that the less fit wins".
  • The two clocks. Pemantle's survey of 2007, of its Theorem 3.6 ("Rubin's Theorem"): "The following result appears in the appendix of [Dav90] and is proved by Rubin's exponential embedding." Jiang and others (2016) use an event of the same kind inside a proof, for equal products: "X(t) = x if and only if color 1 gets its x1-th ball before color 2 gets its (x2 + 1)-st ball and at the same time" the converse.
  • Copying in proportion, in the long run. Janson (2024), Example 14.2: "the colours evolve as independent Yule processes, now with possibly different rates", each count over its exponential growth tending to a Gamma variable, and the slower colour's count growing as a power less than one of the total. That is my Gamma formula's content; my formula for the chance of being ahead after N sales is one line further and was not found.
  • More than in proportion. Rubin's theorem, as above. Oliveira, on the onset of dominance, for equal bins with many balls already in them: "a bin that obtains more than half of the balls at a 'large time' will most likely preserve its position of leadership". Maldonado and others, for a market of many products shown in places of unequal prominence, of which mine is the case of two products shown alike: "Which product wins the entire market share depends on the initial condition and the early dynamics." A theorem of Mitzenmacher, Oliveira and Spencer (2004) covers equal products with unequal starts. My limits 0.570 and 0.545 were not found.
  • Who stays ahead. Krapivsky and Redner (2002), of the first of many equal nodes in a growing network: "The probability that the first node retains the lead approaches a finite constant". My 0.904, 0.763 and 0.697 were not found.
  • Less than in proportion. The limiting share q^(1/(1−g)) to 1 is in Maldonado and others. Pemantle, reporting Khanin and Khanin, of equal colours: "Gaussian when α < 1/2, Gaussian with a logarithm in the scaling when α = 1/2".
  • Not found at all: that copying lowers the better product's chance at a fixed number of sales, from a thousand up. No source the reader found compares the two at fixed N.
  • Not obtained, not read. Davis (1990) and Rényi (1953) themselves, known here through Pemantle and through a paper of 2017 that states Rényi's result; Janson (2004 and 2006), Athreya and Karlin (1968), Khanin and Khanin, Drinea, Frieze and Mitzenmacher, Arthur (1989), Arthur, Ermoliev and Kaniovski. Kattuman and Lewis (2007), which the first search named, has no better product and does not bear on the question. The reader obtained nineteen papers and read none through. It did not search for the page's baseline with no copying, its spread of shares at a thousand sales, or its variant with random copiers. It offered a formula for the far tail (for a = 1, the chance of being behind is about Γ(q + 1)(N/2)^−(q−1)), which fits my "99 times in 100" column to within 3 to 24% and fails for "nine in ten"; I have not used it.

  • The share is a^(−2g)/ζ(2g, a), with Hurwitz's ζ: the waits are independent, so their variances add.
  • For g = 2 and a = 1 the chance is 2 Σ (−1)^(k−1) x_k / sinh x_k, with x_k = πk/√q. The series is the reader's; I derived it again by hand from the Laplace transform of the sum of the waits. Whether it is in print neither of us looked.
  • The other limits are by numerical inversion of a characteristic function, and agree to five places when the terms and the points are halved. Three controls, the reader's suggestions: at another edge, q = 2, the series and the inversion both give 0.795774; with a = 10 and g = 1.5, sampling the two whole times 400,000 times gives 0.7246 (standard error 0.0007) where the inversion has 0.7248; and after 999 sales the Beta formula agrees to six places with a sum made one sale at a time, at a = 0.1, 0.3, 1, 10 and 100, so it holds where a is below one. (The page's own "exact values" for those cases, 0.5512, 0.6507, 0.7997 and 0.9069, had come from the formula itself, as the reader found; they were no test of it.)

The field's words for these things

g is the feedback strength: sublinear, linear, superlinear. q is the fitness ratio and a the initial condition. "Ahead for good" is the end of the competition, whose length is its duration, the time of the last tie; "settled" is monopoly, or lock-in. The early leader who stays is a persistent leader, or first-mover advantage. The two clocks are Rubin's exponential embedding. Copying in proportion with an edge is a diagonal, or triangular, urn.
