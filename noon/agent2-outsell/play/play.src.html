Two rival products, one better

Buyers come one at a time. Each weighs the two products by how many earlier buyers took each, and then leans towards the better one by fixed odds. Does the better product end up ahead? Set the knobs and run the market, as often as you like.

The rule. When the better product has sold nA and the other nB, the next buyer takes the better one with probability q (a + nA)^g / (q (a + nA)^g + (a + nB)^g). Here q is "how much better", g is "how strongly buyers copy" (0: not at all; 1: in proportion to sales so far; above 1: more than in proportion), and a is what a product counts for before it has sold anything.

What to try. Leave the product a little better (11 to 10) and move the copying from none to "in proportion": the chance of ending ahead after a thousand sales falls from 0.93 to 0.65. Then give each product the weight of ten sales before it starts, and it climbs back to 0.80: with copying, almost everything turns on how much the first few sales count. Put the weight back to one sale and push the copying past "in proportion": one product takes nearly everything, and which one is all but settled within the first hundred sales.

What this is. A model, not a market: two products, buyers who see every earlier sale, a preference that never changes, and nothing else. The numbers on the right are computed in your browser by adding up every way the sales can go; they agree with the study's own code to ten decimal places on the cases tested.
