0. In short

  • Nearly every labour-market finding in the README is in print, and it cites no paper. The curve's two properties are the field's two "stylized facts"; the shift after October 2009, the peak of about 2 openings per unemployed in March 2022 and the "soft landing" are standard.
  • The quits-and-pay result is Faberman and Justiniano (Chicago Fed Letter 337, 2015) almost to the digit: 0.66 in the same quarter and 0.78 four quarters ahead, against the README's +0.63 and +0.81.
  • "Moved outward and came most of the way back" is one of two published readings. The other (Figura and Waller; Bok et al.; Foote et al. 2025) says the curve was back near its old place by early 2022 and openings then fell down a steep stretch. The README's measure cannot tell these apart.
  • The −0.97 within 2010 to 2019 is two smooth trends over one expansion: on the README's own series, 12-month changes correlate at only −0.31 (−0.85 in 2001 to 2009).
  • Not reached: the BLS's own pages (refused), so its Beveridge chart and its March 2022 figure are unverified. I used 34 of 35 requests and about 10 of 25 minutes.

1. Claims, written before any search

Saved with its timestamp in /home/agent2/prior2/vacancy/step1-claims.txt. The README presents none of these as new or as known; it names the "Beveridge curve" (line 8) and says nothing else about who knows what, except C11.

  • C1. Monthly JOLTS openings rate against the unemployment rate: r = −0.90 in 2001–2009, −0.97 in 2010–2019, −0.77 pooled to December 2019 ("because the curve sat higher after 2009"), −0.59 over all 309 months to September 2026, −0.08 from 2021 on.
  • C2. At unemployment of 3.4–4.5%, the openings rate averaged 6.33% in 2021–2023 (27 months) against 4.39% in 2017–2019 (34 months), +1.93 points. Over other bands and years the excess is +1.6 to +2.8; for 2024–2026 it is about zero.
  • C3. Openings per unemployed person (levels) peaked at 2.04 in March 2022; above 1 in 79 months since January 2018; 1.06 in September 2026.
  • C4. From March 2022 the openings rate fell 7.5% to 5.0% in 24 months while unemployment went 3.7% to 3.9%. At 36, 42 and 48 months: −3.3/+0.5, −3.2/+0.7, −3.3/+0.6. Unemployment reached 4.4% by late 2025. After the openings peaks "of 2001 and 2007", unemployment rose 1.6 and 4.3 points in 24 months.
  • C5. Highest layoffs-and-discharges rate: 8.6%, March 2020.
  • C6. Quits rate above layoffs rate in 282 of 310 months (91%).
  • C7. Hires rate above separations rate in 213 of 310 months (68.7%), equal in 52; 239 (77.1%) on levels.
  • C8. Quits rate against 12-month growth of average hourly earnings: +0.63 same month, +0.81 a year later, +0.53 a year earlier. To February 2020: +0.57, +0.87, +0.12. From July 2021: +0.88, +0.84, +0.72 (no lead). The openings rate does as well (+0.70, +0.78, +0.63). The correlation rises to 12 months ahead and falls after.
  • C9. In 2009 the openings rate was lowest in April and July, and unemployment highest in October.
  • C10. Hires per opening, yearly: above 1 from 2001 to 2014, below 1 from 2015; 0.997 in 2020; 0.61 in 2022.
  • C11. Average hourly earnings growth was 8.1% in April 2020 and 0.6% in April 2021; that this was the low-paid losing and regaining jobs is "from outside these data" (presented as known, uncited).

2. What is in print, claim by claim

All files are under /home/agent2/prior2/vacancy/dl/. Where the PDF text lost "fi/fl/ffi" ligatures I restore them in square brackets.

C1: FOUND in substance; the four correlations NOT FOUND as numbers

  • Blanchard and Diamond 1989, "The Beveridge Curve", Brookings Papers 1989:1 (26/, a scan, so words run together): "The relationhas two clear features. The first is the largethinloops arounda downward-slopinglocus. The otheris the well-documentedshift to the right over the postwar period."
  • Shimer 2003, NBER w9536, p. 7 (25/): "The correlation of the percentage deviation of unemployment and vacancies from trend is −0.90 between 1951 and 2001." This is the field's benchmark number, and it is for detrended quarterly series.
  • Daly, Hobijn, Şahin and Valletta 2011, FRBSF WP 2011-05, sec. 2.2 (27/): "In June 2011 the actual unemployment rate was 9.2 percent while the level of the unemployment rate on the fitted BC at the observed 2.3 percent vacancy rate is 6.6 percent. This implies the 2.6 percentage point unemployment gap, or implied outward BC shift".
  • Diamond and Şahin 2014, NY Fed Staff Report 687, p. 1 (13/): "this relationship was remarkably stable from the start of JOLTS data in December 2000 through at least the NBER identified trough in June 2009 or possibly through the point of the highest unemployment rate in October, 2009. However, after October 2009, there was a notable shift in the Beveridge curve".
  • Elsby, Michaels and Ratner, July 2014 draft of the JEL 2015 survey, p. 1 (15/): "Two key stylized facts have occupied research on the Beveridge curve. First, at cyclical frequencies unemployment and vacancies move in opposite directions, tracing out a negatively inclined Beveridge locus. Second, the position of this locus has shifted periodically in many developed economies, most notably during the persistent rise in European unemployment in the 1980s, and more recently in the wake of the Great Recession in the United States."

The README's bold line 13 is that last sentence in other words. Its two periods are cut at the published break date (October 2009), so tightness within each follows from where the cut was made. I am sure of the substance. I did not see the BLS chart that is said to draw the curve by period.

C2: FOUND IN PART; the reading is contested, and three sources cut against it

Against "the curve moved out and came back" as a description of 2022–2024:

  • Bok, Petrosky-Nadeau, Valletta and Yilma, FRBSF Economic Letter 2022-24, 29 August 2022 (14/): "A further step shows little evidence of ongoing problems with the job-matching process, suggesting that the Beveridge curve has not shifted substantially relative to where it was before the pandemic." A curve shifted to fit the dots "implies a significant reduction in matching efficiency of about 25%", but direct estimates give "down about 10%".
  • Figura and Waller, FEDS 2024-073, p. 16 (20/): the decline in job finding "implies little change in matching efficiency since early 2022."
  • Foote, Fujita, Michaud and Montes, FEDS 2025-067, sec. 7.3–7.4 (18/): "By the first half of 2021, the data suggest that the Beveridge curve had shifted out relative to its pre-pandemic position." In the second half of 2021 the movements "shifted the Beveridge curve back toward its pre-pandemic position." After March 2022: "this pattern of rebalancing is consistent with a downward movement along a steep Beveridge curve." Footnote 36: "Another interpretation is that the Beveridge curve was not particularly steep in early 2022, but that it continued to shift back even after March 2022. However, separate estimates of matching efficiency suggest that the Beveridge curve remained relatively stable after early 2022."

For it:

  • Blanchard, Domash and Summers, PIIE Policy Brief 22-7, July 2022 (02/): "The outward shift of the Beveridge curve suggests that other factors have been at work, namely lower matching efficiency and higher reallocation"; "an increase in the natural rate of about 1.3 percentage points from its pre-COVID level".
  • Bernanke and Blanchard, NBER w31417, 2023 (11/): "during the pandemic the Beveridge curve shifted upward, implying that, for reasons not fully understood, the job-worker matching process had become less efficient."
  • Barlevy, Faberman, Hobijn and Şahin, Chicago Fed WP 2023-38 (08/): "Since the summer of 2022, the empirical Beveridge curve has exhibited what looks like a puzzling, vertical drop." And: "If labor market developments since early 2022 are interpreted as a partial reversal of the shifts in the Beveridge and Job Creation curves since the start of 2021, then the drop no longer appears perplexing."

Nobody I read publishes the README's number (+1.93 points of openings at given unemployment). The field measures a shift sideways (unemployment at a given vacancy rate: 2.6 points in 2011, 1.3 in 2022) or as matching efficiency.

Which to believe: the README's arithmetic is sound and I reproduce its shape. What I would not believe is the verb. A gap in openings at the same low unemployment rate is exactly what a near-vertical stretch of one curve produces, so the measure cannot separate "shifted" from "steep", and the README does not say a second reading exists. I am sure the dispute exists and is unresolved in print.

C3: FOUND for "about 2 in March 2022"; the 79-month count and September 2026 figure NOT FOUND

  • Barnichon and Shapiro, FRBSF Economic Letter 2022-04, 22 February 2022 (21/): "although the unemployment rate has not reached its past lows, the V/U ratio has climbed to record highs."
  • Benigno and Eggertsson, NBER w31197, 2023 (06/): "We define θ > 1 as labor shortage, meaning that there are more firms vacancies to be filled than workers looking for jobs."
  • Bernanke and Blanchard 2023 (11/): the ratio rose "from less than 1.0 in April 2021 … to a historically high level of about 1.9 (nearly two job openings per unemployed worker) a year later."
  • Michaillat and Saez, arXiv 2206.13012v3, June 2024, sec. 3.3 (07/): "The labor market then steadily tightened to reach 1.97 in 2022Q2." (Quarterly average.)
  • Powell, Jackson Hole, 23 August 2024 (19/): "There were nearly twice as many job openings as unemployed persons from March 2022 through the end of the year".
  • Foote et al. 2025, sec. 7.4 (18/): "the V/U ratio indicated that there were about 2 job openings for every unemployed worker, the highest number since JOLTS data became available in December 2000 and nearly double the 1.2 openings per unemployed worker recorded in February 2020."

The README's series gives 1.21 for February 2020, which agrees. The second decimal of 2.04 depends on the data vintage. The BLS's own figure (0.5 unemployed persons per opening, March 2022) I have only from a search summary. I am sure of the peak and its month; I did not check the 79 months or 1.06.

C4: FOUND as the outcome of a published 2022 dispute; the horizon numbers NOT FOUND, but their table is BDS Table 1

  • Blanchard, Domash and Summers 2022 (02/): "there has never been a historical example where the job vacancy rate came down in a substantial way without a significant increase in unemployment." Their Table 1 gives changes in the vacancy and unemployment rates 6, 12 and 24 months after each vacancy peak. Its rows for February 2000 and March 2007 show unemployment up 1.6 and 4.3 at 24 months, the README's two numbers (line 79).
  • Figura and Waller, FEDS Note, 29 July 2022 (01/): "a decline in the vacancy rate from 7 percent to 4.6 percent would lead to an increase in the unemployment rate of about 1 percentage point or less."
  • Figura and Waller 2024 (20/): "The vacancy rate fell by a little over 20 percent from its peak in early 2022 through August of 2023. Such a decline has never been experienced before outside of recessions." And: "the behavior of the labor market since 2022 has performed almost exactly as we would have predicted in a soft-landing scenario."
  • Powell 2024 (19/): "the historically high level of vacancies relative to unemployment has normalized primarily through a decline in vacancies, without sizable and disruptive layoffs".

The README's later horizons (openings −3.3, unemployment +0.5 to +0.7) fall inside Figura and Waller's 2022 forecast. One error of description: JOLTS begins in December 2000, so the README's "peak of 2001" is the start of the series. BDS date the peak to February 2000 on a longer series. Vintage differences: Barlevy et al. (2023) print 7.4% and 3.6% for March 2022; Foote et al. (2025) print 3.7%, as the README does. I am sure.

4. Sentences that say more than the sources or the numbers allow

  • Front page, "a tight curve within each of the two decades before the pandemic, which then moved outward and came most of the way back." Fair: "Job openings against unemployment since 2000: the well-known Beveridge curve, which shifted after 2009; from 2021 to 2023 openings ran about two points above the old curve and by 2024 were back on it. Economists disagree whether the curve moved or the economy climbed and descended a steep stretch of it."
  • Line 13, "The curve holds within a stretch and shifts between stretches", is given in bold as a finding. Fair: "As the literature has long described (two stylized facts), …; the numbers for 2001–2026 are …". The −0.97 needs "over one ten-year expansion; in 12-month changes, −0.31".
  • Line 16, "It moved outward after the pandemic and came most of the way back." The first half is one reading. The second half rests on line 81, a referee's run that is not in vacancy.py, and line 81 says "about zero", which is all the way. Fair: "At the same unemployment rate, openings were about two points higher in 2021–2023 than in 2017–2019, and no higher in 2024–2026."
  • Lines 18–20 still lead with the 24-month horizon, where the rise in unemployment is smallest. "By late 2025 unemployment had reached 4.4%" understates the README's own series, which shows 4.5 in November 2025. Fair: "By the time openings were back near 4.3% (2025), unemployment was 0.5 to 0.8 points higher."
  • Line 21, "Tightness goes better with pay growth over the next year than over the last." Here the README claims less than the literature and hedges well. But it should say this was reported in 2015, and that average hourly earnings is the measure the field trusts least for this (composition; the Employment Cost Index peaks at two quarters).
  • Line 55 says 309 of 310 months without saying which is missing. The series shows no unemployment rate for October 2025. I believe that is the federal shutdown, but that is from memory.

5. Terms

The README says The field's name
"openings per unemployed person" labour-market tightness, the V/U or vacancy-to-unemployment ratio (θ). The BLS publishes the inverse, "unemployed persons per job opening"
"hires per opening" vacancy yield (related: job-filling rate)
"the curve sat higher", "moved outward" outward shift; the cause is discussed as matching efficiency, reallocation and separations
"openings bottomed before unemployment peaked" counterclockwise loops
"came down without unemployment rising" "soft landing" is right; the reader also needs "steep portion of the Beveridge curve"
"pay growth" average hourly earnings growth; the lead-lag is discussed as the wage Phillips curve and the job ladder
the pay swing of 2020–2021 composition effect
"job openings rate" usually "vacancy rate" in papers. JOLTS divides by employment plus openings; most papers divide by the labour force (Domash and Summers: "the level of job vacancies divided by the size of the civilian labor force"). The README should say which it uses

No term is used wrongly.
