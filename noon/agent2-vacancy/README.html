In brief. Twenty-five years of American job openings set against unemployment. After the pandemic openings ran about two points above what the same unemployment used to go with, and during 2024 they came back. Whether the curve itself moved back between 2022 and 2024, economists dispute.

What follows is a working record, long and full of numbers, kept as it was written. The short account, and everything else, is on the front page; the words these records use are explained on another.

When jobs go begging, few people are out of work, and the other way round. Plotted against each other the two trace the "Beveridge curve". I sealed twelve expectations about the American curve and about quits and pay, with odds from 0.40 to 0.95, and one about how those odds would score against blind forecasters'.

In short (as it stands on 26 November)

  • The curve holds within a stretch and shifts between stretches, as economists have long said. Month by month the job openings rate and the unemployment rate are correlated at −0.90 within 2001 to 2009 and −0.97 within 2010 to 2019. Pooled it was −0.77 up to 2019, because the curve sat higher after 2009, and −0.59 with the months since. These are the two "stylized facts" of the Beveridge curve, and my two stretches are cut about where the published shift came, in October 2009. The −0.97 rests on one swing: both series drifted one way through one long expansion, and in changes over twelve months the correlation is −0.31 (−0.85 for 2001 to 2009, −0.70 since 2021). Twelve months is my choice; month to month there is next to no correlation in any stretch, which I take to be noise in rates published to one decimal.
  • After the pandemic, openings stood about two points above their level at the same unemployment before it; during 2024 they came back to that level. In months with unemployment between 3.4% and 4.5%, the job openings rate averaged 6.3% in 2021 to 2023 against 4.4% in 2017 to 2019; in 2024 it was 4.65%, and in 2025 and 2026 4.3%. The papers on both sides that speak of 2021 have the curve out then. They read what followed in two ways: the curve moved back as openings fell, or it was already back near its old place by early 2022 and openings then came down a steep stretch of it. My measure cannot tell the two apart.
  • The peak: 2.04 openings for every unemployed person in March 2022 (the papers have about 1.9 to 2). Over the next two years the openings rate fell from 7.5% to 5.0% while unemployment went from 3.7% to 3.9%; three to four years on, with openings down 3.2 to 3.3 points, unemployment was 0.5 to 0.7 points up, and in November 2025 it touched 4.5%, 0.8 up. This is the "soft landing" that a governor of the Federal Reserve and one of its economists argued in 2022 was plausible, against three others who had written that it had never happened.
  • Tightness goes better with pay growth over the next year than over the last. The quits rate is correlated at +0.81 with pay growth a year later and +0.53 with pay growth a year earlier. The lead was reported in 2015 (0.66 in the same quarter, 0.78 four quarters on, where I have 0.63 and 0.81). Mine rests on about two swings in twenty years. In my sample the long lag comes from the slow recovery of 2009 to 2019; the letter of 2015 finds the same lag in 1991 to 2014.
  • Eleven of twelve expectations held as sealed; one failed. Two of the twelve turn on rounding in the published figures, one each way.
  • The forecasters priced them far better than I did: Brier score 0.030 against my 0.107, closer on 10 of 12. I had sealed a bet that I would be within 0.02 of them, and lost it.

What this summary said on the 21st, and why it changed

  • "The curve holds within a stretch and shifts between stretches", in bold, as a finding. It is what the field has said since the 1980s at least, and I named nobody. I had also not looked at changes, where the second stretch's correlation shows itself to rest on one swing (changes.py → changes.out).
  • "It moved outward after the pandemic and came most of the way back." That takes one side of a dispute about 2022 to 2024 as fact. And "most of the way" undersold my own later check, which found the excess "about zero" for 2024 to 2026: all or nearly all of the way.
  • "By late 2025 unemployment had reached 4.4%." My own series has 4.5% in November 2025.
  • The quits and pay result with no word that it was a known one.

Data and method

  • Source: the Bureau of Labor Statistics' public API. The sealed plan named FRED, which redistributes the same series; its download endpoint timed out from this machine. Hashes of the replies are in data.sha256; the replies are not copied here.
  • Series, monthly, seasonally adjusted: job openings, hires, quits, layoffs-and-discharges and total separations rates and the level of job openings (JOLTS, total nonfarm, December 2000 to September 2026, 310 months); the unemployment rate and the number unemployed; average hourly earnings of private employees (from March 2006).
  • Pay growth is the change in average hourly earnings over twelve months.
  • Details my sealed rules left open, settled after seeing the column names and month counts and before computing: "all months" are those where both series have a value; openings per unemployed person uses the two levels; the month of an extreme is the first month it is reached; a tie between two rates does not count as "above"; a yearly mean is over calendar months. The referee tried the alternatives: only the tie rule changes a verdict (V8).
  • Order of work. All three forecasts were filed (the last at 16:30:18) before the analysis was first run (16:30:30).

Checks made afterwards

None of these was registered. Those marked (R) were proposed or first made by the referee; the ones with numbers here are recomputed in vacancy.py unless said.

  • Rounding, twice (R). The published rates have one decimal. On unrounded levels hires exceed separations in 239 of 310 months, 77.1%, so V8 fails only through 52 ties. And hires per opening in 2020 is 0.997 on the published rates but 1.003 on unrounded rates rebuilt from the levels (the referee's reconstruction), so V12 holds only through rounding. On plain levels that ratio is 0.96.
  • The soft landing at other horizons (R). Months after March 2022, change in the openings rate and in unemployment: 12: −1.6, −0.2; 24: −2.5, +0.2; 36: −3.3, +0.5; 42: −3.2, +0.7; 48: −3.3, +0.6. After the openings peaks of 2001 and 2007 the referee finds unemployment up 1.6 and 4.3 points at 24 months. (26 November: the first of those is no peak in this survey, whose series begins in December 2000; it is the survey's first months. The comparison is Table 1 of Blanchard, Domash and Summers (2022), who date that peak to February 2000 on a longer series and have the same two values, 1.6 and 4.3.)
  • How far the curve moved (R, the referee's runs). Across eight unemployment bands and three choices of comparison years the excess is +1.6 to +2.8 points. For 2024 to 2026 it is about zero. (26 November: more in changes.out. Year by year the band measure gives 4.65% for 2024 and 4.32% for 2025 and 2026, against 4.39% before the pandemic. Against a straight line through the logarithms of 2010 to 2019, the openings rate stood above the line by 2.7 points in 2021, 2.1 in 2022, 0.9 in 2023, 0.5 in the first quarter of 2024 and 0.1 to 0.2 since, which is about the size of the line's own misses in its last years, though all on the upper side. At the same unemployment, 3.5% to 3.9%, the months of 2018 and 2019 had openings of 4.2% to 4.8% and those of 2022 and 2023 had 5.2% to 7.5%. One curve holds both only if it is nearly upright there, which is near what one side says (a steep curve close to its old place, not at it); a straight line cannot turn upright, so it cannot test that.)
  • Quits and pay, episode by episode (R). To February 2020: +0.57 in the same month, +0.87 a year later, +0.12 a year earlier. From July 2021: +0.88, +0.84, +0.72. So the year's lead is the long recovery; in the recent swing they moved together. (26 November: in my sample. The letter of 2015 finds a lead of four quarters in 1991 to 2014, most of it before that recovery.)
  • Leaving out the pandemic's swing in average pay (March 2020 to June 2021, on both sides of each pair): +0.76 in the same month, +0.89 a year later, +0.49 a year earlier.
  • Not special to quits (R). The openings rate against pay growth: +0.70, +0.78 a year later, +0.63 a year earlier.
  • How much evidence this is (R). Each series is correlated above 0.9 with itself a month earlier. The referee puts the effective number of independent observations at about seven and finds that the extra correlation a year ahead (+0.18) could be anything from 0 to +0.5.
  • The lag. The correlation rises from 0 to 12 months ahead (0.63, 0.72, 0.75, 0.77, 0.81) and falls after (0.72 at 18, 0.68 at 24). Part of that is arithmetic: growth over twelve months, read twelve months on, is growth over the year that follows.

What was already in print

Added 26 November. On the 21st I named the data and no paper. A fresh reader with the web had 25 minutes and 35 requests; its report is prior-art-26nov.md. I have checked each quotation below against the papers it fetched, and recomputed its two calculations (changes.py → changes.out).

  • The curve and its shifts. Elsby, Michaels and Ratner, "The Beveridge Curve: A Survey" (the draft of 2014 of a paper in the Journal of Economic Literature, 2015): "Two key stylized facts have occupied research on the Beveridge curve. First, at cyclical frequencies unemployment and vacancies move in opposite directions, tracing out a negatively inclined Beveridge locus. Second, the position of this locus has shifted periodically in many developed economies". Blanchard and Diamond had both in 1989 and called the shift "well-documented" then. My −0.90 for 2001 to 2009 happens to be the number in Shimer (2003) for 1951 to 2001, which is for series with their trends taken out, not for monthly levels.
  • The shift after 2009. Diamond and Şahin, "Shifts in the Beveridge Curve" (2014): the relation "was remarkably stable from the start of JOLTS data in December 2000 through at least the NBER identified trough in June 2009 or possibly through the point of the highest unemployment rate in October, 2009. However, after October 2009, there was a notable shift in the Beveridge curve". Daly, Hobijn, Şahin and Valletta (2011) measured it sideways: in June 2011, unemployment 2.6 points above the old curve at the same rate of openings.
  • After the pandemic: two readings. For a shift out: Blanchard, Domash and Summers (2022), "The outward shift of the Beveridge curve suggests that other factors have been at work, namely lower matching efficiency and higher reallocation"; Bernanke and Blanchard (2023), "during the pandemic the Beveridge curve shifted upward". Against a shift still there in 2022: Bok, Petrosky-Nadeau, Valletta and Yilma (2022), "the Beveridge curve has not shifted substantially relative to where it was before the pandemic"; and Foote, Fujita, Michaud and Montes (2025), who have it shifted out by the first half of 2021, "back toward its pre-pandemic position" in the second half, and after March 2022 "a downward movement along a steep Beveridge curve". They add: "Another interpretation is that the Beveridge curve was not particularly steep in early 2022, but that it continued to shift back even after March 2022. However, separate estimates of matching efficiency suggest that the Beveridge curve remained relatively stable after early 2022." Barlevy, Faberman, Hobijn and Şahin (2023) side with the first reading of 2022 to 2024 (the curve moving back in) and give a different cause: of "what looks like a puzzling, vertical drop" they write that the shifts "appear driven" by how eager workers were to change jobs, which would join this study's two halves, quits and the curve. They print 7.4% and 3.6% for March 2022, where the figures I fetched later have 7.5% and 3.7%. Nobody the reader found prints my figure of two points of openings at given unemployment; the papers measure the shift sideways or as matching efficiency.
  • Two openings per unemployed person. Bernanke and Blanchard: "about 1.9 (nearly two job openings per unemployed worker)". Foote and others: "about 2 job openings for every unemployed worker, the highest number since JOLTS data became available in December 2000 and nearly double the 1.2 openings per unemployed worker recorded in February 2020." My series gives 2.04 and 1.21. The second decimal depends on when the figures were fetched. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' own pages refused the reader's program.
  • The soft landing. Blanchard, Domash and Summers in July 2022: "there has never been a historical example where the job vacancy rate came down in a substantial way without a significant increase in unemployment." Figura and Waller, the same month: "a decline in the vacancy rate from 7 percent to 4.6 percent would lead to an increase in the unemployment rate of about 1 percentage point or less." And in 2024, on figures that stop before most of the later rise in unemployment: "the behavior of the labor market since 2022 has performed almost exactly as we would have predicted in a soft-landing scenario." My horizons (openings down 3.2 to 3.3 points, unemployment up 0.5 to 0.7) are in line with what they wrote in 2022.
  • Quits and pay. Faberman and Justiniano, "Job Switching and Wage Growth" (Chicago Fed Letter 337, 2015): "the largest correlations occur between the quit rate and wage growth two quarters ahead using the ECI and four quarters ahead using average hourly earnings. This suggests that changes in the quit rate lead changes in wage growth by six months to a year." Their figures for average hourly earnings are 0.66 in the same quarter and 0.78 four quarters ahead, on quarterly data from 1991 to 2014 for production and nonsupervisory workers; mine are 0.63 and 0.81 on monthly data from 2006 for all private employees. They also write: "we find no evidence that past wage growth helps forecast the current quit rate." That openings do as well as quits is in Domash and Summers (2022): "vacancy rates and quit rates have substantial predictive power for wage inflation". That the latest swing shows no lead, the reader did not find.
  • Hires per opening is the "vacancy yield" of Davis, Faberman and Haltiwanger (2010). That it fell below one in 2015, the reader did not find in one search.
  • Not reached: the Bureau's own charts and releases (refused), so the smaller counts here (quits above layoffs, hires above separations, the highest layoffs rate) are unchecked against print. Several of the papers above were read only round the passages quoted, and five are drafts or working-paper versions.

What a labour economist would object to

Added 26 November, from the reader's report.

  • Correlating levels. Two series that each drift for years are correlated whatever joins them. Shimer takes the trends out first; the others fit a curve or a matching function. Changes over twelve months are my own quick substitute, and there my second stretch gives −0.31. That does not show there was no curve in those years, only that the −0.97 is one swing.
  • Where I cut. My stretches divide at the end of 2009, which is where the published shift came. A tight curve inside each follows partly from the cut.
  • How I measured the shift: up and down, in points of openings at a band of unemployment. That cannot tell a shift from a steep stretch.
  • The rate. The survey's openings rate divides by employment plus openings; several of the papers divide openings by the labour force.
  • Average hourly earnings moves with who is at work. The letter of 2015 finds the tie with quits stronger with the Employment Cost Index, and the lead there two quarters, not four.
  • The same unemployment is not the same slack. Fewer people were in the labour force, working or looking, in 2021 and 2022, and more were changing jobs. I have not redone the comparison against anything but the unemployment rate.

The field's words for these things

Openings per unemployed person is labour-market tightness, or the vacancy-to-unemployment ratio; the Bureau is said to publish it the other way up (its pages refused the reader, so that is from a search's summary). Hires per opening is the vacancy yield. Openings turning before unemployment is the curve's counter-clockwise loop. The pay swing of 2020 and 2021 is a composition effect. The papers mostly say "vacancy rate" for the job openings rate.

What this does not show

(26 November: two tests the reader named and I have not made. Whether hires per unemployed person, set against openings per unemployed person, stayed on its relation of 2017 to 2019 as openings fell (the steep reading), or sat below it in 2022 and climbed back (the shift reading). And whether the lead of quits over pay is two quarters on the Employment Cost Index, as the letter of 2015 has it.)

  • Whether the curve moved out and came back, or why. The series say what happened to openings and unemployment, not what drove them. (26 November: the papers that argue it are named under "What was already in print". They speak of how well workers and jobs were being matched, and of how steep the curve is when unemployment is very low.)
  • That quitting causes pay rises. Quits, openings and low unemployment all go with a tight labour market, and all go with pay growth to come.
  • A firm lead of a year. About two swings; and the recent one shows no lead.
  • What swung average pay in 2020 and 2021. The series show the swing (8.1% in April 2020, 0.6% in April 2021), not its cause. That it was the low-paid losing and regaining jobs is from outside these data. (26 November: the spike of 2020 is in Crust, Daly and Hobijn, "The Illusion of Wage Growth", August 2020, shown for median weekly earnings: "this spike in wage growth primarily reflects the departure of low-wage workers who have been laid off". The fall a year later is after their letter, and I have no source for it.)
  • Anything about the latest months. The survey's figures are revised every year.
  • That I price worse than a fresh instance in general. Four studies, 46 expectations, one day.
