Leads (thoughts of mine, unchecked; not instructions)

  1. Use the contraction inside the counting, not only before it. In the third result the lost pairs are summed over all levels P′ of the other trees, L_Y of them. A level of a tree at which no group is full costs nothing (Lemma T's Fact 3). For a multiset of m elements the levels (i, P′) at which a loss is possible at all are those where full groups exist in the right slices; perhaps the sum over P′ can be replaced by a sum over such levels, weighted by how many elements they need, so that it converges without a factor L_Y.
  2. Cells. A signing is bad at d exactly when, for the squarefree part g of d over the primes up to m and d_0 = d/g, every class modulo d_0 ("cell") has a vanishing local sum at a primitive g-th root (from Mann's theorem on minimal relations, or from the slice criterion applied prime by prime). For independent uniform signs the cells are independent, so P(bad at d) ≤ 2^(−number of occupied cells). Only the levels with one or few cells are dangerous; for the squarefree levels (one cell) the second result gives a positive share of good patterns. Can the two be combined (choose the class sums well for the top levels, and use the remaining freedom inside classes for the deeper ones, where only ONE good cell is needed)?
  3. Bottom-up over one tree. By induction on the number of elements, each class C of the p-tree at depth 1 has a signing good in its own right; its slice S_C is then nonzero modulo every Φ_{d″}. What is needed at the root is a choice of such signings with Σ_C S_C good, and, when all p classes are occupied, with the slices not all congruent modulo any Φ_{d″}. Flipping all signs of one class does not disturb the deeper levels.
  4. Sharper constants. The third result records two constants that all its tests say are too pessimistic (single(3, a) = 1/2, and λ(p, a) = 2^(1−p)); with Theorem R of my note any gain in Γ moves the bound 36. A count that uses that the multiset has only m elements (the trees are almost empty) might gain much more: for instance Lemma T for a function with few active leaves.
