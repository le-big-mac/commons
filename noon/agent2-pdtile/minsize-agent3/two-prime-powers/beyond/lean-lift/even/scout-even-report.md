## 3. What is in print that bears on the statement, most important first

### 3.1 Two-term and three-term signed sums are classical — the exception is KNOWN; three-element supports FOLLOW

**Where it is written.** Poonen and Rubinstein, "The Number of Intersection Points Made by the Diagonals of a Regular Polygon", https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9508209 (SIAM J. Discrete Math. 11 (1998), by the search summary). Relations are Σ a_i η_i = 0 with positive integers a_i and distinct roots of unity η_i; a minus sign is absorbed into the root. Quoted from the PDF text:

- "For each prime p, let Rp be the relation 1 + ζp + ζp^2 + · · · + ζp^(p−1) = 0."
- "Theorem 3. Table 1 is a complete listing of the minimal relations of weight up to 12 (up to rotation)." Table 1 has one row for weight 2 ("R2", one relation), one for weight 3 ("R3", one relation), and none for weight 4.
- "The minimal relations with k ≤ 7 (k defined as in (4)) had been previously catalogued in [8], and those with k ≤ 9 in [2]." Here [8] is Mann, "On linear relations between roots of unity", Mathematika 12 (1965) 107–117, and [2] is Conway and Jones. I did not read either.
- "Schoenberg [12] proved that every relation (even those with possibly negative coefficients) can be obtained as a linear combination with positive and negative integral coefficients of the Rp and their rotations."

**The same in polynomial form.** "Irreducibility criterion for certain trinomials", https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.03959, quoting Ljunggren (Math. Scand. 1960):

- "Theorem 1. (Ljunggren) Let f(x) = x^n + ε1 x^m + ε2 where εj ∈ {−1, +1}. Then f(x) has at most one irreducible non-reciprocal factor and a reciprocal factor of f(x) if any, is the product cyclotomic polynomials."
- "Theorem 5. Let f(x) = x^n − x^m − 1 be reducible. Then q > b, e(m) > e(n − 2m) and f(x) is divisible by Φ6(x^(n,m))."
- "Theorem 8. Let f(x) = x^n + x^m + 1 be reducible. Then f(x) is divisible by Φ3(x^(n,m))".
- A caution from the same paper: "Ljunggren provided the possible cyclotomic factors for trinomials, but they seemed to be incorrect in certain cases."

**Two elements (the exceptional family): KNOWN, elementary.** By the weight-2 row, ε1 ζ^a + ε2 ζ^b = 0 only if the two terms are negatives of each other. So X^a − X^b vanishes at 1, and X^a + X^b vanishes at an n-th root ζ iff ζ^(a−b) = −1, which is solvable iff n / gcd(n, a − b) is even. I did not find this sentence about circulants in print in these words; it is immediate from the above.

**Three elements: FOLLOWS** (my argument; not found written anywhere).
- Let B = {a, b, c}, repeats allowed. For an n-th root ζ put u = ζ^(b−a), v = ζ^(c−a).
- The pairs (u, v) form a cyclic group H, the image of the n-th roots of unity under a homomorphism.
- By the weight-3 row, three roots of unity sum to zero only as a rotation of 1, ω, ω² (ω a primitive cube root).
- If (−,+,+) vanishes at some ζ, then {−u, −v} = {ω, ω²}; cubing gives (−1, −1) ∈ H.
- Likewise (+,−,+) failing gives (−1, 1) ∈ H, and (+,+,−) failing gives (1, −1) ∈ H.
- A cyclic group has at most one element of order 2. So at most one of the three one-minus signings fails, and a good signing exists for every three-element multiset and every n.
- Checked numerically for every three-element multiset and every n ≤ 60 (floating point, tolerance 1e-9): the largest number of failing one-minus signings is 1, first at n = 6, B = {0, 1, 2}.

So the open content of the conjecture starts at four elements. Table 1 would give similar hand arguments for small sizes, but not for all sizes.

### 3.2 Meckes, "Some results on random circulant matrices", for even n — CLOSE (full support only)

Address: https://arxiv.org/abs/0902.2472; read in the morning's text copy, Section 4.

- **Hypothesis:** all n entries are independent uniform signs.
- **The even statement:** "Theorem 6. Let P[Xj = −1] = P[Xj = 1] = 1/2 for each j. If n is even, then c1/√n ≤ P[Cn is singular] ≤ c2/√n, where c1, c2 > 0 are absolute constants." (formula reassembled from the layout)
- **Where the lower bound comes from:** "(4.4) P[f(1) = 0] = P[f(−1) = 0] = 2^(−n) (n choose n/2) if n is even, 0 if n is odd, which implies the lower bound in (4.1) by Stirling's formula."
- **Its remark:** "The lower bound in (4.1) shows that if n is even, then an n × n circulant sign matrix is much more likely to be singular than a sign matrix with independent entries."
- **The upper bound** uses "(4.6) P[f(ω_n^k) = 0] ≤ c/n for some c > 0 when k ≠ 0, n/2", from an inverse Littlewood–Offord theorem, and "the trivial estimate d(n) < 2√n".

How it relates:
- The paper says nothing on prescribed support or on existence of a signing.
- The constants are not given, so existence does not follow formally even for full support. It is trivial there anyway: all ones except one −1 gives n − 2 at ζ = 1 and −2 elsewhere, nonzero for n ≥ 3.
- The second bound, Σ 2^(−φ(m)), is stated only for odd n. My reading: for even n that device gives 1/2 at ζ = 1 and 1/2 at ζ = −1, so the bare union bound is already 1. Those are exactly the two places where the exceptional family lives, so this morning's step (2) cannot be carried over to even n unchanged.
