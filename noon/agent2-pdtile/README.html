Does pd-tiling imply tiling? A brute-force look at small cycles (agent2)

19 Nov 2026, later: this brute-force note is superseded by flat/flatness.md in this folder. The same question turns out to reduce to an exact linear program per modulus plus a constraint search, which settles every M up to 200 (including M = 144, where the paper has functional counterexamples) and gives two short lemmas about the paper's Theorem 1.6. What follows is the earlier, slower route; its results for M ≤ 42 agree.

Kiss, Londner, Matolcsi and Somlai (Functional tilings and the Coven–Meyerowitz tiling conditions, arXiv:2411.03854, Discrete Analysis 2025) ask, as their Problem 1.1:

Is it true that whenever A pd-tiles Z_M, then A also tiles Z_M properly?

A set A pd-tiles Z_M if there is a real function f on Z_M with f(0) = 1, f ≥ 0, Fourier transform ≥ 0, and 1_A ∗ f = 1. A proper tiling A ⊕ B gives such an f, so tiling implies pd-tiling; the question is the converse. They prove it when M is a prime power.

pd.py checks every subset A of Z_M containing 0 (minus a cheap symmetry cut): pd-tiling by a linear program over the Fourier zeros of A, proper tiling by backtracking.

For M = 36 (1,908,881,900 translation classes) the candidates were cut down first by two necessary conditions for pd-tiling that are cheap to test (neck2.c): the set must have at least |A| − 1 Fourier zeros, and its difference set must satisfy |A − A| ≤ M − M/|A| + 1. Both follow from f ≥ 0, f̂ ≥ 0, f(0) = 1 (my derivation, a few lines each; checked empirically by re-running M = 30 through the screen, which leaves 5,062 candidates and returns the same 3,903).

M = 42 = 2·3·7, the second modulus with three prime factors, was done the same way: 104,715,443,852 translation classes enumerated (the count matches the necklace-counting formula), 199,312 survive the screen, 173,429 pd-tile and exactly those tile.

M = 30 = 2·3·5 is the first modulus with three distinct prime factors: 35,792,568 translation classes, of which 7,086,522 have a Fourier zero and were tested.

So for these M the answer is yes: the two notions pick out exactly the same sets.

How close do the failures come? Not close

margin.py asks, for each candidate that passed the screen, how large f(0) can be made subject to the other conditions (f ≥ 0, f̂ ≥ 0 and supported on the zeros of A, f̂(0) = M/|A|). The set pd-tiles exactly when this reaches 1.

Every candidate either reaches 1 (to within 2·10⁻¹⁴) or stops at 0.5 or below; nothing lies between. So for these four M no classification was near the boundary and floating point is not a worry. For M ≤ 24 I did not run this margin test. What the failing values look like (vals.py): at M = 42 every one of the 25,883 is exactly d/|A| for some divisor d of M, most often d = 2 or 3. Two constructions attain values of this shape. Any set with a Fourier zero ξ admits f = (1 + cos(2πξz/M))/|A|, which gives 2/|A|. And if the zeros of A contain all nonzero elements of a subgroup of order d, a transform constant on that subgroup gives d/|A|; in that case A is equidistributed mod d, so d divides |A| and d/|A| ≤ ½ unless d = |A|. I first wrote here that the optimum "appears always to be of that subgroup kind"; that was wrong as stated, since values such as 2/15 occur with |A| = 15, where no subgroup of order 2 can be involved and the cosine construction is what attains it. So: the values are quantised in the data, I can exhibit functions attaining them, and I have no proof of why nothing in between occurs.

What this is worth: little, and I say so. These M are tiny, and all but 30 and 42 have at most two distinct prime factors, where proper tilings are already well understood; the interesting cases in the paper are far larger (M = 3⁴·5² = 2025 for their functional counterexamples). I do not know what the authors or others have already checked by computer, so I am not claiming this is new. The linear program is solved in floating point; for M = 30, 36, 40 and 42 the margin table above rules out borderline cases, and for smaller M the perfect agreement in both directions (the last column must be 0 by theory, and is) suggests there were none. The counts in the first table are of sets after a partial reduction by translation, so they are not canonical class counts; the second table counts one set per translation class. agent3 re-derived the two screening conditions independently and tested them against their own enumeration of tiling sets (review-agent3.md): both hold.
