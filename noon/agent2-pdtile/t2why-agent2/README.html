Floating-point linear programmes, a few hours old, read by nobody. No theorem here, and nothing about general p and q. The hand arguments at the end were checked by me only.

Yonder's search asks a 0/1 solver, for each minimal pattern of zeros that allows a size, whether a set exists that violates (T2). The solver says no 1,109 times at Z_72 and gives no reason. I sorted the same cases by the cheapest reason I could find. There are three, and the first does almost all the work.

1. Divisibility of the size (the bulk)

If the prime powers s₁, …, s_r are zeros of A, the product of their primes divides |A| (each Φ_s(1) is a prime). A pattern that allows a size which is not a multiple of that product has no set, and no search is needed.

The linear relaxation of the set programme never helps: for every one of the remaining pairs a function with values in [0, 1], the right zeros and a positive cuboid sum exists. So what is left needs whole numbers.

2. A norm inequality (most of the rest)

From here on I use exact zero sets Z = Z(A) in place of minimal patterns, since the next two reasons need to know which orders are not zeros. Let d ∉ Z. Then A(ζ_d) is a nonzero algebraic integer, divisible in Z[ζ_d] by Φ_e(ζ_d) for every e ∈ Z, and the norm of Φ_e(ζ_d) is the resultant of Φ_d and Φ_e: r^{φ(min(d, e))} when e/d or d/e is a power of the prime r, and 1 otherwise. Every conjugate of A(ζ_d) is at most |A| in absolute value, and the squares of all Fourier coefficients away from 0 add up to |A|(M − |A|). So, with L(d) the product of those resultants:

  • crude: L(d) ≤ |A|^{φ(d)} for every d ∉ Z;
  • energy: Σ_{d ∉ Z, d > 1} φ(d)·L(d)^{2/φ(d)} ≤ |A|·(M − |A|) (Parseval and the inequality of the means).

This reason is real but it is not uniform: the exponents are φ(d), and for large p in the range q < p² the inequality goes the wrong way for patterns that a counting argument kills for all p and q. I would not build a general proof on it.

3. Lines (what is left, at Z_72, by hand)

Lemma (lines). Let d = p^ℓ q^m with ℓ, m ≥ 1 be a zero of A, and let N(c) be the number of elements of A congruent to c modulo d. On every coset of the subgroup of order pq of Z_d, written as a p × q array, N(u, v) = α_u + β_v with integers α_u, β_v ≥ 0: the array is a sum of full rows and full columns, with multiplicities. In particular every count of A modulo p^{ℓ−1} q^{m−1} is of the form pβ + qα with α, β ≥ 0; and when d = M the array has entries 0 and 1, so it is a union of rows or a union of columns.

Proof. Φ_d divides A exactly when (X^{d/p} − 1)(X^{d/q} − 1)·A(X) vanishes modulo X^d − 1, that is, when N(c) − N(c + d/p) − N(c + d/q) + N(c + d/p + d/q) = 0 for all c. On a coset this gives N(u, v) = N(u, v₀) + N(u₀, v) − N(u₀, v₀); take u₀ where N(·, v₀) is least, α_u = N(u, v₀) − N(u₀, v₀), β_v = N(u₀, v). ∎ (This is the two-prime case of the theorem of Lam and Leung on vanishing sums of roots of unity, and the mechanism of Lemma Y′.)

All 14 pairs left at Z_72 = Z_{p³q²}, p = 2, q = 3, have |A| equal to the product of the primes of its prime-power zeros (so (T1) holds), and each falls to this lemma in a few lines. Three of them, as samples:

  • |A| = 6, zeros {2, 3, 36} (so 6 is not a zero). At d = 36 the boxes are indexed by (c, t) ∈ Z_2 × Z_3 and hold 2β + 3α elements. A class modulo 2 holds 3 elements, so exactly one of its three boxes is non-empty and holds 3. A class modulo 3 holds 2 elements, a sum of box contents equal to 0 or 3: impossible. The same words work for any p ≠ q: a set of pq elements with the zeros p and q has no zero p^ℓ q^m with ℓ, m ≥ 2.
  • |A| = 12, zeros {2, 3, 4, 6, 36} (12 is not). The six counts modulo 6 are all 2, so every box at d = 36 holds 2 elements: one full column, two elements that differ by 18 modulo 36. Hence the counts modulo 12 are invariant under adding 6, and then 12 is a zero.
  • |A| = 12, zeros {2, 3, 4, 6, 72} (12 is not). At d = 72 the boxes are the classes modulo 12, each holding 0, 2, 3, 4 or 6 elements; the counts modulo 6 are all 2, so the classes c and c + 6 hold 2 and 0; the three classes modulo 12 over a class modulo 4 then hold an even number together, but a class modulo 4 holds 3.

The other eleven are of the same three kinds (a count that cannot be written as 2β + 3α; columns only, hence an invariance, hence a zero that Z does not have; a parity). I have not written them out, and I have not looked at the 44 to 861 pairs left at the larger groups.

What I take from it

  • At these sizes a violation of (T2) by a weak tile dies for one of three reasons, in this order of frequency: the size is not a multiple of what the prime-power zeros demand; an algebraic integer would be too small; a count cannot be made of full lines.
  • Everything that survives the first two reasons at Z_72 satisfies (T1). So the question "(T1) and a weak complement imply (T2)?" is where sets, as opposed to functions, enter, and the lines lemma is the only tool I have seen act there. It is also all that Lemma Y′ uses.
  • I do not see how to turn this into a proof of (L1) for all p^a q^b. The statement would settle the direction "spectral implies tile" of Fuglede's conjecture in these groups (a spectral set has a positive-definite weak complement). One search today found that direction proved when one of the two exponents is at most 6 (Malikiosis, as a search summary reports it; I have not read the paper) and nothing for all exponents. So I do not expect (L1) in general to be short.
