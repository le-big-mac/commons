A third addition: other shapes, and a ceiling

Added in the night of 24 to 25 November, with its own sealed sheet and its own referee. The second addition ended on "It is one shape. For a square or a triangle I have no prediction". This does that sum for any convex shape. On paper it comes out that the spread of the count, set against its mean, is the same for ellipses, triangles, parallelograms and regular polygons and is smaller for every other convex shape; and 384,000 fresh clouds were counted against sixteen lines written first, of which sixteen held.

The sum for any convex shape. Let the shape K have area A and hold n points, ρ = n/A. The layers follow the flow that moves every point of the edge inward at the cube root of the curvature; let T be the time K takes to vanish under it (3/4 for a disc of radius 1). The mean count is α·ρ^(2/3)·T, as before. For the spread one more thing is needed: ψ(p), the time by which the shape vanishes sooner if a little area is taken from it at the place p at the moment the front passes p, per unit of that area. The integral of ψ over the shape is T. A patch of the cloud that is a little too thin or too thick, or whose points merely lie luckily, moves the front on or back by an area whose variance the first addition measured (D, per unit of area crossed), and each unit of area so gained is worth ψ of time. Adding the patches:

variance of the count, number of points left to chance:   ρ^(1/3) · D · I,       I = ∫ ψ² over K
variance of the count, exactly n points:                  ρ^(1/3) · (D · I − (4/9)·α²·T²/A)

(shape/notes.md has the working; shape/theory.py the numbers.) For the disc ψ = r^(−2/3)/2π and these are finding 10 and the second addition's. Nothing is fitted here either: α = 1.3259, D = 1.081.

14. For any convex shape the spread of the count is fixed by two numbers of the flow, T and I. Both are computed by shape/flow.c, which runs the flow forward in the shape's support function and then solves once backward for ψ; T is computed a second way by shape/erode.c (affine erosion, a different scheme), and the two agree to 2 parts in 10,000 on all nine shapes. (A tenth of I comes from the last thousandth of the area, which flow.c takes as an ellipse's; stopping at a ten-thousandth instead moves I by 7 parts in a million at most, and a starting shape a hundred times less rounded at its corners moves T and I by as little: shape/stopcheck.out.) For shapes of area π:

triangle (any) 1.09321 1.3333
square, rectangle 1.04807 1.3333
regular hexagon 1.02016 1.3333
half disc 1.02843 1.3304

triangle with round corners 1.07121 1.3056

The first column is the mean count over a disc's of the same area and number of points: 1.0481 and 1.0932 are what the first study measured for squares and triangles (4.8 and 9.3 in 100 more layers).

15. A ceiling: I is never more than (4/3)·T²/A, and is exactly that for ellipses, triangles, parallelograms and regular polygons. The second column of the table came out at 4/3 for the polygons to five figures, which sent me back to paper. Write g for ψ on the edge of K itself, taken per unit of the angle θ of the edge's normal instead of per unit of length, and L for the operator 1 + d²/dθ². Then (shape/notes.md, 4 and 5)

I = (3/2) · ∫ g · L⁻¹g dθ,

so the integral of ψ² over the whole inside of the shape is fixed by ψ on its edge; and from that, with Minkowski's inequality for mixed areas,

T²/A  ≤  I  ≤  (4/3)·T²/A,

with the upper end reached exactly when ψ is the same all along the edge in the sense that matters: when moving the edge a little, in any way the shape allows, changes T only as it changes A^(2/3). An ellipse is such a shape (the flow's time is least for it, at given area). So is every triangle and every parallelogram, because moving a side of one gives another of the same family, for which T/A^(2/3) is the same number; and every regular polygon, by symmetry. In counts: since the mean is α·ρ^(2/3)·T,

variance / mean²  =  ((D/α²)·(I·A/T²) − 4/9) / n       (exactly n points; without the 4/9 when left to chance)

so for every convex shape the standard deviation of the count is, when the points are many, at most 0.6127/√n of its mean with exactly n points and 0.9055/√n with the number left to chance, and those are the figures for a disc, a triangle, a square. In the units of the plate (variance × n / mean²) the ceiling is 0.3754, and 0.8199 left to chance. A third bound comes from the affine isoperimetric inequality: I is never less than a disc's of the same area (shape/notes.md, 7). So with the number left to chance an ellipse's count has the least variance of any convex shape's, as it has the least mean; what the ceiling adds is that in proportion to the mean nothing has more than the ellipse, and the triangle and the square have as much.

flow.c computes I both ways, summed over the inside and from the edge: they agree to 3 parts in 100,000 on 52 shapes (shape/ceiling.out). The two ways share the backward solve, so that tests the identity and not the solve. The solve is tested by the other program alone: erode.c gives the change of T when each side of a polygon is moved, and from those the edge formula gives I, to 4 parts in 10,000 of flow.c's for a triangle and for a square with a corner cut off (shape/stest.py); and for the quarter disc it gives the change of T when the arc is pushed out in three places and when each flat side is moved, to 2 parts in 10,000 of what flow.c's g says (shape/arccheck.out). Of the 52 shapes, nine are at the ceiling to 5 parts in 100,000 (disc, ellipse, three triangles, square, rectangle, regular pentagon and hexagon: five shapes, if copies under affine maps are counted once), none is above it, and the lowest is an equilateral triangle with its corners rounded, 1.3056 against 1.3333; nine shapes built to go lower (two other triangles rounded, a square with a corner cut off and then rounded) did not, and nor did any shape met on the way when eleven shapes were followed along the flow (the least there 1.309, good to about 0.001; shape/along.out). How low a convex shape can go I do not know.

Were those two chance? I peeled fresh clouds to see (146,000 of them, in no verdict; shape/after.out, K). 30,000 fresh discs give 0.3777 (0.0031): the first set was a high draw. 76,000 fresh quarter discs give 0.3796 (0.0019), which is nearer but still 2.8 errors above the quarter disc's line and 2.1 above the ceiling; and 40,000 fresh half discs, peeled to see whether such corners matter, give 0.3725 (0.0026), on their line. What is left is not the quarter disc's alone. At 100,000 points every kind of cloud stands a little above the theory: the sheet's nine sets together by 0.7 (0.3) parts in 100, and with the fresh clouds twelve sets by 0.8 (0.2). At 30,000 points the three sets stand on it: 0.999 (0.004). Set against that common level (the six ceiling sets' 0.3774) the quarter disc is 0.9 of an error high. I have no account of an excess that is there at the larger size and not at the smaller; it is 1.9 errors from being the same at both.

The first study's triangles, which had looked more variable than this (0.112 and 0.118 for the variance over n^(1/3), with errors of 0.011 and 0.022, from a few hundred clouds), give 0.0967 (0.0006) with 50,000, where the theory says 0.0964.

17. The triangle with round corners falls short of the ceiling by what was worked out. Its variance over the square of its mean, divided by the plain triangle's, was sealed at 0.9547. With 50,000 clouds of each: 0.9595 (0.0086) at 100,000 points and 0.9560 (0.0086) at 30,000; together 0.9577 (0.0061), half an error from 0.9547 and 6.9 errors from 1. The tests. shape/stest.py (92 tests when the broken copies were tried) holds the nine shapes written out a second time from their definitions, the evenness of the generator's points, T and I for the disc and the ellipse, the two programs against each other, and the runner of clouds. Faults were then planted, one at a time, in copies: 58 faults in the five programs, of which 57 were noticed (shape/smutants.out); 24 faults in the scoring script, tried on made-up runs drawn from the theory and from eleven other worlds (shape/ruler.py, 36 cases): 24 were noticed, after a first run that let three through and the two cases it wanted were added (shape/ymutants.out); and 22 faults in the plate's own script, of which 22 were noticed (shape/pmutants.out). Each runner first puts the unbroken copy through the same path. Twice that control failed, which made the run void: the copies of the programs had no peeling program beside them (kept as shape/smutants-first-run.out, where all 58 are "noticed" for that reason alone), and two checks I had just added to the plate's test were themselves wrong. The one fault of the 58 that got through draws the disc's points from a box 1 part in 100 short on one side, so that a sliver of 6 parts in 10,000 of the disc is left empty. Run beside the true program on 2,000 discs each, it gives the same count: means 0.10 (0.06) apart, variances 3.801 and 3.795 (shape/passed.out). That is the ceiling at work: for a disc of a fixed number of points, area taken from the edge shortens the flow and thickens the cloud by amounts that cancel to first order. A test of the strips along the sides of each shape's box was added and notices it (shape/stest.py now has 101 tests).

What the third addition does not say

  • The ceiling is a fact about the equation, not a theorem about peeling. It is proved (by me, on one night, with a pencil) for the formula above: a linear answer, through ψ, to a white noise of strength D. That formula is the first addition's equation carried to a general shape; what the clouds test is whether it describes them, in nine shapes, to about 1 part in 100.
  • One shape tests the shortfall. The half and quarter disc fall short by a half and a third of one part in 100, which 8,000 clouds cannot see; only the rounded triangle was made to fall far enough, and it was chosen because it falls furthest of what I tried.
  • 1.3056 is the least of 52 shapes, not the least there is. The true floor for convex shapes is somewhere between 1 and 1.3056.
  • Which polygons are at the ceiling I know only in part. A polygon is there exactly when no way of moving its sides changes T/A^(2/3) to first order. Triangles and parallelograms are (nothing can change it), regular polygons and kites are (by symmetry), and their affine copies. A trapezoid is not (1.3328), nor a square with a corner cut off (1.3195). The referee's blind step could not tell four polygons with an incircle from 4/3; at ten times its accuracy six such polygons fall short by between nothing (a kite) and 0.00012 (an irregular hexagon): close, and not there (shape/incircle.out).
  • The discs from the new generator came out 2 parts in 100 above the line (2.3 errors), and above the discs from the old one by 2.2 in 100 (1.6 errors). Fresh discs say it was chance (finding 16); the two sets of discs have the same mean count to 3 parts in a million, and at 30,000 points the same generator's discs are on the line (0.3753). But every ratio "over the disc's" on the sheet is lower for it.
  • A part in 100 at 100,000 points is not accounted for (finding 16). If it is D, the clouds of 100,000 want 1.085 where the level problem gave 1.081 and the clouds of 30,000 want 1.080; the first addition's D had two corrections of that size already. The shortfall of the rounded triangle is a ratio and does not feel it.
  • D is not measured again here. Read backwards from each set of clouds, if T and I are right, D comes out between 1.078 and 1.101 (shape/after.out, D); the six sets at the ceiling together give 1.0836 (0.0019), a quarter of one part in 100 above 1.081. That is conditional on the theory it would test. And the ceiling is only as well placed as D: at 1.073 (the level problem's first figure) it would stand at 0.3694, at 1.095 (what the second addition's discs gave) at 0.3860.
  • Corners. In the flow ψ is unbounded at a corner at the first moment, and I expected the triangle to show it at 100,000 points. It does not: 0.3765 (0.0024) and, at 30,000, 0.3747 (0.0024).
  • Convex shapes, even density, the plane.
  • Not in print, as far as three searches went (the variance of the number of layers of a cloud in a general shape; the sensitivity of the flow's vanishing time; either with "polygon" or "triangle"). The nearest things are the same as before: Calder and Smart's open problem on the fluctuations, and Calka and Quilan's limit laws for the first few layers, in a ball and in a polytope. That convex curves shrink to elliptical points under this flow I have from Eppstein, Har-Peled and Nivasch's account of Sapiro and Tannenbaum and of Angenent, Sapiro and Tannenbaum (quoted in sources.md); I have not read those papers.
