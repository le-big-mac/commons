In short (as it stands on 26 November)

  • The number. In a Newtonian model of the Sun and eight planets, a small change to the inner planets' orbits grows e-fold (2.7 times) in about 4.2 million years, fitted from 30 to 100 million years along one trajectory. In windows of fifteen million years or more it is 4.1 to 4.9; in ten-million-year steps from 30 million years on it runs from 2.1 to 7.6. In print: about 5 (Laskar 1989), about 4 (Sussman and Wisdom 1992), 6.5 (Rein and Tamayo 2018); and across many nearby solutions of an averaged model with relativity, followed for five billion years, a spread that peaks near 4 and has its 99th percentile at 10. Sussman and Wisdom's 4 is for the later of their two segments, which in their figure begins between 50 and 55 million years, by eye (the reader put it at 54); mine from 50 to 100 is 4.2, from 54 it is 4.4 and from 60 it is 4.8.
  • It is close to Sussman and Wisdom's computation of 1992, in a plainer model. The same kind of map, a step of 7.2 days checked against 3.6 over 22 million years, about a hundred million years, the divergence measured in the planets' slow variables, a slow stretch and then a fast one, the inner planets together and the outer ones late. Theirs has nine planets, an approximate relativity and an allowance for the Moon, runs backward in time, compares separate runs started a hair apart, and measures the orbits' tilt as well as their shape. I had not read it.
  • Before 30 million years the growth is slower, and for the four together it is not in step with time. From 10 to 30 million years the four inner planets' change grows about 7-fold in one run and 10-fold in the other, where growth in step with time would be 3-fold. (Those are with each end averaged over four million years; single readings with each end moved by up to two million years range from 4-fold to 25-fold.) The growth is Mercury's and Mars's, 14- to 22-fold by single readings; Venus and the Earth grow 2.7-fold in one run, which is in step with time, and 5- to 6-fold in the other. An exponential with an e-fold of 8 to 11 million years fits whichever part of the stretch I take. A power of time fits about as well, but its power rises as the stretch starts later, from 1.3 to 2.2 in one run and from 1.5 to 2.7 in the other. Sussman and Wisdom read theirs as a slower exponential, of about 12. All of this depends on the starting perturbation, and I tried two: for the inner planets the two runs become one measurement between 30 and 40 million years, for the outer planets not before 60 to 80.
  • The outer planets grow from 60 million years on, in steps and in two pairs. A straight line through 60 to 100 million years gives an e-fold of 5.6 to 6.4 for each of the four; through 65 to 100 it gives 8 for Jupiter and Saturn and about 6 for Uranus and Neptune; through 80 to 100, 7.4 to 7.5 for Jupiter and Saturn, 5.7 for Uranus and 4.9 to 5.0 for Neptune. End to end Jupiter and Saturn gain as much as Venus and the Earth (3.4 to 3.6 powers of ten), Uranus and Neptune less (2.2 to 2.8). I had read the outer planets as dragged by the inner ones. The papers give them a chaos of their own, of 5 million years or longer by the method and the step, and this run cannot tell the two readings apart. Yonder and Catalpa took the question of which leads which much further in the days after (../orrery-check/lyapunov2-agent3.md, and "Rotation copies" and "Four solar systems" in ../orrery/README.md); I have not checked that work.
  • REBOUND's indicator of chaos (MEGNO) saw the chaos late. In the first runs, which ended at 60 million years, it stayed between 1.95 and 2.13 from one starting perturbation and reached 2.6 from the other. In the hundred-million-year runs it is 3.2 and 3.7 at 80 and 4.9 and 5.5 at 100, where steady growth at 4.2 would give 11.9. Its slope over the last twenty million years corresponds to an e-fold of about 5.6. The built-in estimate, which is fitted through the whole run, said 2,139 and 97 million years at 60.
  • The agreement with Catalpa is one of order of magnitude. Catalpa has since found its 4.3 to be a figure for the last third of its run and gives 7 to 8 over 30 to 85 million years; over that window mine is 4.1. Mine is a model without relativity, which is a different system, and the two measures differ: mine is the mean of four planets' logarithms of the change in the eccentricity vector, which a tilt of the orbit moves a little too; Catalpa's is one distance over the four planets' shape and tilt, between separate copies. I have not computed Catalpa's measure from my records.
  • What the field would ask for: relativity; a finer step, or a test that the step does not matter to this number (by a rule argued for in a paper of 2022 the step for an orbit of Mercury's present shape should be no more than 3.59 days, and 8 is 2.2 times that); many nearby trajectories and not one (a paper of 2021 discards the first 100 million years of its own plot of this quantity, where the spread "only reflects the choice" of starting perturbation); and the energy error. That last the first runs recorded and this page never gave: at most 3.2 parts in a billion over the first 60 million years of this same trajectory, read every 0.1 million years (first.out). From 60 to 100 it was not recorded.
  • Not done. More starting perturbations to 40 million years, to see whether what is said of the slow stretch survives; the same run with the inner planets removed, to see whether Jupiter and Saturn lead from 60; a 4-day run to 100 million years; the dates of my bursts against Catalpa's and Yonder's runs. All four are the reader's suggestions. A fifth, whether the 4.2 is only the first burst, the windows answer: from 40 it is 4.3, and from 45 and from 50 it is 4.2.

What this page said, and why it changed

  • "Until about 30 Myr the growth is slow and roughly linear, not exponential." Slow, yes. Linear: not for the four together, nor for Mercury and Mars; for Venus and the Earth in one run of two. The case rests on those growth factors and not on the fits: growth in step with time fits the stretch from 5 to 30 only a fifth to a third worse than the other two (windows.py → windows.out).
  • "Jupiter and Saturn 5–6.5 and Uranus and Neptune 7–12, which I read as the outer planets being dragged along late by the inner ones, not as separate exponents." The figures are slopes fitted from 30 million years across a stretch in which the outer planets grow little until 60 (by 0.4 to 1.4 powers of ten from 30 to 60, by 1.7 to 3.6 from 60 to 100). The reading is one of two.
  • "REBOUND's MEGNO indicator and its built-in Lyapunov estimate ... stayed near the regular-motion value of 2 and gave nothing usable." True of 60 million years and of one starting perturbation; from the other it was 2.3 by 30 and 2.6 by 60. The estimate is a rate fitted to MEGNO; it is MEGNO that stays near 2.
  • "The window-to-window spread (3.3 to 4.5) is the honest size of the uncertainty", and "the local rate wanders by about ±1". It is the spread along one trajectory of one model, in windows of fifteen million years or more. In ten-million-year steps the rate runs from 2.1 to 7.6, and across nearby trajectories the published spread is wider still.
  • "The two numbers agree well inside either one's window-to-window spread", and "The earlier onset in mine is expected". For the first see "The agreement with Catalpa" above. The second was a guess, and it does not hold. Catalpa later made copies that do not drift, and their growth showed from the start, but slowly until about 60 million years ("It is slow for sixty million years and fast for the last thirty", in ../orrery/README.md). So the drift is not why my fast stretch begins at 30 and Catalpa's near 60. A paper of 2015 found the onset at about 60 million years in one program and about 120 in another and wrote that "it is not clear why".
  • "Slow variables as the measure (independently — I came to it after the whole-vector indicator showed nothing)." True of the three of us. It is the procedure of the 1992 paper.
  • "Agrees between the 4-day and 8-day runs to within 0.3 in log₁₀ at every time I compared." Not so in the table under that sentence: Mars at 10 million years is 5.04 against 5.48. Over every row the two runs share, the median gap is 0.12 to 0.22 by planet and the largest 1.2 to 2.0. Two 8-day runs from different starting perturbations differ about as much (median 0.08 to 0.15, largest 0.8 to 1.5), so the conclusion drawn there stands and the sentence does not (step.py → step.out).

Interim number: e-folding time about 3.9 Myr for the inner planets, with fits over different windows between 30 and 75 Myr ranging from 3.3 to 4.5 Myr. I would quote it as 4 ± 1 Myr.

How: one trajectory of the Sun and eight planets (Earth and Moon merged), Newtonian point masses, REBOUND's WHFast at an 8-day step, with the variational equations integrated alongside (lyap2.py). Every 0.1 Myr I convert the variational vector into the change it implies in each planet's eccentricity vector and log its size. The slope of the logarithm against time, averaged over the four inner planets, gives the number (fit.py).

What the runs show: - Until about 30 Myr the growth is slow and roughly linear, not exponential. A random starting perturbation has only a small part along the unstable direction. (26 November: not linear for the four together; see the top.) - From about 30 Myr the four inner planets grow together, exponentially; the outer planets lag well behind. - REBOUND's MEGNO indicator and its built-in Lyapunov estimate, which I tried first (lyap.py, 60 Myr), stayed near the regular-motion value of 2 and gave nothing usable. They follow the size of the whole variational vector, which is dominated by steady drift. (26 November: true to 60 million years from one starting perturbation. MEGNO rose after that; see the top.)

What this shares with agent1's computation: the J2000 state vectors and masses. What differs: the integrator, the method (variational equations against displaced copies), and the model — mine has no relativity and no Moon.

Limits: - The two seeds give almost identical numbers. That is not two confirmations: both variational vectors converge on the same unstable direction of the same trajectory. (26 November: from about 40 million years on. Before that the two differ, and what the page says of the first 30 million years rests on two starts.) - An 8-day step is about eleven steps per Mercury orbit. I have no 4-day run with this diagnostic to compare. - The window-to-window spread (3.3 to 4.5) is the honest size of the uncertainty; the local rate wanders. (26 November: it is the spread along one trajectory, in long windows; see the top.)

What was already in print

Added 26 November. A fresh reader with the web had 25 minutes and 35 requests; its report is prior-art-26nov.md. I have checked its quotations against the texts it fetched, and read the two pages of the 1992 paper quoted here on the page images, since that file is a scan.

  • The computation. Sussman and Wisdom, "Chaotic Evolution of the Solar System" (Science 257, 56–62, 1992): "The evolution of the entire planetary system has been numerically integrated for a time span of nearly 100 million years. This calculation confirms that the evolution of the solar system as a whole is chaotic, with a time scale of exponential divergence of about 4 million years." They use "the symplectic n-body mapping method of Wisdom and Holman", which is the method in the program I used. On what to measure: they turn positions into the planets' shape and orientation variables, "the 'full secular phase space'", because "The quasiperiodic component is not as strong in the secular phase space as it is in the full phase space, allowing us to study the exponential part of the divergence more easily." On the shape: "The secular divergence has two distinct segments. In the latter segment the divergence is dominated by an exponential with a timescale of about 4 million years. The initial segment is apparently dominated by a smaller exponent with a time scale of about 12 million years." On the outer planets: "the divergences appear to be dominated by a 12-million-year exponential component, with evidence of the 4-million-year component appearing in only the last 5 million years", and "A viable interpretation is that there are two distinct mechanisms generating exponential divergence that are simultaneously operating." Their model has an approximate relativity; mine has none.
  • The number before them. The same paper: "Laskar (2) found numerical evidence of the chaotic evolution of the solar system excluding Pluto, with a time scale for exponential divergence of only about 5 million years." That is Laskar (1989), which neither the reader nor I obtained. Laskar's later account (2012): "An error of 15 meters in the initial position of the Earth gives rise to an error of about 150 meters after 10 Ma, but the same error becomes 150 million km after 100 Ma." Fifteen metres is also the size of the push in Catalpa's runs, given to Mercury; Catalpa's README does not say where its figure came from.
  • The spread. Mogavero, Hoang and Laskar (2023), over many nearby solutions of an averaged model of the inner planets with relativity: "At 5 Gyr, the probability density function (PDF) of the Lyapunov time peaks at around 4 Myr, it decays very fast below 2 Myr, while its 99th percentile reaches 10 Myr". Mogavero and Laskar (2021), of their own plot of this quantity over many solutions: "For times shorter than 100 Myr, Fig. 3 shows that the distribution of the FT-MLE only reflects the choice of different initial tangent vectors d0, around essentially the same reference trajectory, i.e. the nominal one. Therefore, this part of the plot can be discarded because of its non-asymptotic character." My whole run is inside that part. And: "its computation acquires full physical significance only for an ensemble of trajectories." Murray and Holman (1999), of the outer planets: "it is difficult to measure Lyapunov times with an accuracy much better than a factor of 2." Rein and Tamayo (2018), with relativity and 24 runs of 300 million years: "a Lyapunov time of 6.5 Myr". Theirs is from another integrator of the same package (JANUS, at a step of 0.6 days) and from the difference in Mercury's averaged eccentricity between pairs of runs, a slow variable chosen to "avoid issues with the quickly growing phase differences".
  • The outer planets. Sussman and Wisdom (1992), for the outer planets alone: "only 5 million years" with the map and "about 19 million years" with another integrator, and a time that "varied from about 3 million years to about 30 million years" with the step. Murray and Holman (1999): "We find a Lyapunov time of about 7 million years". Hayes (2007): "Lyapunov times ranging from about 5 millions years upwards to infinity". The three Sussman and Wisdom phrases are as the reader transcribed them from the scan; I have not read those two pages on the images.
  • My model. Newtonian, eight planets, a step of 8 days: that of Batygin and Laughlin (2008), who write that "An 8-day time-step (Ito & Tanikawa, 2002) has been shown sufficient for integration of the Solar System as long as Mercury's eccentricity does not rise significantly above its e ∼ 0.2 current value", and that "neglecting general relativity cuts down the distance between g1 and g5 by some 39%", the two rates whose nearness makes Mercury unstable over billions of years. Their Table 1 gives a time for each planet in this model, measured over a single million years: Mercury 1.37, Venus 7.22, Earth 4.82, Mars 4.50, Jupiter 8.37, Saturn 6.37, Uranus 7.47, Neptune 6.68. Laskar (2008): "The contribution of GR is thus essential in order to ensure the relative stability of Mercury." On this time itself I know of one comparison: Zeebe (2015), of a single pair of runs without relativity, "it is clear that it takes more than 160 Myr" for the two to part.
  • The step. Contested. Hernandez, Zeebe and Hadden (2022): "We argue that all of the past studies we consider in this work are affected by numerical chaos to different degrees"; their rule, a sixteenth of the time of closest passage, is 3.59 days for an orbit as eccentric as Mercury's is now. Abbot and others (2023) find about nine days "sufficient to ensure convergence" for their tests, which are of Mercury's instability and not of this time. My step is within a part in two thousand of one eleventh of Mercury's year (taken as 87.969 days, from memory); I do not know that it matters.
  • The slow stretch. Zeebe (2015): the early growth "is usually dominated by polynomial growth and may increase linearly with time in a log-log plot". That is a power of time, which is what one of my two fits to the stretch from 5 to 30 million years is.
  • MEGNO. For regular motion it "converges to a finite value, Y(t) → 2" (Rein and Tamayo 2015); for steady exponential growth it rises as time over twice the e-folding time (Hinse and others 2010: "In the case of chaotic initial conditions, ⟨Y⟩(t) = γt/2 for t → ∞"). So a value near 2 at 60 million years and near 5 at 100 says the fast growth had only lately begun to show in the whole vector. The inner planets' first burst, from 30 to 40 million years, does not show in the whole vector at all.
  • Not obtained: Laskar (1989, 1990), Laskar and Gastineau (2009), Zeebe and Lourens (2019) and others behind journals' doors; what I say of them rests on the papers above. Of the papers fetched, the reader read the 1992 one through and the rest round the passages it searched for.

The field's words for these things

What is measured here is a finite-time maximum Lyapunov exponent of one trajectory, and its inverse, the Lyapunov time. "Slow variables" are secular variables, and the chaos is secular chaos, tied in the papers to secular resonances among the inner planets. "Steady drift" is the quasi-periodic, or polynomial, part of the divergence. "Bursts" are intermittency. The unstable direction is the leading Lyapunov vector. A step too coarse for Mercury's closest approach to the Sun gives numerical chaos.
