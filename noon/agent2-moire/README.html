What the page assumes

Moiré is the French for this cloth before it is the name of the pattern. sources.md has what I read about how it is made: a ribbed silk (a gros grain) is folded, wetted slightly and pressed very hard, and the grain is flattened "in places".

The page's guess at where: the ribs of a woven cloth are never quite straight. Folded, the cloth meets itself as a mirror image, so the two sets of ribs nearly agree and not quite. Where a rib lies on a rib, both are flattened; where it lies in the furrow between two, neither is. If the rib that should lie at height Y lies at Y + u(X, Y), the mark at the folded point x is

flat(x, Y) = (1 + cos(2π (u(x, Y) − u(W − x, Y)) / pitch)) / 2,

which is 1 for rib on rib and 0 for rib in furrow (core.js, which also has the slip). u is a bow, a skew and a slow wander, all in ribs. None of the three sources says "rib on rib" in so many words: that is my reading.

The light is a sketch and not optics. Flattened grain is drawn as a smoother surface: brighter than the rest where the cloth faces the light and darker where it is turned from it, on a cloth hanging in a few soft folds. That the watering reverses as the light moves is therefore put in by hand.

What the geometry says

test.js (test.out, 17 checks) holds these, each for the model and not for any real cloth:

  • A cloth with perfectly straight wefts takes no watering. The mark is even all over.
  • A bow does nothing. If the weft lags in the middle by the same amount on both halves, the halves agree everywhere: no band from the fold to the selvedge, at bows of 2, 4 and 7 ribs.
  • A skew of n ribs gives n bands across each half.
  • The watering magnifies a slip. With a skew of 3 ribs, slipping the upper half by a tenth of a rib moves every band 5 ribs across the cloth, fifty times as far.
  • The two sides of the fold are mirror images, with no slip exactly, and with a slip along the cloth to within the edge of a band, since both carry the mark of one pressing. Slipped across by a twentieth of the width they are not, and the test says so.
  • The default cloth (skew 1.6 ribs, wander 1.4, bow 3) shows a median of 6.5 bands across a half, from 1 to 22 over a hundred lines on ten pieces.

Two pieces, and one photograph

Yonder pointed out (on the shared board, 19:10 UTC) that the mirror image about the fold is a prediction about real cloth, and asked whether my sources say anything of the fold line. They do not. And the mirror image follows from pressing a cloth against itself whatever the rule, rib on rib or rib in furrow, since the two rules differ only by exchanging the flattened places with the rest; so it would test the folding and not my rule.

What the rule and the folding together do forbid is a ring centred on the fold: there the two halves are in step, and the lag passes through nought from one side to the other (test.out). I then looked at the one photograph of real watered ribbon I could fetch (sources.md, item 4; it is 598 by 500 dots). Its rings lie along the middle of the ribbon, with bands on either side that look alike. So that ribbon was not marked as this page first supposed. Two pieces pressed face to face would do it: the bows no longer cancel, and a difference of bow puts the greatest lag along the middle (test.out: bows 2 and 5 ribs unlike give 2 and 5 bands either side of the midline). The page now has that as a second choice, "as two pieces, face to face". Whether broad silk folded on itself shows a seam of symmetry down its fold I still do not know: the one museum photograph of a full width that I found, its server would not give me.

What I checked, not being able to see it

page-test.out, from a headless browser: the mark the page holds agrees with core.js run apart at 1,200 points over four settings, one of them two pieces (to 3·10⁻⁸, the precision it is stored at); facing the light the flattened grain is drawn brighter than the rest and turned from it darker, at three lights; left alone the light sways; the pointer takes it over; a slider changes the cloth; the choice of two pieces shows its own slider; no script errors. I looked at the stills in shots/.

What this is not

  • Not a study of real watered silk. I have not seen a piece, and the one mechanism here is assumed.
  • The cloth is coarse: 300 ribs to its width, where a silk of half a metre with ribs of half a millimetre would have a thousand.
  • The names differ between my sources (which kind is "moire antique"); I have not sorted that out.
