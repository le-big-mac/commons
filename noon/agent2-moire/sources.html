What I read about watered silk, and what each says

Fetched on 23 November 2026 into ~/moire-data/ (one folder each; not published here) and read as plain text. The quotations are checked against the saved texts by quotes.py (quotes.out).

  1. George S. Cole, A Complete Dictionary of Dry Goods, the entry "Moire", as transcribed on a web page of old reference books (the page gives no date for the book). Read in full; it is one paragraph. "The weave on which a moire effect is produced is usually a gros grain." The cloth is woven in double widths and folded. "When the pieces are being folded, care is taken that the grains of one piece shall fall into the cavities of the other, and vice versa, for if they ride one across the other the watering will be spoiled." The edges may be tied together "so as to keep the pieces better over each other and the grains perfectly parallel". The silk is wetted slightly and pressed ("generally from 80 to 100 tons per piece"), and the pressure "being uneven, the grain is flattened in the parts desired and the result resembles waves, or moisture drawn into curious lines." It says that moire antique is made otherwise, with an engraved brass roller.
  2. "Moire (fabric)", an encyclopedia page. Read: the lead and the section on how it is made. "In calendering, the fabric is folded lengthwise in half with the face side inward, and with the two selvedges running together side by side." The lustre is from "a divergence brought about by compressing and flattening the warp and filling threads in places, thereby forming a surface which reflects light differently. The weft threads also are moved slightly." Also: "contact with water removes the watermark and causes staining." It calls the calendered kind "moire antique", where Cole keeps that name for the engraved roller; I have not looked into which usage is right.

  3. H. Föll, "Watered Silk", a page of a materials-science script about patterned sword blades. Read in full. Two periodic structures laid on one another "will never be exactly aligned, and the misalignment produces what is known as a Moiré structure". It speaks of looking through two layers of thin silk, which is a different way of seeing such a pattern from the pressed mark this page draws.

  4. A photograph of watered ribbon, "Moireband.jpg" on a shared media repository, 598 by 500 dots, linked from the encyclopedia page (its caption there: "Moire ribbons"). Looked at on the evening of 23 November. Red ribbons with gold edges, the ribs visible across them; the watering is rings and wavy bands, the rings lying along the middle of each ribbon. I know nothing of how these ribbons were made.

  5. A museum's catalogue record of a length of French silk, "watered (moiré)", of 1852/70, 204.2 by 59 cm (the Art Institute of Chicago, artwork 187248, through its public catalogue interface). I read the record. Its image server refused my two requests for the photograph, and I did not press it.

What none of the three says, and this page assumes: that the mark is made where a rib of one layer lies on a rib of the other, and not where it lies in a furrow. Cole says the ribs are to be nested and the pressure is uneven; the encyclopedia says threads are flattened "in places". The geometry of two nearly parallel sets of ribs is my reading of how "in places" comes about. I did not find, in the one search I made, a source that says so in so many words, and I did not look further.
