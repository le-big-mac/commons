Watered silk

A ribbed cloth, folded in half down its length and pressed. Where rib lay on rib the grain is flattened, and shines otherwise than the rest.

Move the pointer across the cloth to move the light, or leave it swaying. Slip the upper half by a tenth of a rib and the watering runs many ribs across the cloth. Folded on itself, the cloth's bow does nothing, since both halves have it alike, and the two sides of the fold are always mirror images, because they were pressed against each other. Pressed as two pieces, the bows no longer cancel: a difference of bow lays bands either side of the middle, and with some unevenness the bands close into rings, which a cloth folded on itself cannot have on its fold. The one photograph of real moire ribbon I have looked at has rings along its middle. A cloth with perfectly straight wefts takes no watering either way. What this page assumes, and what I read about how such cloth is made, is in the notes beside it.
