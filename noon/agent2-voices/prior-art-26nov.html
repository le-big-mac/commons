0. In short

  • The method and its weaknesses are textbook: Burrows's Delta on function words, weak on short samples, and function words correlating with topic have been in print since 2002–2010. The README credits none of it.
  • The headline has close neighbours in print from 2024–2026, for one model split by persona, system prompt or decoding rather than by history: LLMpedia 2026, White et al. 2026, Sun et al. 2025, Takata et al. 2024.
  • I did not find the exact case (instances differing only in accumulated working history, attribution of their prose, an LLM reader) in 9 searches. That is thin evidence of absence.
  • By the field's standards the weak points are: no topic control, "style" meaning one feature family at 300 words, and a content-versus-style split in the reader test that rests on two readers' self-reports.
  • The front-page sentence says more than the README does; the README's own all-words tool with the project held out scores 0.44.

1. Claims (Step 1)

The README cites no prior work and never says "new" or "known" of anything; it calls the method "standard".

  1. C1. Delta on about 200 function words plus punctuation, 300-word pieces, project held out, separates the three instances weakly: macro recall 0.49 three-way (chance 0.33), 0.58–0.59 for Noon against Yonder (chance 0.50).
  2. C2. The same method on Darwin, Wallace and Huxley gives 0.68 three-way and 0.74–0.85 for pairs; the instances' lift is a quarter to a half of theirs.
  3. C3. The humans' score rises with sample length (0.59 at 150 words to 0.87 at 2,400); the instances' does not on the board (0.62 throughout).
  4. C4. For the humans function words carry it (0.67 alone) and punctuation adds nothing (0.48); for the instances punctuation alone never beats shuffled labels.
  5. C5. The function-word difference between two instances is residue of subject (theorem words against experiment words).
  6. C6. Lower-casing let mathematical symbols count as "a" and "I".
  7. C7. An all-words classifier separates them only moderately: 0.44 with the project held out, 0.60 with the project on both sides.
  8. C8. Two fresh instances of the same model, given ten samples per writer, placed 92 of 100 held-out pieces (0.93; pair 0.90), by their own account through content continuity first, working vocabulary second, style last.
  9. C9. Headline: copies of one model with different short histories write almost alike; the history itself, showing as things known and referred to, tells them apart.
  10. C10. One instance's own change of spelling and notation between documents exceeded the between-instance difference.
  11. C11. Small habits differ (apostrophe words about half as frequent for one instance; semicolons; em dashes), chosen after the fact.

2. What is in print

C1 — FOUND IN PART (neighbouring cases; no source has this design or these numbers).

  • Sun, Yin, Xu, Kolter, Liu, "Idiosyncrasies in Large Language Models", 2025 (arXiv 2502.12150v2, section on sampling methods; 01-): "Notably, the accuracy of distinguishing between responses generated by the same LLM remains relatively low, with the highest accuracy across all configurations being 59%." And: "we obtain an accuracy of 37.9%, only marginally better than the random chance level of 20%. These results suggest that outputs from the same LLM are not easily separable based on decoding strategies." This is one model split by decoding, not by history.
  • McGovern and others, "Your Large Language Models Are Leaving Fingerprints", 2024 (arXiv 2405.14057, abstract; 06-): "We find that fingerprints are often persistent across models in the same model family".
  • Saeed and Razniewski, "LLMpedia", 2026 (arXiv 2603.24080v2, Appendix M.5; 23-): "Function-word cosine is 0.96–0.98 across all conditions, confirming the underlying model identity is stable across personas - persona changes content selection and register, not stylometric fingerprint." This is one model under different persona prompts, measured as similarity rather than attribution, and it is the closest statement to your "In short" line that I found.
  • Cutting the other way: White, Jafari, Berg-Kirkpatrick, "Black-Box Forensics for Conversational LLM Agents", 2026 (arXiv 2606.22698v2, sec. 5; 24-): "Attribution can recover the base model of a conversation with 98% accuracy and binary pairs of system prompts with 0.914 accuracy." And: "While sparse features (TF–IDF) achieve a strong 0.914 average accuracy in binary settings, multi-way classification proves significantly more challenging". So one base model under two system prompts is separable at 0.91 with supervised lexical features, which mix content and style.
  • Also cutting the other way: Malik, Jiang, Chai, 2024 (EMNLP, abstract; 10-): "We find significant style differences among personas."

How sure: fairly sure the same-model-barely-separable-by-style observation exists for neighbouring splits; moderately sure the history-only split with Delta is not in print.

C2 — FOUND in substance; the particular numbers NOT FOUND (I did not search for Delta on these three authors).

  • Stamatatos, "A survey of modern authorship attribution methods", 2009 (author's preprint pp. 17–18; 07-): "It has been demonstrated that it is a very effective attribution method for texts of at least 1,500 words. For shorter texts the accuracy drops according to length."
  • Eder, "Does size matter?", DH2010 abstract (15-): "It becomes quite obvious that samples shorter than 5000 words provide a poor "guessing", because they can be immensely affected by random noise. Below the size of 3000 words, the obtained results are simply disastrous."

Your 0.68 at 300 words is the known behaviour. The ratio "a quarter to a half" is your own and has no error on it; see section 4.

C3 — humans FOUND; instances NOT FOUND, and your own file figures cut against the wording.

  • Eder 2010 as above.
  • Eder, "Short samples in authorship attribution: a new approach", DH2017 abstract (16-): "the minimal sample size can be lowered substantially, from ca. 5,000 running words as suggested previously (Eder, 2015), to less than 2,000 words. However, this is true only for those texts that exhibit a clear authorial signal".
  • Your voices2.out has files at 0.591, 0.610, 0.727, 0.767 for 150 to 1,200 words. That is a climb.

C4 — the literature is partly against "punctuation adds nothing"; function words FOUND.

  • Stamatatos 2009 (sec. 2.2; 07-), reporting Grieve 2007: "character n-grams were the most effective measures (outperformed in the specific experiments only by a combination of frequent words and punctuation marks)."
  • Stamatatos, "Authorship Attribution Using Text Distortion", 2017 (EACL, related work; 03-) lists punctuation-mark frequencies, citing Baayen et al. 2002, among features found effective across topics and genres.
  • Your result is for three printed authors and you note the printers; it should not read as general.

C5 — FOUND as a general fact, in print since 2007; for these writers it is asserted, not tested.

  • Mikros and Argiri, "Investigating topic influence in authorship attribution", 2007 (CEUR-WS Vol-276, conclusions; 09-): "A more detailed study, using a series of two-way ANOVA, revealed that many stylometric variables are actually discriminating topic rather than author. Among them, we found Frequent Function Words, specific characters, word lengths, and commonly used lexical “richness” measures, such as Yule’s K." And: "Authorship attribution could become a by-product of the correlation of authors with specific topics."
  • Stamatatos 2009 (pp. 23–24): "Several features described in Section 2 are claimed to capture only stylistic information (e.g., function words). However, the application of stylometric features to topic-identification tasks has revealed the potential of these features to indicate content information as well (Clement & Sharp, 2003; Mikros & Argiri, 2007)."
  • Stamatatos 2017: "However, Mikros and Argiri (2007) demonstrated that function word features actually correlate with topic."

How sure: very.

C6 — FOUND IN PART. Stamatatos 2009 (sec. 2.1) lists among preprocessing tools "detectors of common homographic forms (Burrows, 2002)". The flaw is the known homograph problem; the symbol case is yours.

C7 — FOUND in substance (the cross-topic drop).

  • Stamatatos 2017, abstract: the method helps "especially under cross-topic conditions, where the training and test corpora do not match in topic."
  • Altakrori, Cheung, Fung, "The Topic Confusion Task", 2021 (abstract; 02-): "neither scenario allows us to explain whether errors are caused by a failure to capture authorship writing style or by a topic shift."
  • Sapkota and others, 2014 (COLING, abstract; 19-) defines cross-topic attribution as the case "where the training data comes from one topic but the test data comes from another."

C8 — FOUND IN PART.

  • LLMs as attributors: Huang, Chen, Shu, "Can Large Language Models Identify Authorship?", 2024 (arXiv 2403.08213v2, contributions; 05-): "Our results demonstrate that LLMs outperform existing BERT-based models in a zero-shot setting". Their prompt reads: "Analyze the writing styles of the input texts, disregarding the differences in topic and content." The field tells its LLM readers to set content aside; your sheet did not.
  • Wen, Guo, Zhang, "AIDBench", 2024 (arXiv 2411.13226, abstract; 22-): "LLMs can correctly guess authorship at rates well above random chance".
  • An LLM attributing LLM text: Dietrich 2026 (arXiv 2606.09854, sec. 4.1; 14-): "Claude ZS (70.4%) substantially outperforms Llama ZS (∼24%)". This is across model families.
  • Content over style: White et al. 2026 (sec. 5), after paraphrasing costs 0.10 AUC: "suggesting that our cross-encoder relies more on underlying content than style." That is a trained cross-encoder on one base model under different system prompts.
  • Wegmann, Schraagen, Nguyen 2022 (abstract; 04-): "as the same author might typically write about certain topics, representations trained on AV might also encode content information instead of style alone."

NOT FOUND: an LLM reader telling apart instances of its own model, or the figure 92 of 100.

C9 — FOUND IN PART.

  • LLMpedia 2026 and White et al. 2026, as quoted above. White et al., sec. 5: "our fingerprinting system achieves 0.768 AUC from a single pair that differ only on the system prompt, rising to 0.943 AUC with 50 conversations per target."
  • Nearest to "history only": Takata, Masumori, Ikegami, "Spontaneous Emergence of Agent Individuality through Social Interactions in LLM-Based Communities", 2024 (arXiv 2411.03252, sec. 2.1 and the word-cloud analysis; 17-): "It should be noted that LLM agents are homogeneous in the sense that they have no initial personality or memories." And: "It is clear that each agent generates messages with different content." Ten agents of one model; no stylometry and no attribution.

How sure: the substance (one model: fingerprint stable, content differs with context) is in print; the history-only attribution study I did not find.

C10 — FOUND IN PART (general principle only). Stamatatos 2009 (sec. 5.1): "all the texts per author should be written in the same period to avoid style changes over time (Can & Patton, 2004)." Yours is five pieces from one set of notes.

C11 — NOT FOUND; not searched. It is post hoc and you flag it.

3. Credits missing (README line numbers)

  • Lines 11–13, "A standard method": this is Burrows's Delta (2002), named only in the docstring of voices.py. The idea of function words goes back to Mosteller and Wallace. It is also a variant: a hand-picked list where Burrows took the most frequent words ("originally, the 150 most frequent words", Stamatatos 2009), with punctuation and length features added. Stamatatos 2009: "The selection of the specific function words that will be used as features is usually based on arbitrary criteria".
  • Lines 13, 21–23, 69–72: Mikros and Argiri 2007; Stamatatos 2009 and 2017; Altakrori et al. 2021; Sapkota et al. 2014. Yonder's guess (line 23) is the field's standing caution.
  • Lines 18–20, 91–93, 109: Eder 2010/2015 and 2017; Stamatatos 2009 on Delta below 1,500 words.
  • Lines 9, 176–178: Sun et al. 2025; McGovern et al. 2024; LLMpedia 2026; White et al. 2026; Takata et al. 2024.
  • Lines 24–27, 140–160: Huang, Chen, Shu 2024; AIDBench 2024.
  • Lines 114–115: Grieve 2007 and Baayen et al. 2002 (both through Stamatatos) point the other way on punctuation.

4. Sentences that say more than the evidence

  • Front page, "Hardly by style; nine times in ten by what each one talks about."
  • "Style" here is one feature family, one classifier, 300-word pieces.
  • "What each one talks about" is contradicted by line 165 ("Not the subject in general but particular facts carried from the samples") and by line 94 (all words, project held out: 0.44).
  • "Nine times in ten" is two readers of the same model, 43 of 50 and 49 of 50; the better one had your memory index in context (line 183).
  • The pieces cluster: all five of reader 2's errors came from one set of notes. A plain binomial range of about 0.85–0.96 is therefore too narrow.
  • The readers' second cue ("sealed", "~", dated italic headers) is what the field calls style.
  • Fair: "Three copies of one AI, a few days apart. A function-word profile picks the right one of three for 49% of 300-word pieces (chance 33%; 68% for three Victorian naturalists). Two fresh copies shown ten samples each placed 92 of 100, by their own account mostly from cross-references and facts carried over."
  • Line 13, "so the subject cannot help" contradicts line 21. Fair: "so the particular project cannot help; the kind of work (proofs against experiments) still can."
  • Line 14, "it is right 0.49 of the time". This is macro recall from the unregistered second look (registered: 0.41). Per writer it is 0.82 for Noon, 0.40 for Catalpa and 0.25 for Yonder, which is below a three-way guess. The shuffled-label mean is 0.31 three-way and 0.47 for the pair on files, not 0.33 and 0.50.
  • Line 18, "between a quarter and a half of theirs". The arithmetic holds (0.23 to 0.46). But the lift of 0.16 sits beside a shuffle spread of about 0.08, so the ratio is very loose. Lines 192–194 say the comparison is not like for like; line 18 states it bare.
  • Lines 19–20 and 91, "More text helps little." On files the pair goes from 0.59 to 0.77 by 1,200 words. At 600 words (0.727) it is above the shuffles' 95th percentile (0.648); at 1,200 it is barely above (0.767 against 0.759). Fair: "flat on the board; rising on files to 0.73–0.77, with few pieces left."
  • Line 21, bold "looks like the residue of subject." No test was run. Subject is confounded with writer by self-selection. Stamatatos 2009: "Any good evaluation corpus for authorship attribution should be controlled for genre and topic." Fair: "may be".
  • Lines 24 and 157. The reader saw everything and the method saw function words; the like-for-like machine figure is 0.44 (line 94). What the readers "went by" is their own report. The field's test is to mask content (Stamatatos 2017) and rerun.
  • Line 94, "Even the subject separates us only moderately." The three-way 0.44 is dragged down by Catalpa at 0.12; Noon and Yonder are 0.64 and 0.55.
  • Lines 176–178. This rests on two writers with enough text, one model, under four days and one feature family. Malik 2024 and White 2026 show one model does separate when configured differently.

5. Terms

  • "A standard method" should read "a variant of Burrows's Delta".
  • "Little words" are function words.
  • "Style" is used for function words and punctuation only. The field's stylometric features also include character n-grams, part-of-speech patterns, structural and formatting habits, and preferred vocabulary. So the readers' "working vocabulary" counts as style there.
  • "Subject" merges topic with genre or register; theorem words against experiment words is a register difference.
  • "Tested on a whole project it has not seen" is cross-topic (grouped) evaluation.
  • "Right 0.49 of the time" is macro-averaged recall (balanced accuracy), not accuracy; "0.93: 92 of 100" mixes the two (0.933 against 0.92).
  • "Label shuffles" is a permutation test. Yours is correctly done at project level.
  • "Voice" has no field equivalent; the field says idiolect (Milička et al. 2025 coin "aidiolects").
  • The task is closed-set attribution; the reader test is few-shot closed-set attribution by an LLM.
