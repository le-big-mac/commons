Report of the school-mathematics reader on the three new paragraphs

I read the brief and /home/agent3/door/check-small/extract-root.md, nothing else. I could follow the three paragraphs in the main. One sentence stopped me outright, and one figure does not agree with a sum I was asked to do (20 marks, where I get 18).

1. What the three new paragraphs say

On every clock, however the m marks are placed, the bad ways of choosing the signs are at most 3.12/√m of all the ways, and fewer than 2/√m when m is odd. So with many marks nearly every way is good. Nothing that shrinks faster than "a number over √m" can hold, because marks all on one hour give about 0.8/√m.

It comes from clocks with an odd number of hours (1.56 and 1 there), doubled by the statement about all the ladders. Two fresh instances proved it on paper, building on the second proof of the night before; a third proved a part. Three others refereed and found no gap.

Not proved is 1/√m on odd clocks for an even number of marks from ten on. A search did not find the statement in print, though two special cases are; neither Lean nor any person has checked it.

2. The first new paragraph

What is now known. These are floors that hold for every clock and every placing, not the true shares. - 100 marks: at least 68 ways in 100 are good. - 101 marks: more than 80 in 100 are good. - 10,000 marks: at least 96 in 100 are good.

"3.12 divided by √m of all the ways", for 100 marks. √100 = 10 and 3.12 ÷ 10 = 0.312. So at most 31.2 ways in 100 are bad and at least 68.8 are good. On first reading I took the phrase wrongly, as 3.12 divided by "√m of all the ways"; the examples in the next sentence put it right.

From how many marks on it says more. - Odd numbers: 2/√m is under a half once √m is over 4, so from 17 marks on. This agrees with the text. - Even numbers: 3.12/√m is under three quarters once √m is over 4.16, that is m over 17.3, so from 18 marks on. With 18 marks, 3.12 ÷ 4.243 = 0.735, which leaves 26.5 in 100 good. The text says 20. Either 20 is a slip, or it is compared with something sharper than "the quarter" that the page does not give. I could not tell which.

"The rate cannot be bettered". I took "the rate" to mean the "divided by √m", not the number 3.12. The example: with all the marks on one hour, the weights sit at one point on every circle, so they balance only when pluses and minuses cancel. For an even m that is about 0.8/√m of the ways, about 8 in 100 for 100 marks. So no bound can fall faster than a number over √m.

Where I was left guessing. - What "rate" means, and whether 3.12 itself might still be lowered. The third paragraph suggests it might; this one does not say. - Why the bad ways on one hour are exactly the equal splits. I had to work it out. - Whether the "2/√m" for odd numbers is also the best rate. The example has no bad ways at all for an odd number. - The 20.

3. The second new paragraph

How 1.56 and 1 become 3.12 and 2. The paragraph beginning "It is enough to look at clocks with an odd number of hours" gives it, and I could follow it there: - The bad falls on any clock are at most twice the falls that balance at an odd divisor. - Those are the bad falls of the same marks wound back onto the clock of the odd part, with the same number of marks, so the same √m. - Hence twice 1.56 is 3.12 and twice 1 is 2.

The new paragraph says only "doubles what is found there". That is enough for someone who has read the earlier paragraph.

Who proved what. - The author: the question, the counts that suggested the square root, and three thoughts for a proof. - Two instances: proofs for every number of marks. Both stand on the second proof of the night before and add "a count of the pieces into which a divisor splits the marks". - The shorter proof: by hand, and gives 5.8. - The numbers 3.12 and 2: rest on sums computed by program. The 3.12 rests also on the night before's tables (up to 46 marks) and on two of its results on the divisors 3, 9, 27 and so on (beyond 46). - A third instance: a part only, 2/√m on clocks of a prime power or 2, 4, 8 … times one.

Who checked, and how. Three referees, one text each, in two looks. They found no gap, their recomputed numbers agree, and none of the 1.85, 89 and 5.2 million markings they counted goes against a bound.

What was not checked. - The last replacements have been read by no referee. - Lean has not checked it, and no person has read it.

What the paragraph does not say. - Whether the author has read the proofs. - Whether the referees checked how the earlier results are used. - Whether the referees' programs were their own. - How many marks the counted markings had.

How far I would trust it. I would trust the shape (a number over √m) fairly well. I would hold the exact 3.12 and 2 more loosely: they hang on programs and on a chain of earlier texts, and the two proofs share one base, so they are not two separate confirmations.

Two sentences too heavy at one reading.

  • "The shorter proof is by hand … 3, 9, 27 and so on." I could not tell:
  • which text gives 3.12 and 2;
  • whose "its tables" and "its results" are, since the paragraphs above give the 3, 9, 27 work to the first and third texts, yet both new proofs are said to stand on the second;
  • whether "by hand" covers only what the proof adds, since the second proof it stands on rests on computations.

4. The third new paragraph

Not proved. That on odd clocks the bad ways are at most 1/√m for an even number of marks from ten on. It is proved for odd numbers, and the worst the author knows for even numbers is about 0.94/√m.

Two things I had to guess here: - Why "ten". I guessed that for 4, 6 and 8 marks the exact shares of the night before (3 in 8, 11 in 32, 39 in 128) are already under 1/√m. I checked, and they are; the text does not say this. - What "the largest that I know" is. I took it as the largest share found in the author's own runs. The text does not say for which marking or how many marks.

In print, by the search's report. - The same rate for the case of exactly one mark on every hour (the paper of 2009). - For any one fixed clock, the statement with a number that grows with the number of divisors, from "the classical bound for a single divisor".

Here I stumbled: the classical bound named above is "3 in 8", with no √m in it. I had to suppose it has a √m form too.

What the statement adds. One number serves for every clock and every marking.
