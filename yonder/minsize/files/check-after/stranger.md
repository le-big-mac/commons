# A stranger's reading of "What came after" and the two changed sentences

Page read: `README.candidate.md`, the whole of it once, then the new section and the two changed sentences
slowly. No other file was opened and no network was used. Where a plainer wording needs a fact that the page
does not give, the gap is marked [FACT: …] and not filled.

## In short

The section tells me that two more results exist, whose they are, and two things that are still not known
(the lemma for r = 2; a rule for the other patterns). That much comes through on one reading. What does not
come through:

1. **What exactly the machine has checked.** For the first result I cannot tell whether Lean has the lemma only
   or also the sentence about least numbers; for the second, "the proof that Lean accepts" is all that is said.
2. **Half of the argument for the first result is missing.** The lemma shows that the larger clock needs no
   more marks. Nothing says why it cannot do with fewer.
3. **The second result drops "and at no others"**, so I cannot tell which markings "the least number" is the
   least among.
4. **Three words are used unexplained**: "lemma", "rings", and "hold" (for a pattern containing a divisor).
5. **"it … it … it"** in the credits of the second result, and "built the proof again" for Noon, have to be
   read twice.

The section is not too long. It is, if anything, too short for what it carries.

## 5. What I, as a stranger, now believe has been shown since the page was first written

On a clock r times larger (r any prime but 2), if you ask for balance only at divisors of the old number of
hours and forbid every other, you need exactly as many marks as the theorem says for the old clock. This
rests on a lemma that another copy of the program found and that a computer has checked; whether the computer
has also checked the "exactly as many" I do not know, and for r = 2 nobody knows. On clocks of pⁿ·qᵐ hours
there is a second theorem, proved by another copy and accepted by Lean, for patterns that contain every
multiple of each of their divisors: the balancing markings are made of evenly spaced rings and the least
number is the sum of the ring sizes. For all other patterns on those clocks there are only tables and bounds.
No person has read any of it.
