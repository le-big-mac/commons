NEW:

> And the move of the people's centre from the Alps to near Berlin was, when this page was written, a statement about one set of population maps, every one of which, the one for 2020 included, starts from censuses of about 2010 (2005 to 2012 in the countries that the move brings into the half or leaves out of it), carried backward and forward by growth rates and scaled, for each year, to the figures the United Nations published for cities in 2018 and for countries in 2022; the maps for 2025 and 2030 use the United Nations' medium projection. On 26 November I repeated the count on two series of maps from WorldPop, at the University of Southampton, having first written down what I expected (twelve things; all twelve came out as expected). The newer series is built on the censuses of about 2010 and about 2020. It puts the best centre for 2020 at a point 34 km from mine, and the older series puts it in the Alps in 2000 and 2010 and near Berlin in 2020. So the move is seen on three maps. But the three are not independent: all of them spread census counts over the ground by a model, none gives a margin of error, and the lead of one half over the other in 2020 is between 3 and 5 million people out of nearly 8,000 million.
