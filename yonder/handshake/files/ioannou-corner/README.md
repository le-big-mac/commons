**The question.** Ioannou (2014; I have the discussion paper of 2011) evolved error-prone automata in the repeated prisoner's
dilemma and found Always-Defect the winner in 22 of 30 runs at 4% error and 19 of 30 at 2%, and diverse history-dependent
machines at 1% and 0%. My study (populations of 100 started from all-defect, errors in the move only, 200,000 generations and more) found the most cooperation at 1 to 3% noise. The reader of the prior-art check guessed that
his set-up sits in a corner of my own map where I get all-defect too: a population of 30, ten of them newly made by mutation in every
generation, 500 generations, errors of two kinds. This run puts my machinery in that corner. It is not a replication of his
algorithm: selection is my study's, with no keeping of the best twenty, so that here the mutations fall on a third of the whole population, the best machines included, where his twenty best pass unchanged and only the ten newcomers are mutated; payoffs are exact where his are played out; games end at random after 200 rounds on average where his last exactly 200; mutations are my study's, which add and remove states, where his change on average one element of a fixed string; and machines
have up to eight states where his have exactly eight. (The sealed sheet names "no crossover" among the differences. That was a slip of mine: the discussion paper describes selection and mutation only, in its section 4 and Figure 3. I have not seen the published version.)

**What was done** (`corner.py`). The study's machines, mutations, exact payoffs and selection, with two things added:
errors in reading the other's move as well as in making one's own, and a start from 30 random eight-state machines as well
as from all-defect. Games last 200 rounds on average (after each round another follows with probability 0.995). Sixteen cells of 30 runs: two starts, errors of one kind or both (each kind at the stated rate), at 0, 1, 2 and 4%; at 0% the two kinds are one and the same setting, run twice with different seeds. Measured over the
last 100 generations of each run: the commonest machine (the one with the most individual-generations), and the share of cooperative moves.

**What came** (`corner_score.out`). In every one of the sixteen cells all-defect was the commonest machine in 30 runs of 30,
with 14 to 17% of moves cooperative (the mutants' doing, and at 4% partly all-defect's own slips). Commonest is not all: all-defect held about four-fifths of the population on average (77 to 82% by cell), and 22% in the least of the 480 runs. No run was led by the handshake, the three-state machine behind the hump in my study (it defects until the other has defected twice running, then cooperates until crossed).

**What it shows and does not.** In this corner my machinery gives all-defect whatever the error rate, 0% and 1% included. So
it gives the machine he found at 2% and 4%, but in every run where he had it in 19 and 22 of 30, and it gives the same at 0% and 1%, so the agreement owes nothing to the errors. The hump my study found did not appear here. It does not reproduce his
diverse winners at 1% and 0%, so the difference between his low-error result and his high-error one is not explained by
this run. No difference between his algorithm and mine was tested by it.

**After the fact** (`corner_after.py`, `corner_after.out`; not sealed; random start, errors of both kinds at 0% and 2%, 30
runs a cell, one ingredient changed at a time, then two and three together). Runs of 30 in which all-defect was the commonest machine, at 0% and at 2%:

| | 0% | 2% |
|---|---|---|
| the corner (30 machines, a third mutated, 500 generations) | 30 | 30 |
| one mutant a generation instead | 19 | 19 |
| 100 machines instead | 29 | 30 |
| 5,000 generations instead | 30 | 30 |
| 30 machines, one mutant a generation, 5,000 generations | 28 | 27 |
| 100 machines, one mutant a generation, 5,000 generations | 19 | 25 |

So in my machinery all-defect leads with either ingredient alone: with a third of the population mutated each generation it leads even among 100 machines (29 and 30 runs of 30), and among 30 machines with one mutant a generation it leads in 28 and 27 once the run is ten times as long. That heavy mutation favours all-defect is in Ioannou's paper for his own algorithm: his footnote 7 says that at two and three times his mutation rate "the machine Always-Defect is hugely favored". But "enough by itself" would say too much for the small population. The last row has neither ingredient and is itself led by all-defect in 19 and 25 runs of 30; against it the small population adds nine runs at 0% and two at 2%, and two runs in thirty is within chance. What the table shows clearly is the heavy mutation: 30 against 19 at each rate, among 30 machines over 500 generations. With one mutant a generation and only 500 generations, eleven runs of thirty are still led by something else,
which I took for the random start not yet worn off. A side run by the reader of this note (the same cell from the all-defect start; `check-scripts/check_placebo.out`; not sealed, checked by nobody) gave all-defect in 30 runs of 30 at 0% and in 24 at 2%. So that reading fits at 0%; at 2%, six runs of thirty were led by something else from the all-defect start too, and the random start is at most part of it. The last row is the nearest to my study's own settings (though with a random start, errors of both kinds and games twice as long), and there the handshake
leads three runs of thirty at 2% and none at 0%; the study's runs are forty to sixty times longer.

**A reader's side runs** (`check-scripts/`; made by the fresh instance that checked this note, after it had seen everything above; not sealed, and checked by nobody). All on the corner's machinery: 30 machines, 500 generations, random start, errors of both kinds, 30 runs a cell. (1) With selection switched off, so that the game plays no part, a one-state machine was still the commonest in 60 runs of 60 (all-defect in 31, all-cooperate in 29). Mutating a third of the whole population every generation puts one-state machines on top by itself, and the game only chooses which of the two. (2) With selection 3, 10 and 30 times as strong, all-defect still led in 21 to 26 runs of 30 at 0% and in 29 or 30 at 2%. (3) With selection done as section 4 of his paper describes it (the twenty best copied unchanged, ten newcomers copied from well-scoring machines and mutated once) and everything else as here, all-defect led in 6, 16, 18 and 29 runs of 30 at 0, 1, 2 and 4%, with mean payoffs 2.47, 1.86, 1.72 and 1.39 and cooperation falling from 71% to 14%. He has 19 of 30 at 2% and 22 of 30 at 4%, and average payoffs of about 2.85, 2.55, 1.95 and 1.45 by eye from his Figure 4. A second writing of the same run gave 1, 18, 21 and 29, and over 240 further runs at 0% all-defect led in one run in six. It is not his result at 1%, where he reports no all-defect winner. If this stands, the corner as I ran it differed from his set-up in what matters most, which is where the mutations fall; and with his way of selecting, my machinery gives a fall of cooperation with error much like his, and no hump at these four rates. It is one reader's unreviewed work and wants doing again under seal.

**The second sheet: his selection scheme, written from his section 4** (`expectations-2.md`; `elite.py`, `elite_run.json`,
`elite_score.out`; added after the reading). After the reader's side run (3) above I wrote the scheme myself from the paper,
without opening the reader's script. Thirty machines are kept as strings of exactly eight states. Each generation every machine
is scored against all thirty, its own twin included; the twenty best pass unchanged; ten newcomers are made, each the better of
a pair drawn with chances in proportion to score, with each of its 25 elements (eight moves, sixteen arrows, the start state)
changed with a 4% chance. 500 generations, a random start, errors of both kinds, 30 runs at each rate. Still not his: payoffs are exact where his are played out (one match of 200 rounds a pair, as far as the paper says), and games end at random after 200 rounds on average where his last exactly 200.

The paper gives the scheme a paragraph and a figure, and in six places I had to choose. (1) What a string is made of. The paper gives each element "a 4% independent chance of mutation, which implies an expectation of 1 element-mutation per string", so a string has 25 elements; that they are eight moves, sixteen arrows and the start state is my guess. (2) How many elements change. The text has each element changed with a 4% chance; Figure 3 says "switching one element at random". I followed the text, which is nearly word for word from Miller (1996), whose approach he says he uses, and Miller is plain that each place has its own chance. (3) How the pairs are drawn. The paper says only "with the probabilities biased by their scores". I took chances in proportion to the raw score; Miller draws in proportion to a normalised score (the score less the mean, over the standard deviation, plus 2, cut off at zero). (4) Which of a pair is copied. It is "the better performer" in the text and "the winner of the pair's match" in Figure 3; I took the one with the higher total score. (5) Ties for the twentieth place. The paper is silent. In `elite.py` the machine listed first wins and the twenty kept are listed first, so a newcomer never displaces a kept machine that scores exactly what it scores; and with exact payoffs a newcomer that behaves as a kept machine does scores exactly what that machine scores. With payoffs played out, as his are, such ties hardly arise once there are errors. (6) What makes a machine "the winner" of a run. The paper does not say. Mine is the commonest machine over the last 100 generations, as in the first run. None of the other readings was tried under seal.

He reports Always-Defect as the winner in 19 of 30 runs at 2% and 22 of 30 at 4%, and diverse history-dependent machines at 1% and 0%. My columns are all over the last 100 generations of a run, and the standard errors of my four mean payoffs are 0.07, 0.06, 0.12 and 0.08. The Figure 4 column is the end of his smoothed curves (the second reader measured 2.85, 2.54, 1.94 and 1.45 off the page image); over generations 400 to 500 his curves average about 2.80, 2.52, 1.89 and 1.45, which is the fairer thing to set beside mine.

Four of the eight sealed lines failed (D1, D2, D3 and D8; Brier 0.35, worse than always saying 50%). For D1 to D3 I had taken the reader's counts (6, 16, 18 and 29) for what the scheme gives, and my writing of it gave 0, 1, 9 and 20. D8 failed for another reason. Its line of reason says that no run led by the handshake "is reported by the reader"; the reader's report names none, but the reader's own output (`check-scripts/check_elitist.out`) lists the handshake as the leader in 7 runs of 30 at 1% and 7 at 2%. D8 would have failed on the reader's run as well. The two writings differ in the mutation, and in how ties for the twentieth place are broken (the reader's at random, mine in favour of the machine listed first). Mine changes elements of an eight-state string, as the paper's text describes, so about a third of newcomers (36%) are unchanged copies and changes in states a machine does not use are carried along. The reader's minimises each machine and applies my study's mutation once, which works only on the states a machine uses and can add or remove a state. It does not always change something: by the second reader's count 24, 44, 43 and 63% of its newcomers at the four error rates were the same machine as their parent, against 49, 53, 58 and 67% of mine. With the reader's kind of mutation put into my script and
nothing else changed (`elite_after.py`, `elite_after.out`; not sealed), the counts are 5, 18, 18 and 30, which is the reader's result within the scatter of thirty runs. So the difference between the two writings is the mutation, as far as that one test goes. The test cannot show a slip in what the two scripts share (the payoff routine, and the same reading of the paper where it is short), nor one in my string mutation, which it takes out; the second reader read that routine against the paper and found none. Nor is the difference in the unchanged copies: the reader's own run with 36% of newcomers left unchanged gave 8, 14, 18 and 30. What it is in the two kinds of mutation that does it, I have not found out.

What the second run shows, with the caution that it is one writing, by me, of a scheme the paper describes in a paragraph:

- With mutation done as his text has it and payoffs exact, my mean payoffs over the last hundred generations are within 0.15 of the end of his Figure 4's curves at every error rate, and all-defect takes over as the error rises, as in his paper. That is less than it looks. At 2% all-defect leads 9 runs of 30 against his 19, which is beyond chance (p = 0.02) if his winner is counted as mine is, so his "clear winner" at 2% is not reproduced. And earlier in the run my payoffs are above his at 1, 2 and 4%: about generation 50 they are 2.25, 2.09 and 1.64 against his 2.00, 1.71 and 1.34, which is two to three of my standard errors (his are not given). The table falls short of showing that my writing is his algorithm.
- There is no hump at these four rates. Cooperation falls at each step of the error rate (the first step, from 84% to 75%, is about two standard errors; no rate between 0% and 1% was run). The rise of cooperation with a little noise in my study does not appear here. This run does not say which of that study's differences the rise depends on: the study starts from all-defect, has a hundred machines, errors in the move only, another selection and another mutation, and runs at least four hundred times as long.
- At 1% error the three-state machine of my study (Fortress3 in print, by the prior-art note) leads more runs than any other machine (11 of 30, counting its three entry states, which are the same three states begun at each in turn), and at 2% it is second to all-defect (7 against 9, which thirty runs cannot tell apart). It is not peculiar to my kind of mutation: with the reader's kind it led 5 to 9 runs of 30 at 1% and 5 to 8 at 2%. His paper says of the winners at 1% and 0% only that they
  were diverse and history-dependent; whether this machine was among them it does not say.
- How a newcomer is mutated matters here as much as the error rate does: at 1% all-defect is the commonest machine in 1 run of 30 with one kind of mutation (the sealed run) and in 18 with the other (not sealed), which is far beyond chance, and the mean payoff is 2.56 with the one and 1.79 with the other.
