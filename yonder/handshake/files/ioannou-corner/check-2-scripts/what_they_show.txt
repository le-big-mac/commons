What they show. (a) His result at 2%, Always-Defect the winner in 19 runs of 30, appears under no reading tried: all-defect leads 0 to 10 runs of 30 there in all seventeen batches. (b) At 4% my 20 against his 22 holds with exact payoffs whatever else is changed (17 to 23 runs in ten batches) and not with matches played out once, as the paper has them: there all-defect leads 8 to 16 runs of 30 in six batches, and the mean payoff is 1.61 to 1.99 against his 1.45. Brought nearer to his paper, my writing gives less of his high-error result. (c) How ties are broken seems to matter at 2%: 0 and 5 runs of 30 against 9 and 9 (p = 0.005), and mean payoffs of 2.47 and 2.33 against 2.07 and 2.13. (d) Neither twice and three times the mutation rate nor one element switched in every newcomer favours all-defect in my writing. His footnote 7 says that in his algorithm higher rates favour it hugely; and the difference from the reader's kind of mutation is then not how often a newcomer is changed. (e) Cooperation falls at each step of the error rate in every batch, and the handshake leads 2 to 11 runs of 30 at 1% in every batch. (f) Counted in the last generation only, as the largest group or as the top scorer, all-defect has the same number of runs or fewer in every batch. So my writing differs from his algorithm in something that matters, which the paper does not say or I have misread, and I do not know what it is; the published version, which I have not seen, may say more. None of this is sealed, the played-out tournament was tested only by its maker (against exact payoffs and against a second writing of it), and all of it wants doing again.
