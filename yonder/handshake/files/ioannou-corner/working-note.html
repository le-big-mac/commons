The question. Ioannou (2014; I have the discussion paper of 2011) evolved error-prone automata in the repeated prisoner's dilemma and found Always-Defect the winner in 22 of 30 runs at 4% error and 19 of 30 at 2%, and diverse history-dependent machines at 1% and 0%. My study (populations of 100 started from all-defect, errors in the move only, 200,000 generations and more) found the most cooperation at 1 to 3% noise. The reader of the prior-art check guessed that his set-up sits in a corner of my own map where I get all-defect too: a population of 30, ten of them newly made by mutation in every generation, 500 generations, errors of two kinds. This run puts my machinery in that corner. It is not a replication of his algorithm: selection is my study's, with no keeping of the best twenty, so that here the mutations fall on a third of the whole population, the best machines included, where his twenty best pass unchanged and only the ten newcomers are mutated; payoffs are exact where his are played out; games end at random after 200 rounds on average where his last exactly 200; mutations are my study's, which add and remove states, where his change on average one element of a fixed string; and machines have up to eight states where his have exactly eight. (The sealed sheet names "no crossover" among the differences. That was a slip of mine: the discussion paper describes selection and mutation only, in its section 4 and Figure 3. I have not seen the published version.)

What was done (corner.py). The study's machines, mutations, exact payoffs and selection, with two things added: errors in reading the other's move as well as in making one's own, and a start from 30 random eight-state machines as well as from all-defect. Games last 200 rounds on average (after each round another follows with probability 0.995). Sixteen cells of 30 runs: two starts, errors of one kind or both (each kind at the stated rate), at 0, 1, 2 and 4%; at 0% the two kinds are one and the same setting, run twice with different seeds. Measured over the last 100 generations of each run: the commonest machine (the one with the most individual-generations), and the share of cooperative moves.

What came (corner_score.out). In every one of the sixteen cells all-defect was the commonest machine in 30 runs of 30, with 14 to 17% of moves cooperative (the mutants' doing, and at 4% partly all-defect's own slips). Commonest is not all: all-defect held about four-fifths of the population on average (77 to 82% by cell), and 22% in the least of the 480 runs. No run was led by the handshake, the three-state machine behind the hump in my study (it defects until the other has defected twice running, then cooperates until crossed).

What it shows and does not. In this corner my machinery gives all-defect whatever the error rate, 0% and 1% included. So it gives the machine he found at 2% and 4%, but in every run where he had it in 19 and 22 of 30, and it gives the same at 0% and 1%, so the agreement owes nothing to the errors. The hump my study found did not appear here. It does not reproduce his diverse winners at 1% and 0%, so the difference between his low-error result and his high-error one is not explained by this run. No difference between his algorithm and mine was tested by it.

The second sheet: his selection scheme, written from his section 4 (expectations-2.md; elite.py, elite_run.json, elite_score.out; added after the reading). After the reader's side run (3) above I wrote the scheme myself from the paper, without opening the reader's script. Thirty machines are kept as strings of exactly eight states. Each generation every machine is scored against all thirty, its own twin included; the twenty best pass unchanged; ten newcomers are made, each the better of a pair drawn with chances in proportion to score, with each of its 25 elements (eight moves, sixteen arrows, the start state) changed with a 4% chance. 500 generations, a random start, errors of both kinds, 30 runs at each rate. Still not his: payoffs are exact where his are played out (one match of 200 rounds a pair, as far as the paper says), and games end at random after 200 rounds on average where his last exactly 200.

The paper gives the scheme a paragraph and a figure, and in six places I had to choose. (1) What a string is made of. The paper gives each element "a 4% independent chance of mutation, which implies an expectation of 1 element-mutation per string", so a string has 25 elements; that they are eight moves, sixteen arrows and the start state is my guess. (2) How many elements change. The text has each element changed with a 4% chance; Figure 3 says "switching one element at random". I followed the text, which is nearly word for word from Miller (1996), whose approach he says he uses, and Miller is plain that each place has its own chance. (3) How the pairs are drawn. The paper says only "with the probabilities biased by their scores". I took chances in proportion to the raw score; Miller draws in proportion to a normalised score (the score less the mean, over the standard deviation, plus 2, cut off at zero). (4) Which of a pair is copied. It is "the better performer" in the text and "the winner of the pair's match" in Figure 3; I took the one with the higher total score. (5) Ties for the twentieth place. The paper is silent. In elite.py the machine listed first wins and the twenty kept are listed first, so a newcomer never displaces a kept machine that scores exactly what it scores; and with exact payoffs a newcomer that behaves as a kept machine does scores exactly what that machine scores. With payoffs played out, as his are, such ties hardly arise once there are errors. (6) What makes a machine "the winner" of a run. The paper does not say. Mine is the commonest machine over the last 100 generations, as in the first run. None of the other readings was tried under seal.

For D1 to D3 I had taken the reader's counts (6, 16, 18 and 29) for what the scheme gives, and my writing of it gave 0, 1, 9 and 20. D8 failed for another reason. Its line of reason says that no run led by the handshake "is reported by the reader"; the reader's report names none, but the reader's own output (check-scripts/check_elitist.out) lists the handshake as the leader in 7 runs of 30 at 1% and 7 at 2%. D8 would have failed on the reader's run as well. The two writings differ in the mutation, and in how ties for the twentieth place are broken (the reader's at random, mine in favour of the machine listed first). Mine changes elements of an eight-state string, as the paper's text describes, so about a third of newcomers (36%) are unchanged copies and changes in states a machine does not use are carried along. The reader's minimises each machine and applies my study's mutation once, which works only on the states a machine uses and can add or remove a state. It does not always change something: by the second reader's count 24, 44, 43 and 63% of its newcomers at the four error rates were the same machine as their parent, against 49, 53, 58 and 67% of mine. With the reader's kind of mutation put into my script and nothing else changed (elite_after.py, elite_after.out; not sealed), the counts are 5, 18, 18 and 30, which is the reader's result within the scatter of thirty runs. So the difference between the two writings is the mutation, as far as that one test goes. The test cannot show a slip in what the two scripts share (the payoff routine, and the same reading of the paper where it is short), nor one in my string mutation, which it takes out; the second reader read that routine against the paper and found none. Nor is the difference in the unchanged copies: the reader's own run with 36% of newcomers left unchanged gave 8, 14, 18 and 30. What it is in the two kinds of mutation that does it, I have not found out.

What the second run shows, with the caution that it is one writing, by me, of a scheme the paper describes in a paragraph:

  • With mutation done as his text has it and payoffs exact, my mean payoffs over the last hundred generations are within 0.15 of the end of his Figure 4's curves at every error rate, and all-defect takes over as the error rises, as in his paper. That is less than it looks. At 2% all-defect leads 9 runs of 30 against his 19, which is beyond chance (p = 0.02) if his winner is counted as mine is, so his "clear winner" at 2% is not reproduced. And earlier in the run my payoffs are above his at 1, 2 and 4%: about generation 50 they are 2.25, 2.09 and 1.64 against his 2.00, 1.71 and 1.34, which is two to three of my standard errors (his are not given). The table falls short of showing that my writing is his algorithm.
  • There is no hump at these four rates. Cooperation falls at each step of the error rate (the first step, from 84% to 75%, is about two standard errors; no rate between 0% and 1% was run). The rise of cooperation with a little noise in my study does not appear here. This run does not say which of that study's differences the rise depends on: the study starts from all-defect, has a hundred machines, errors in the move only, another selection and another mutation, and runs at least four hundred times as long.
  • At 1% error the three-state machine of my study (Fortress3 in print, by the prior-art note) leads more runs than any other machine (11 of 30, counting its three entry states, which are the same three states begun at each in turn), and at 2% it is second to all-defect (7 against 9, which thirty runs cannot tell apart). It is not peculiar to my kind of mutation: with the reader's kind it led 5 to 9 runs of 30 at 1% and 5 to 8 at 2%. His paper says of the winners at 1% and 0% only that they were diverse and history-dependent; whether this machine was among them it does not say.
  • How a newcomer is mutated matters here as much as the error rate does: at 1% all-defect is the commonest machine in 1 run of 30 with one kind of mutation (the sealed run) and in 18 with the other (not sealed), which is far beyond chance, and the mean payoff is 2.56 with the one and 1.79 with the other.
