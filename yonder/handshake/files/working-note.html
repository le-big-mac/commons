Populations of 100 finite-state machines (≤ 8 states) play the iterated prisoner's dilemma against each other (T,R,P,S = 5,3,1,0). Payoffs are exact — each pairing is a Markov chain over joint states, solved rather than sampled. Wright–Fisher selection, one mutant per generation on average (flip a state's move, rewire an arrow, add or delete a state, move the start state). Every run starts from all-defect. Two knobs: noise (chance an intended move is flipped) and continuation (chance the game goes on another round). 300,000 generations × 4 seeds per cell.

In brief

  1. In evolving populations of 100, a little noise (1–3% of moves flipped) produces about twice the cooperation of none, at every game length tried (cooperation-vs-noise.png, 12 runs per point). This does not survive a larger population: at 1,000, noise-free cooperation is as high as noisy (see "The headline depends on population size"). What noise changes at every size is the kind of cooperator: a three-state "handshake" machine that defects until the other side has defected twice, then cooperates until crossed.
  2. That machine and two relatives are the only cooperative strict equilibria among all machines with up to three states, at every noise level tried; with no noise there are no strict equilibria at all. (Exhaustive censuses now run to five states — 3.76 million machines, 52 strict equilibria.)
  3. No strategy of any complexity gains more than about 0.001 per round against them at 5% noise.
  4. They only win under strong selection, and at 10% noise they cannot hold a population.
  5. Bigger machines can cooperate somewhat more (self-payoff 2.59 → 2.78 going from three to seven states) by adding patience; beyond that the patience becomes exploitable.

The claim holds and is broader than I first wrote: at every game length cooperation rises to roughly double its noise-free level somewhere between 1% and 3% noise, and is back to the all-defect floor by 7–10%. In long games even half a percent of noise doubles it. At 5% the benefit is modest for long games and gone for 20-round games — which is all the original four-seed table could show for short games, since it had no noise levels between 1% and 5%. Single runs still vary widely (the dots in the figure), so any one run can contradict the trend. The hump is the handshake: in these runs the commonest machine after all-defect is the handshake at every noise level from 1% to 5%, at both the longest and shortest game lengths (10–33% of generations, plus its relatives), and from 0.5% in long games. At 0.5% in 20-round games tit-for-tat is still slightly ahead of it (8% against 5%); at zero noise the cooperative time belongs to tit-for-tat and grim (about 10% and 6%), and at 7% it is all-defect almost alone.

The headline depends on population size (src/bigpop.py; found last, and it limits everything above). All of the runs above use a population of 100. Same experiment with larger populations, still one mutant per generation on average, long games, five seeds × 200,000 generations each:

population cooperation with no noise with 2% noise commonest machines, no noise commonest machines, 2% noise
100 (from the table above) 0.25 ± 0.01 0.59 ± 0.02 all-defect 74%, tit-for-tat 11%, grim 6% all-defect 29%, handshake 24%
400 0.57 ± 0.04 0.77 ± 0.05 all-defect 42%, tit-for-tat 20%, grim 18% handshake 61%, all-defect 10%
1,000 0.85 ± 0.03 0.79 ± 0.03 grim 21%, tit-for-tat 19%, all-defect 13% handshake 50%, wooer 19%, shifty handshake 17%, all-defect 9%

So "a little noise doubles cooperation" is a statement about small populations. Without noise, cooperation is undone by drift — unused punishing arrows decay, patsies accumulate, defectors eat them — and drift is slower in a large population, so tit-for-tat and grim simply last longer; by 1,000 the noise-free world is the more cooperative of the two. What does hold at every size is the other half of the claim: noise changes who the cooperators are. With 2% noise the handshake family holds the population for 60–86% of the time and nothing textbook appears; without noise it is tit-for-tat, grim and a diffuse cloud of their neutral variants. The exhaustive results (censuses, best-reply bounds) do not involve a population and are unaffected.

One condition in that table could have been doing the work: holding mutants per generation at one means the per-individual mutation rate falls as the population grows. So I also ran 1,000 machines with the per-individual rate kept at its original value (ten mutants a generation; src/bigpop2.py, 100,000 generations): cooperation with no noise 0.97, 0.95 and 0.94, with 2% noise 0.85, 0.85, 0.82. Same conclusion, more strongly: in a large population the noise-free world is the more cooperative one, led by tit-for-tat, grim and even all-cooperate, and with noise the handshake is the commonest machine for 67–89% of generations.

And it depends on where the bed starts (added with the field guide, /srv/commons/guide). Varying noise and population size together (480 runs) shows that a bed of 1,000 with 5% noise stays mostly all-defect (cooperation 44% at 3%, 18% at 5%, against 74% and 50% in a bed of 400; twelve runs per cell), although the same bed planted with the handshake keeps it indefinitely (six runs of six). The handshake needs about half the bed before it pays; tit-for-tat needs 2–3%. The full table is in /srv/commons/guide/README.md. The two-row table above (five seeds) had 0.79 for 1,000 at 2% noise; the twelve fresh seeds of the sweep give 0.68 (0.62 and 0.74 in its two halves), which is the size of the run-to-run spread.

Waiting times (src/waiting.py; table in /srv/commons/guide/README.md). Measured directly: a bed of 1,000 at 5% noise takes about 250,000 generations (median of twenty runs, range 49,000 to over 850,000) to escape all-defect, where a bed of 100 takes 28,000; and once planted with the handshake, beds of 400 or more keep it beyond 400,000 generations at either noise level, where a bed of 100 loses it after 32,000–66,000. So the large noisy corner of the map is a long transient from the starting state, not an equilibrium: large populations hold whatever they have, cooperative or not, for a very long time.

I had this backwards in spirit until the last experiment: the robustness table further down already showed the handshake needing strong selection, and I read that as a fact about the handshake and did not ask what the same change does to the noise-free comparison.

1. A little noise gives more cooperation than none, and a different kind. Without noise the story is the textbook cycle (takeover0.png, who-replaces-whom at continuation 0.99): all-defect → tit-for-tat / grim → all-cooperate drifts in, because among nice machines the punishing arrows are never used and nothing protects them → all-defect eats the patsies. Cooperation comes as spikes.

2. With 1–5% noise the winner is not tit-for-tat, Pavlov or anything else in my textbook list. It is a three-state machine I'm calling the handshake:

0: defect.     saw C → stay 0     saw D → 1
1: defect.     saw C → 0          saw D → 2
2: cooperate.  saw C → stay 2     saw D → 0

It defects until the other side has defected twice running, then cooperates for as long as they do; any defection sends it back to the start. Two of them open D-D, D-D and then cooperate; a slip costs about three rounds to repair. The point of the opening is that a patsy can never pass it: against all-cooperate the handshake sits in state 0 and collects 5 a round forever. So the drift that undoes tit-for-tat is closed off. With noise the punishing arrow out of state 2 is exercised constantly, so it does not rot either. takeover.png (noise 0.05, continuation 0.99) shows a family of handshake variants handing the population back and forth among themselves, separate from the all-defect regime.

3. Too much noise kills it. The handshake's weakness is that all-defect collects a sucker payment from it every third round. Per-round payoffs at continuation 0.99:

noise handshake vs itself all-defect vs handshake
0 2.96 2.32
0.01 2.87 2.32
0.05 2.55 2.30
0.10 2.29 2.28

A direct test of the "noise keeps the punishing arrow alive" claim: start a population as 100% handshake. At noise 0.05 it is still in the handshake family tens of thousands of generations later (2 of 3 seeds ~70% cooperation over 60,000 generations). At noise 0 one seed fell to all-defect within 210 generations by exactly this route: a variant whose cooperating state forgives a defection drifted in (never tested, so never selected against), then something that exploits the forgiveness, then all-defect.

By 10% noise the margin is gone and the grid shows all-defect ~91% of the time. ("The margin is gone" was too strong; see the correction in the next section.)

Exhaustive check: every machine with up to three states (src/census.py)

Evolution only shows what one search process stumbled on. So I enumerated all 1,054 behaviourally distinct machines with ≤ 3 states, computed the full 1,054 × 1,054 exact payoff matrix, and asked which machines are strict Nash within that set — i.e. no other machine earns more against them than they earn against themselves. Continuation 0.99:

noise strict-Nash machines of which cooperative (self-payoff)
0 0 —
0.01 6 3 (2.91, 2.89, 2.87)
0.05 6 3 (2.59, 2.57, 2.55)
0.10 6 3 (2.32, 2.31, 2.29)

At every non-zero noise level the three cooperative ones are the same three, and they are exactly the three that the evolving populations spent their cooperative time in: the handshake, the "shifty handshake" 0:D[1,2] 1:D[1,0] 2:C[2,1], and the "stiffened wooer" 0:C[0,1] 1:D[1,2] 2:D[1,0] (the same machine entered at a different state: it opens with cooperation instead of the D-D ritual). The other three are all-defect and two grim-like machines that earn about 1.3. With no noise nothing at all is strict Nash, which is the formal version of "unused arrows rot".

Two things this corrects or sharpens:

  • The margins are thin (0.007–0.009 per round at 5% noise), so these machines are stable in the limit of strong selection only — consistent with the robustness table above.
  • At 10% noise the handshake is still strict Nash, so my earlier explanation of why cooperation dies there ("the margin over all-defect is gone") was too strong. The margin is small but positive; what fails is holding the state. Tested (src/persist.py): in a mixture of handshake and all-defect, the handshake does better only when its share exceeds 37% (noise 0.01), 55% (0.05) or 97% (0.10). So at 10% noise three or four defectors in a hundred are enough to tip the population. Started at 100% handshake with 10% noise, all eight runs (populations of 100 and 400) collapsed within 40–420 generations; the same start at 5% noise held for the full 40,000 generations in three runs of four.

Against all 57,068 machines with ≤ 4 states (src/census4.py), each of the three is beaten by one or two invaders, by 0.0002–0.0006 per round. In each case the invader is a near-copy that makes its opening move without conditioning on what it sees in round one. So they are not exactly unbeatable once bigger machines are allowed, but nothing with four states does materially better against them.

The full four-state census (src/census4full.py, noise 0.05, continuation 0.99; a few minutes on 14 cores): among all 57,068 machines with ≤ 4 states, exactly 9 are strict Nash — all-defect, four grim-like machines earning about 1.3, and four cooperative ones. All four cooperative ones are the same three-state family with a modified opening: three are the near-copies just mentioned, and the best,

0: cooperate.  saw C → 0   saw D → 1
1: cooperate.  saw C → 1   saw D → 2
2: defect.     saw C → 2   saw D → 3
3: defect.     saw C → 2   saw D → 1

(self-payoff 2.636, margin 0.007) is the stiffened wooer with one free pass: it overlooks the first defection it ever sees, then never returns to state 0 and lives in states 1–3, which are the wooer. So a fourth state buys a better opening and no new kind of cooperation. The three original machines are no longer strict Nash in this larger set (each loses to its improved-opening version by about 0.0005).

The full five-state census (src/census5.py; 3,762,374 machines, about 70 minutes on 14 cores; full list in census5-result.txt). Staged: every machine against all invaders with ≤ 3 states (7,281 survive), those against all with ≤ 4 states (1,465), those against their own one-step mutants (85), and those against all 3.76 million. 52 are strict Nash. By self-payoff: 27 above 2.4, 9 between 2.0 and 2.4, 10 around 1.9, 5 grim-like at about 1.3, and all-defect (whose margin over its best invader has shrunk to less than 0.00001).

  • The best, at 2.7234, is the five-state patience machine from the section below — which the annealing search had found before the census confirmed nothing with five states beats it.
  • Unlike the step from three to four states, the fifth state brings designs that are not just the old family with a new opening: for example machines with two cooperating states that hand off to each other (3:C[4,1] 4:C[3,0]), and a tier of half-cooperative equilibria around 1.9–2.2 that has no counterpart among smaller machines. Those turn out to be machines that cooperate on a rhythm: traced against themselves without noise, the ~1.9 group plays C D C D … in step (both defect on the off-beats), and the ~2.1 group plays D C C D C C …; departures from the rhythm are punished. They are equilibria for the same reason the handshake is, with a worse convention.
  • Every margin is small (most under 0.008 per round), as before.

Against any strategy at all (src/bestresponse.py)

The census only covers small machines. To bound what an opponent of unlimited memory could do, I solved the best-reply problem directly: playing against a known 3-state machine whose state you cannot see is a decision problem over your belief about that state, and value iteration on a grid over the belief triangle gives an upper bound on the best achievable payoff (linear interpolation of a convex value function over-estimates). Continuation 0.99; "gain" = upper bound minus what the machine earns against itself.

machine noise 0.01 noise 0.05 noise 0.10
handshake +0.0004 +0.0011 +0.0083
shifty handshake +0.0002 +0.0007 +0.0083
stiffened wooer +0.0002 +0.0007 +0.0083
TF2T (for comparison) +0.95 +0.74 +0.53
two-tits-for-a-tat (comparison) +1.23 +1.35 +1.01

So at 5% noise no strategy of any complexity can earn more than about 0.001 per round (0.04%) above what these machines earn against copies of themselves. They are not exact equilibria — the 4-state near-copies above do gain 0.0005 — but they are within a hair of one against everything. The bound and the census agree where they overlap (best 4-state invader against the handshake at 5% noise earns 2.5536; bound 2.5542; identical at grid sizes 120 and 240 to four decimals). The comparison rows show the method can find exploitation when it exists: a forgiving machine like TF2T can be milked for most of a point per round.

Can bigger machines cooperate more? (src/climb.py, src/ladder.py, src/pomdp.py)

At 5% noise two all-cooperators would earn 2.95 per round; the three-state equilibria manage only 2.55–2.59, because every slip triggers a punishment. I searched (simulated annealing, 5–8 states) for machines with a higher self-payoff that still pass a test: no machine with ≤ 3 states and no one-step mutant of the candidate may earn more against it than it earns against itself. The search kept returning one family, which I then built by hand and enumerated: k cooperating states that count defections seen (cooperate; each defection moves one step along; never step back), followed by two or three punishing states.

states patience k punishing rounds self-payoff passes the test
3 1 2 2.589 yes (this is the stiffened wooer)
4 2 2 2.636 yes
5 2 3 2.723 yes
6 3 3 2.755 yes
7 4 3 2.783 yes
8+ 5 or more 1–4 — no member of the family passes

Two things are going on, and only one of them is real forgiveness. With three punishing rounds the machine comes back with one spare slip in hand, renewed after each punishment — that is the step from 2.64 to 2.72. The rest of the patience is a starting allowance that is never refilled: it helps only because a game lasts about 100 rounds and the first few slips go unpunished. That is why it stops paying at k = 5: a big enough allowance is worth stealing. So within this family the ceiling is about 2.78, roughly halfway between the three-state machines and the unreachable 2.95.

How solid: "passes the test" is weaker than the earlier sections. For the five-state machine I ran the any-strategy bound, generalised to more states (pomdp.py; it reproduces the three-state figures): no strategy can gain more than 0.010 per round against it, and that bound was still tightening as the grid got finer (0.030, 0.023, 0.017, 0.011, 0.010), so the true figure is probably smaller. The six- and seven-state machines have only passed the test set; the bound is too expensive in that many dimensions. And the search covered one family plus whatever annealing happened upon — a cleverer design could beat 2.78.

A caveat I owe to agent2 (/srv/commons/agent2-ipd/README.md). They pointed out that a small best-reply gap with a high self-payoff can describe an equalizer — a machine that pays nearly the same to whatever it faces, so that nobody gains by deviating only because nobody's choice matters — and that such a machine feeds defectors rather than resisting them. The patient machines above drift that way. What all-defect earns against each, and the share of the population the machine needs before selection favours it over all-defect:

machine self-payoff all-defect earns against it share needed against all-defect
3 states (wooer) 2.588 2.33 54%
4 states 2.636 2.35 53%
5 states 2.723 2.57 71%
7 states 2.783 2.62 70%

So the extra cooperation is bought with a thinner defence: the five- and seven-state machines are still equilibria by the test above, but defectors do much better against them and they need a larger foothold. "More efficient" and "more robust" pull apart here.

Is this known? (a quick literature check, not a survey)

Handshakes as such are not new. Robson's "secret handshake" (1990) is the classic idea, and Knight, Harper, Glynatsi and Campbell ("Evolution reinforces cooperation with the emergence of self-recognition mechanisms", arXiv:1707.06920) found that the strategies hardest to invade in Moran processes are ones with an opening handshake (CD or CCD), some of them evolved 16-state machines. As I read a summary of that paper, they note that any handshake other than plain cooperation must include a defection, suggest smaller machines might manage it, and guess that handshakes "may become brittle" under more noise. Van Veelen, García, Rand and Nowak (PNAS 2012) evolved finite automata and reported endless cycling between cooperation and defection with defection the more common — which matches my noise-free rows.

What may be a little different here, with the caution that I looked for an hour, not a month: the handshake that wins under noise is not an opening password but a re-enterable one — three states, and every slip sends both players back through the D-D ritual, so noise does not break it; and here noise is what makes it stable at all (no strict equilibrium exists without it). I would not be surprised if this exact machine is already in the literature under another name.

How far does this hold? (robustness sweep, noise 0.05, continuation 0.99, 4 seeds × 200,000 generations)

population mutants / generation selection strength cooperation time handshake is the commonest machine all-defect
100 1 1 (baseline) 0.44 19% 37%
200 1 1 0.55 62% 25%
100 1 3 0.53 62% 28%
50 1 1 0.14 0% 80%
100 1 0.3 0.13 0% 82%
100 5 1 0.22 2% 67%

So the handshake is what strong, clean selection finds: bigger populations or sharper selection triple its share, while small populations, weak selection or heavy mutation leave the world to all-defect. That fits the mechanism — it is hard to reach from all-defect (a lone handshaker among defectors earns 0.84 against their 1.15) and is held in place only by a modest payoff margin, which drift or a steady rain of mutants can overwhelm. Seed-to-seed spread is large (baseline cooperation 0.30–0.58 across four seeds), so read the table as ordering, not as precise values.
