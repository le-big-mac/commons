# The handshake

*A note written while a simulation ran. The machine in it is real — it is the one the evolving
populations kept arriving at (see README.md). The village is not.*

In the atrium of the old exchange the traders had a custom that visitors found perturbing. Two
strangers meeting for the first time would each cheat the other. Not grandly — a short measure, a
shifty count of change — and then, having been cheated back, would do it once more. Only after the
second round of petty wrongness did they clasp hands, and from then on they dealt crisp and fair,
sometimes for a lifetime.

Visitors took it for a mispronounced sort of friendship, or for a punishment with the reason left
unstated. They were wrong on both counts. The traders remembered what had come before.

Before, the village had been uncaring: everyone welched on everyone, and everyone was poor. Then a
generation of reciprocal dealers came up — fair to the fair, hard on cheats — and the place grew
rich, and stayed rich long enough that nobody had cheated in living memory. So the hardness went.
Nobody decided to drop it; it simply was never used, and what is never used is not passed on. The
children of the reciprocal were merely kind. They were patsies, beneficently robed, heralding good
faiths to anyone who walked in from yonder — and what walked in ate them. The village was poor again
within a season. This happened more than once. The encores were the cruel part.

The custom that ended the cycle asked one thing of a newcomer: show me you can defect. Twice, so I
know it was not a slip. Someone who only ever conceded could not pass, and so could not be let in, and so
could not become the soft spot through which everything was lost. It was a strange thing to demand
as the price of trust — proof of the capacity for its opposite — and it was not free. The uncaring
could collect on it: cheat, be cheated, cheat, and on the third meeting find a hand held out and
take what was in it. A village of handshakers paid that toll to every wolf, one meeting in three,
and survived only so long as wolves earned less than neighbours did.

And it needed error. That was the part no one would have guessed. In a world where nobody ever
slipped, the clause *if they cheat you, begin again* would have gone the way of the old hardness —
unexercised, then unremembered. It was because hands shook and counts went wrong, a few times in a
hundred, that the clause was invoked every week, and stiffened by use, and kept. A little noise
was the whetstone. A lot of it, and nobody could tell a neighbour from a wolf, and the wolves won.

None of them could have told you this. They only knew that you cheat a stranger twice, and then
you don't.
