The Bed

A bed of small machines plays the prisoner's dilemma among themselves. Those that earn more leave more offspring; now and then one mutates. Nothing is scripted: what you see is being computed as you watch.

A hundred plants stand for the whole bed, grouped by kind. Each plant shows how its kind opens a game against its own twin when nothing slips: a leaf for cooperation, a thorn for defection, first round at the bottom. Flower colour marks the kind.

Things to try

Set slips to 0% and wait: cooperation arrives in brief spikes and is eaten each time.

Set slips to 1–3% with long games: after a while a kind called the handshake, or the same machine under one of its two other names, tends to take the bed and hold it for a long while, though not for good. It defects until the other has defected twice, then cooperates until crossed.

Set slips to 10%, or make games short: thorns.

Make the bed 1,000 with no slips: tit-for-tat and grim now last, and the bed is mostly leaves.

Make it 1,000 with 5% slips: thorns for as long as you care to watch. Then plant a handshake colony in the same bed and try to wait it out.

It can take some tens of thousands of generations for anything to happen. That is part of the result.

What is this?

A live version of an experiment written up on the page this came from. Payoffs between machines are exact (each pairing is a small Markov chain, solved rather than simulated); reproduction is a Wright–Fisher process with on average one mutant per generation whatever the size of the bed; machines have at most five states, where the study allowed eight. The bed opens with selection set to strong, twice the strength of the study's runs, which makes the take-over quicker and longer-lasting. The contrast between no slips and a few slips is a small-population effect: with a thousand machines instead of a hundred, the noise-free bed stays cooperative much longer (tit-for-tat and grim last), and what slips change is which kinds do the cooperating. The arithmetic in this page was checked against the original Python to six decimal places.

The kind this page calls the handshake is a known machine: Fortress3 of Ashlock and Ashlock (2006). The stiffened wooer is the two-period mutual punishment automaton of Iwasaki, Joe, Kandori, Obara and Yokoo (2012), which begins by cooperating; the handshake and the shifty handshake are that same machine begun at its other two states. Any of the three may be the one that takes the bed, and their plants differ only at the bottom: two thorns, one, or none.
