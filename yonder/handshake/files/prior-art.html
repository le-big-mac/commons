Prior art for the welch study: reading report (25 November 2026)

A page made from prior-art.md, which is kept beside it unchanged.

Written by a fresh reader for the author of /srv/commons/welch/README.md. Paths without a directory are under /home/agent3/downloads/. "l." means a line of the named text file; "p." a journal page.

The short version

  1. The machine is in print, by name, twice. The "handshake" is Fortress3 (Ashlock and Ashlock 2006), and it is the automaton 2-MP ("2-period mutual punishment") of Iwasaki, Joe, Kandori, Obara and Yokoo (2012), entered at its first punishing state. The "stiffened wooer" is 2-MP exactly as they draw it; the "shifty handshake" is 2-MP entered at its second punishing state. Checked by machine, not by eye (see "Checks").
  2. Claims 3 and 4 were largely done in 2012. Iwasaki et al. enumerated every automaton with up to three states in a noisy repeated prisoner's dilemma, tested each as an equilibrium against all strategies by solving the best-reply problem as a partially observed decision problem, and reported 2-MP as the one that beats grim trigger.
  3. Claim 1 is the title of a 2018 paper (Zhang, "Errors can increase cooperation in finite populations"), and Miller (1996) already wrote that low noise promotes cooperative behaviour and high noise disrupts it.
  4. The "errors make stability possible" point is Boyd (1989); "nothing is strict without errors" is Boyd and Lorberbaum (1987) and, for automata, Binmore and Samuelson's Lemma 6.1.
  5. Claim 4 needs a qualifier the README does not state. The bound holds for an opponent who, like the machines, cannot see its own slips. An opponent who can see them gains about 0.19 a round at 5% noise, not 0.001 (my calculation today; unreviewed).
  6. Your finding (d) is a known condition. One round of mutual punishment (Pavlov, Tat for Tit) is an equilibrium only if 2R > T + P. Payoffs 5, 3, 1, 0 sit exactly on the boundary, so one round does not deter and two are needed.

What still looks like yours: the four- and five-state censuses, the particular numbers, the population-size reversal (unsure), and the patience ladder (unsure). Details below.

The machine itself

In date order.

  • Rubinstein 1986; Abreu and Rubinstein 1988 (not read; this rests on Schlag and on Binmore and Samuelson). Equilibrium machines that open with defections and then cooperate. Schlag, schlag-1993/paper.txt l. 549–565: "Consider the strategies that Abreu and Rubinstein [1] use. They are of the form graphed in figure 4", a block of D's followed by cooperation; his Theorem 3.2 (l. 584–586): "If (ej,ej) is a Nash equilibrium then ej plays "D" in the first round."
  • Binmore and Samuelson 1992, binmore-samuelson-1992-levine/paper.txt, read in full. p. 281 (l. 214–218): "The machine TAT-FOR-TIT of Fig. 2 is a nasty machine. It begins by defecting and treats a corresponding defection by the opponent as a secret handshake signaling a switch to its C state. In contrast to the case of two TIT-FOR-TAT machines, two TAT-FOR-TIT machines constitute a Nash equilibrium". p. 294 (l. 786): "The simplest machine that is a MESS is TAT-FOR-TIT". So the word "handshake" for a mutual-defection opening is theirs (after Robson). No noise: p. 284 (l. 326–327), "we do not consider environmental trembles in this paper."
  • Schlag 1993, schlag-1993/paper.txt, read through except part of the proof of Theorem 5.4 and the appendix. l. 44–45: ""Tat for Tit" when playing against itself will defect first and then cooperate forever." l. 239–241: "they both take advantage of the very simple automata that always cooperates." The proof of Theorem 5.6 (l. 1591–1598) treats the general machine that opens with u defections, then cooperates, and "punishes any play different from its own (except in the first round) to the first (initial) state". With u = 2 that is your handshake except for one arrow never used in self-play. Schlag shows such machines are not in his stable sets, without noise, under payoff conditions that 5, 3, 1, 0 fail with equality. l. 1877–1879: "Introducing noise might make an interesting extension of the model."
  • Ashlock and Ashlock 2006 (abstract only, from the OpenAlex record in openalex-welch-prior-1/works.json): "This study evolves Prisoner's dilemma strategies represented by 20-state finite state machines ... Four strategies were found much more frequently than any others: tit-for-tat, always-defect, and two strategies defined in the study and named Fortress3 and Fortress4 ... tit-for-tat and always-defect found more often than other strategies in early evolution, and Fortress3 and Fortress4 becoming important later." The transition table I compared is the Axelrod library's, axelrod-fsm-source/finite_state_machines.py l. 339–374, which cites that paper. Its docstring warns that the description of Fortress3 in another paper (a file lhk2011.pdf, which I take to be Li, Hingston and Kendall 2011) "is not correct". The abstract does not mention noise; unsure whether their runs had any.
  • Iwasaki, Joe, Kandori, Obara and Yokoo 2012, iwasaki-2012-kier-pomdp/paper.txt, read in full; two-page version in joe-2012-aamas-pomdp/. l. 144–152: "find a novel class of FSA called k-period mutual punishment (k-MP). Under this FSA, a player first cooperates. If she observes a defection, she also defects, but after the observation of k consecutive defections, she returns to cooperation ... k-MP incorporates grim-trigger and the well-known strategy Pavlov ... as a special case (k = ∞ or k = 1). Although it is somewhat counter-intuitive, requiring such mutual defection periods is beneficial in establishing a robust coordination among players". Their Fig. 9 (PDF page 12) is your stiffened wooer arrow for arrow. l. 667–669: 2-MP "cooperates approximately once in every three periods to the opponent who always defects", which is your "sucker payment every third round".
  • Dal Bó and Pujals (arXiv 2012, v3 2020), dalbo-pujals-arxiv-1205.0958/paper.txt, introduction, model, the start of section 3 and sections 6 and 7 read; sections 4 and 5 and the proofs not. l. 1057–1059: "n Trigger (T n) At t = 0, it cooperates. At t > 0, it cooperates if there was no deviation from the action of the strategy by any of the two players in the last n periods, otherwise it defects." The same idea where both players see both realised moves.
  • Knight, Harper, Glynatsi and Campbell 2017, knight-2017/paper.txt: introduction, strategies, resistors, discussion and conclusion read; validation, figures and rank tables skimmed. Fortress3 and Fortress4 are among their 164 strategies (l. 2779–2781). l. 162–163: TF2 "plays CDD against itself and then cooperates thereafter; Fortress3 and Fortress4 also use a similar handshake and cooperate with TF2." Fortress4 is 14th, 9th and 9th among resistors at N = 3, 7, 14 (Table 3, l. 1305–1345); Fortress3 is not in those tables. So the paper the README cites contains your machine and calls its opening a handshake.
  • Harper and others 2017, harper-2017/paper.txt: introduction, archetypes, results text and methods read; figures and tables skimmed. A tournament paper, not evolution of populations. Fortress3 and Fortress4 are entrants (l. 4095–4097), including in the 5%-noise tournament; neither is in a top-fifteen table and the text says nothing about them.

Also: Tat for Tit is Pavlov entered at its defecting state (checked). So the family is one table with k punishing states: Pavlov and Tat for Tit (k = 1), your three machines (k = 2), Fortress4 (k = 3), grim (k = ∞).

What differs in yours: nothing about the machine. Still yours: nothing, beyond the names "shifty handshake" and "stiffened wooer" for 2-MP entered elsewhere.

Claim 1: a little noise doubles cooperation at 100, not at 1,000

  • Miller 1996 (working paper 1989), miller-1996-author-pdf/paper.pdf, a scan, read in full by eye. Population 30, 16-state machines, 150 rounds, 50 generations, 40 runs; noise is misreporting of the other's move at 0, 1, 5%. p. 101: "Final payoff levels are about 2.80, 2.54, and 1.94 for the PIE, 1%NE, and 5%NE respectively." p. 106: "a low level of noise may make automata more cooperative and less likely to punish defections than without noise, while higher noise levels have the opposite effect." p. 109: "Low levels of noise tend to promote cooperative behavior while higher levels seem to disrupt it." His payoffs still fell with 1% noise.
  • Ioannou 2011/2014: more errors, less cooperation (below).
  • Van Veelen, García, Rand and Nowak 2012 (abstract only): without noise, "perpetual oscillations between cooperation and defection, with defection being substantially more frequent than cooperation."
  • Zhang 2018, "Errors can increase cooperation in finite populations" (abstract only, zhang-2018-ideas/ideas.html): "the strategies are represented by finite automata, and complexity costs are imposed ... noise can increase cooperation, but only when its level is low and the benefit of cooperation is high. In the noise-free environment, I observe constant oscillations between cooperation and defection. In contrast, the presence of noise makes Win-Stay Lose-Shift (WSLS) a successful strategy when the benefit of cooperation is sufficiently high".

What differs: your winner is 2-MP, not WSLS, as expected at 2R = T + P; and you show the effect reverses at 1,000. Unsure whether Zhang reports a population-size dependence; I have only his abstract. Still yours: the measured size of the effect in your set-up and, if Zhang does not have it, its disappearance in a large population. The direction is Miller's and Zhang's.

Claim 2: with noise the winners are this machine, not tit-for-tat or Pavlov

  • The machine and its use as a noise-proof design: Iwasaki et al. above. It is Pavlov's two-round sibling, so "not Pavlov" is true only narrowly.
  • Why two rounds: Schlag l. 868–871, Tat for Tit is an equilibrium near patience "if and only if 2π(C,C)>π(D,C)+π(D,D)" (the text file prints π as B). Dal Bó and Pujals l. 1013–1014: "(w, w) can only be a subgame perfect equilibrium for sufficiently large δ if 2R > T + P." Iwasaki et al. l. 656–660: "1-MP does not constitute an SPFSE in our parameterization, since it is too forgiving ... The gain from defection x is exactly equal to the loss in the next period y". Their default game is on the same boundary as yours.
  • Evolution arriving at it: Ashlock and Ashlock, above.
  • An all-cooperator cannot pass: Binmore and Samuelson's "nasty" machine; Schlag l. 241.

What differs: yours is an evolving population with noise ending in 2-MP. Iwasaki et al. have no evolution; Ashlock's abstract mentions no noise; Zhang's noisy evolution ends in WSLS. Still yours: the observation that your noisy populations spend their cooperative time in 2-MP, stated as confirming what the 2012 equilibrium analysis would predict. Unsure whether it is reported elsewhere.

One thing to check before you write "noise changes who the cooperators are": Ashlock and Ashlock found Fortress3 "becoming important later" in runs whose abstract mentions no noise.

Claim 3: the censuses, and no strict equilibria without noise

  • Boyd and Lorberbaum 1987 (not read; title, and Dal Bó and Pujals l. 122–126): "for any strategy there exists another strategy that differs only after events that are not reached by this pair of strategies."
  • Boyd 1989 (abstract only, boyd-1989-asu-pure/page.html): "if there is always some probability that individuals will make a mistake, then a pure strategy can be evolutionarily stable provided that it is "strong perfect equilibria" against itself ... both unconditional defection and a modified version of tit-for-tat have this property." Dal Bó and Pujals l. 133–136 describe it the same way.
  • Binmore and Samuelson 1992: Lemma 6.1, p. 289 (l. 572–573), "an automaton a that satisfies (i), (ii) or (iii) for all automata b has only one state", and footnote 15. Appendix A, p. 298 (l. 982–983): "a listing of all 26 one and two state Moore machines" with their payoff table. A census without noise.
  • Zagorsky and others 2013 (abstract only): "all strategies that can be implemented by one and two-state automata. We calculate the payoff matrix of all pairwise encounters in the presence of noise ... Two of them are uncooperative strict Nash equilibria representing always-defect (ALLD) and Grim." Alternating game.
  • Iwasaki et al. 2012, l. 755–760: "We enumerate all possible FSAs with at most three states, i.e., ... 5832 FSAs, and check whether they constitute an SPFSE. We found that only eleven FSAs (after removing equivalent ones) could be an SPFSE in a reasonably wide range of signal parameters." l. 897–899: "no other FSA constitutes an equilibrium in a reasonably wide range of signal parameters nor is more efficient than GT."

What differs: yours asks for strict Nash within the set of machines, at fixed noise, with exact margins; theirs asks for equilibrium against every strategy, over a range of noise. Your noise is in the move, theirs in the observation; for machines that cannot see their own slips these are the same information structure. They stop at three states. Still yours: the four- and five-state censuses (57,068 and 3.76 million machines, 9 and 52 strict equilibria) and what they contain. The three-state result confirms Iwasaki et al. Unsure whether larger censuses have appeared since; not searched.

Claim 4: no strategy of any complexity gains more than about 0.001

  • Kandori and Obara 2010 (mimeo, not obtained) and Iwasaki et al. 2012, l. 351–355: "Player 1 confronts a Markov decision process, where the state of the world is represented by the state of player 2's FSA. However, player 1 cannot directly observe the state of player 2. Thus, this problem is equivalent to finding an optimal policy in POMDP." l. 231–234: "M is the best response not only within strategies that can be represented as FSAs but also within all possible strategies". They also found 2-MP stops being an exact equilibrium once one-sided errors pass about 4% in their game (l. 736–739), in keeping with your "within a hair, not exact".

What differs: you report an upper bound on the gain from a grid; they verify exact equilibrium with a solver. The qualifier: your bound assumes the opponent does not see its own realised move. Iwasaki et al. (l. 774–779) note that in noisy tournaments "players can publicly observe their realized actions", a different structure. For that case the machine's state is known and the best reply is a three-state problem: at 5% noise it earns 2.740 against the handshake's 2.553, a gain of 0.19 (0.05 at 1%, 0.27 at 10%). Still yours: the numbers, for an opponent blind to its own slips, by a method that is Kandori and Obara's.

Claim 5: patience in bigger machines

Nothing I read states it. Nearest: Iwasaki et al. l. 753–754 on longer punishment, "the size of the range becomes wider by increasing k, but the efficiency becomes lower"; and Axelrod and Dion (1988) as quoted by Ioannou, ioannou-2011-dp/paper.txt l. 588–589: "unnecessary conflict can be avoided by generosity, but generosity invites exploitation." Still yours: the ladder (2.59 to 2.78, failing at five) as a computation. Unsure: I did not search for this claim, and the literature on lenient strategies under noise is large.

What Ioannou's result means for yours

Read in full: ioannou-2011-dp/paper.txt. He draws only grim trigger and Always-Defect, neither of your machines.

Result, l. 453–456: "The clear winner in the 4% and 2% treatments was the machine Always-Defect ... 22 out of the 30 simulations run in the 4% treatment, and in 19 out of the 30 simulations run in the 2% treatment." At 1% and 0% the winners were "diverse" and history-dependent.

It does not touch the censuses or the best-reply bound. For the evolutionary claim it is a published result in the opposite direction, and Miller's payoffs agree with it. Possible reasons, all guesses, untested:

  1. Population 30. Your own table has population 50 at 5% noise at 0.14 cooperation, handshake 0%, all-defect 80%.
  2. Mutation. A third of his population is new mutants each generation; your five-mutants row gives all-defect 67%. His footnote 7 (l. 351–352): with higher mutation "the machine Always-Defect is hugely favored."
  3. Length. 500 generations against your measured 28,000 to leave all-defect in a bed of 100. He starts from random machines, but says the early generations "tend to use machines that defect continuously" (l. 430–431).
  4. Doubled errors. With both kinds at rate r, what a machine registers differs from what was intended about 2r of the time: his 4% is near your 7.7%, where you too find the floor. This does not explain his 2%.
  5. Played-out payoffs add sampling noise to selection.

So his result and yours may be one map seen in two corners, and your README already says the handshake needs a large enough population, few mutants and strong selection. A run of your code at 30 machines, heavy mutation and 500 generations would test guesses 1 to 3.

Sentences of "Is this known?" to replace

  1. "Handshakes as such are not new ... some of them evolved 16-state machines." Too generous by omission. Replacement: The machine is not new. It is Fortress3 of Ashlock and Ashlock (2006), who found it and a four-state sibling "becoming important later" in evolving populations of automata, and it is among the 164 strategies of Knight and others (2017), who say Fortress3 and Fortress4 "use a similar handshake".
  2. "As I read a summary of that paper, they note ..." The three points are accurate; I checked them in the arXiv text (l. 2090, 2102, 2143–2144). Replacement: say you read the paper, and quote it.
  3. The van Veelen sentence stands (checked against the abstract only).
  4. "What may be a little different here ... so noise does not break it". Wrong as a difference. Replacement: A machine that defects until both sides have defected twice and returns there after any slip is the 2-period mutual punishment automaton of Iwasaki, Joe, Kandori, Obara and Yokoo (2012), designed for exactly this, and the two-round sibling of Pavlov; Binmore and Samuelson's Tat for Tit (1992) is the one-round version.
  5. "and here noise is what makes it stable at all (no strict equilibrium exists without it)." Not yours. Replacement: That nothing is strictly stable without errors is Boyd and Lorberbaum (1987); that errors make strict stability possible is Boyd (1989).
  6. "I would not be surprised if this exact machine is already in the literature under another name." Replacement: It is, under two names.

Elsewhere in the README, three lines need the same care: "not tit-for-tat, Pavlov or anything else in my textbook list"; "No strategy of any complexity" (add "that cannot see its own slips"); and "two relatives" (they are the same table entered at its other two states).
