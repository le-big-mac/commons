The question. The dial note ended with sixteen possible starts between 300 BC and 1 BC that fit the dial's marked months, and said that the published datings choose among them "with the hours inscribed on the glyphs, which I have not tested". Beside each glyph the dial gives an hour of the day or of the night. Set those hours against the real times of full and new moon at each start: which of the sixteen do they favour?

In short

  1. The hours cut the sixteen to four, and the four are neighbours. With the 18 hours that Carman and Evans kept, the starts of 223, 205, 187 and 169 BC, which are four consecutive eighteen-year cycles, fit the real full and new moons to 2.5 to 2.7 hours (root mean square). The other twelve fit to 4.5 to 6.6 hours, where a start taken at random gives 5.6. This is in Carman and Evans: the hours, they write, "may be used to restrict the solutions to a single Saros eclipse series", and at these four starts the solar eclipse of month 13 falls on consecutive members of the series they find (solar Saros series 44). Their Figure 17 shows the same valley.
  2. The hours cannot tell the four apart. The place can, if the hours are taken as engraved. Their scores are 2.70, 2.56, 2.52 and 2.63 h, and 187 BC is ahead of 205 BC by three hundredths of an hour, which means nothing. But the cycle is a third of a day longer than a whole number of days, so each cycle's hours fit at a longitude 115° further west than the last: 141° E, 26° E, 89° W, 156° E. With the hours as they are engraved, only 205 BC puts the dial in the Greek world. But the mechanism has a small Exeligmos dial that tells the user to add 8 or 16 hours in the cycles after the first, and with those added 187 BC fits at 31° E and 169 BC at 36° E, as the second and third cycles of a dial whose first is 205 BC (check-scripts/c6b_author_fix.out). So what the place fixes is the cycle the hours were written for. This is the published argument in another form: Carman and Evans call the step the "rule of 8 h", use it at a meridian 1 h 35 m east to rule out two cycles in every three, and conclude that the dial fits 205 BC "with the exeligmos dial set at 0"; Jones takes that setting as given. With the longitude kept between 10° and 40° east, 205 BC stays at 2.56 h and the next best of the sixteen is at 4.49.
  3. Taken alone the hours do not fix the start. Among all 3,711 lunar months that could be the dial's first, 54 fit the 18 hours better than 205 BC does, the best to 1.9 h, and only one of the 54 (187 BC) is among the sixteen that fit the months. They do narrow the field, to about one start in 37 (101 fit under 3.0 h), but not to a date. The hours follow the Moon's and the Sun's anomalies and know nothing of the nodes; the months follow the nodes and know nothing of the anomalies. It takes both, and a longitude, and that is how the papers argue: Carman and Evans use the Moon's anomaly to choose among starts already tied to the nodes, Freeth joins his model of the months to his model of the hours, and Jones selects on the node and the apogee before he tests the hours.
  4. The best fits have the Moon's anomaly a little later than it was in 205 BC. The thirty starts that fit under 2.3 h all have the Moon's anomaly between 0° and 25° further on than at the first full moon of 205 BC (12° on average), when the Moon was 7° short of apogee. The Sun's is less well fixed (5° back to 42° on, 20° on average). On average, then, the thirty have the Moon's anomaly at 185.4° and the Sun's at 181.5°, counted from perigee. That is Carman and Evans's own fit to the same 18 hours, 184.7° and 180.4° (their Table 12), where the sky of 205 BC had 173.3° and 161.5°; they do not claim the Moon's figure to better than 26°.
  5. The lunar hours fit much better than the solar ones: 1.3 h for the nine lunar hours of the 18 and 3.4 h for the nine solar, each at a longitude of its own, 28° E and 25° E. Carman and Evans found this: 1.32 h and 3.32 h, at one longitude and from the instants of greatest eclipse, and "the solar eclipse times are of lower quality".
  6. Another reading removes one of the four outliers, and worsens one of the 18. Carman and Evans had the sixth hour of the day for the lunar glyph of month 120 and noted that the reading was not certain. Freeth (2014) gave "12?" for it, and Iversen and Jones (2019) print the twelfth of the day with a query. At 205 BC and one longitude the difference for that glyph falls from 7.6 h to 1.6 h. Iversen and Jones also read the solar glyph of month 119 an hour later, which takes it from 5.3 h to 6.3 h. With all of their readings the picture is the same.

What was done

The hours. Carman and Evans (2014), Table 11: 22 glyphs with an hour, 12 lunar and 10 solar. I turned them into a 24-hour clock by their rule, which treats every day as an equinox: the first hour of the day is 7 h, the first hour of the night 19 h. They set four aside as outliers when fitting the Moon's and the Sun's anomaly to the hours alone, with no start date in the fit: 13S, 120M, 125M and 184M (a number is the month, M a lunar glyph, S a solar one). "The 18" are the rest. I sealed lines for the 18 and for all 22. Other editions read a few of the hours differently: Freeth (2014, Table S3) has 119S as the tenth of the night and 125M as the second of the day, and Jones (2020, Appendix 1) sets his own readings, Freeth's and Carman and Evans's side by side. Iversen and Jones's are tried below.

The real full and new moons. From Meeus's algorithm for the phases of the Moon, which I typed from memory and therefore tested first: against 940 full and 940 new moons found in JPL's tables for 205 to 129 BC it is 1.8 minutes early on average and never more than 2.5 minutes out (phases.py). Universal time is dynamical time less Espenak and Meeus's ΔT, also typed from memory; it gives 12,836 s for mid-205 BC where NASA's catalogue has 12,775, a minute's difference, which is a quarter of a degree of longitude. The reader took both checks across the three centuries (check-scripts/c3_phases.out). Against all 1,435 eclipses of the catalogue from 300 BC to 1 BC the algorithm's full and new moons stay within 19 minutes of the instants of greatest eclipse, with no drift from one half-century to the next, so there is no sign of a slip outside the years JPL's tables cover. The polynomial for ΔT runs 66 s above the catalogue's column in 300 BC and 49 s above it in 1 BC, which is the size of the correction the catalogue makes for the Moon's secular acceleration (0.0000129 s times the square of the years from 1955, as the reader remembers it): not a slip of memory, then, but a term left out.

The ruler (hours.py; tried on made-up dials first, hours.py trial; I kept no output of the trial, and the reader's rerun, which gives these numbers, is check-scripts/trial_rerun.out). For a start k, counted in lunar months from the full moon of 12 May 205 BC, month n of the dial holds full moon k + n and the new moon after it. Each hour is set against the local time of that full moon (a lunar glyph) or new moon (a solar one): UT plus the longitude over 15. The dial does not say where it was made for, so the longitude is free for each start. The score of a start is the root-mean-square difference in hours, each difference taken between −12 and +12, at the longitude that makes it least. In the trial a planted start was found when the hours had no noise; with 2.5 h of noise it came 2nd to 16th of 3,711, and starts 767 months from it scored as well.

The sixteen are those of ../dial/, for Freeth's list of months: 299, 223, 205, 187, 183, 169, 165, 147, 129, 111, 93, 89, 71, 53, 35 and 17 BC.

What this is and is not

  • It is a replication, and most of what it finds is in Carman and Evans. They fit the Moon's and the Sun's equations to the hours and use the fitted anomaly of the Moon to pick the Saros series (their sections 13 and 15). Then they make the comparison made here. They set the 18 hours against the catalogue's times of the eclipses, or of true full and new moon where there was no eclipse, at a meridian 1 h 35 m east, and sum the squared differences for each start of the series; their Figure 17 is the valley of my whole cycles, and among the one start in three that the rule of 8 h leaves they find the lowest at 205 BC (sections 16 and 17). In section 21 they do the same for every candidate eclipse of ten Saros series, with 0, 8 and 16 hours added (Figure 30). Points 1, 2 and 5 above are in those sections, point 4 is their Table 12, and the choice between 259, 205 and 151 BC is their section 22.1. Jones picks out starts by the node and the apogee, by the Almagest's theory, and then sets the hours against computed full and new moons cycle by cycle, two hours east of Greenwich. Freeth builds a model of how the hours were made and takes the anomalies from its best fit. What is mine is the longitude left free at every start (Carman and Evans fit one at 205 BC, in their section 20, and compare the starts at a fixed meridian), every one of the 3,711 months tried as a start, and the count in point 3. All four routes give 205 BC, and none does it from the hours alone: each needs the nodes, and all but Freeth's a place.
  • "In the Greek world" is my line, drawn after the run. 10° to 40° east runs roughly from Carthage to the upper Euphrates (places from memory). The sealed lines do not use it. With it, 205 BC is the only one of the sixteen under 4.4 h. Three things qualify that. The line is close to Carman and Evans's 95% interval for the longitude of best fit, 7.8° to 42.8° E, and they say of it that "no part of the Greek world of the eastern Mediterranean may be safely ruled out on the basis of the eclipse times alone". It holds for the hours as engraved: with the Exeligmos dial's 8 or 16 hours added, 223, 187 and 169 BC fit at 21°, 31° and 36° E. And it leans on the sixteen: 259 BC, which the dial note leaves out by one solar month, fits at 10° E with the 18 hours (3.20 h) and at 20° E with the 22 (3.67 h, where 205 BC has 3.91).
  • The longitude is good to about nine degrees. A scatter of 2.56 h over 18 hours leaves their mean uncertain by 0.6 h, which is 9°. Carman and Evans give ±8.7° at 67% and ±17.5° at 95% about their 25.3° E, and sum it up as "the Aegean Sea with an uncertainty of about an hour of longitude". Beyond that the longitude carries any error in ΔT (a minute of time is a quarter of a degree; the uncertainty of ΔT for that century is a few minutes, as I remember the tables), the choice of tables (the full moons Jones computed run 17 minutes, 4°, earlier than JPL's with the catalogue's ΔT; check-scripts/c2b_jones_table1.out) and any offset in how the hours were reckoned. My own longitudes are 0.7° too far east by JPL's tables and the catalogue's ΔT, which put 205 BC at 25.5° E: 1.8 minutes from Meeus's algorithm and a minute from ΔT (check-scripts/c2_ruler.out).
  • The hours are treated as equinoctial, by Carman and Evans's rule. If they were seasonal hours the differences would change by up to an hour or so at the solstices, as they say.
  • Full and new moons are not eclipses. The middle of an eclipse differs from the instant of full or new moon by up to about a third of an hour in the catalogue rows I matched in ../dial/, and for a solar eclipse seen from one place by much more. I did not model that. Carman and Evans use the instant of greatest eclipse where there was one; over the 18 their differences and those from JPL's full and new moons agree to 0.12 h (root mean square) once the longitude is taken out (check-scripts/c2_ruler.out).
  • Point 4 is an observation and not a finding about the maker. That the best fits have the Moon a little past where it was says the hours were not computed from the true anomalies of 205 BC to the degree; Carman and Evans, Freeth and Jones each argue that the start was chosen with the Moon at apogee at the first full moon (Carman and Evans credit the idea to Jones and add the Sun at its own apogee); I have not tested that. The 12° is well inside what they allow: Carman and Evans do not claim the Moon's anomaly from the hours to better than 26°, and Jones allows 15°.
  • What I did not find in print is the count of how many starts fit the hours as well with the months left out (point 3). That is not a claim of novelty: when the first reader read this I had made no search for it beyond the papers named here, and the six searches made since, which are in the next point, are not a survey. The nearest thing in them is Carman and Evans's Figure 30, which tries every candidate eclipse of ten Saros series as the eclipse of month 13, with 0, 8 and 16 hours added; all of those are starts that the nodes allow. The reader searched the texts of the four papers for the return after 767 months and did not find it; they had no network and could not look further either.
  • Searches, added after the reading. Six searches of the web on the evening of 25 November, for the dial's hours set against real eclipses or full and new moons, for a longitude fitted to them, for every month tried as a start, for the 767 months, and for work since 2020 that disputes 205 BC. They turned up the four papers used here and nothing that tries every month as a start or counts the starts that fit the hours alone. The search for the 767 months found nothing on them; the search for a fitted longitude found only Carman and Evans's; and the later paper of Voulgaris and others (2024) came up in the last of the six. That is six searches and not a survey; a paper I did not find may well have it.
  • Another date has been proposed, and the engraved hours do not match the real full and new moons that follow it (added after the reading; after_check.out). Voulgaris, Mouratidis and Vossinakis argue that the pointers were first set for 22 and 23 December 178 BC: on the 22nd there was a new moon, with an annular eclipse of the Sun and the Moon near apogee, and the 23rd they take as the winter solstice. They number the cells one lower than everyone else (see the dial note) and put that eclipse at the end of their first cell, which is everyone else's second. In the usual numbering, then, month 1 begins at the new moon of October 178 BC, which is the first of the four starts below; the other three are the months after it, tried in case their numbering is read another way. The four starts about that date, with month 1 beginning at the new moons of October, November and December 178 BC and January 177 BC, score 5.78, 5.67, 5.41 and 5.56 h with the 18 hours, each at its own best longitude: what a start taken at random gives (the median of all 3,711 is 5.58). With all 22 hours they score 6.03, 6.70, 6.12 and 5.86 h, where the median is 5.89, and adding the Exeligmos dial's 8 or 16 hours moves the longitudes and leaves the scores as they are. In the terms of point 4, at the first of the four the Moon's mean anomaly at the first full moon is 138° further on than in 205 BC and the Sun's 176°, where the thirty starts that fit the hours best have 0° to 25° and 5° back to 42° on. The first paper also allows that the maker began one or two Saros cycles, or one or two Metonic cycles of 235 months, after that date; the four starts moved on by each of those score 5.34 to 6.64 h with the 18 hours. (The 22-hour scores are in after_check.out; the rest of this is the second reader's, from this folder's code, check-2-scripts/s2_with_author_code.out; the four scores above also came out the same from JPL's tables and the catalogue's ΔT by code of their own, check-2-scripts/s1_date178.out.) I have read their abstract, their section 3.2 and the passages of their paper and of a later one of theirs (2024) that a search of the text for "hour" turns up, and found no comparison of the engraved hours with real times in either; I have not read the rest. The second reader read both papers through and found no such comparison either. The first paper lists the engraved hours of five cells (its Table 2) and says that the eclipse of 22 December 178 BC began "close to the 1st hour" of the day; it sets no engraved hour against a real time. The later paper says in its introduction that the mechanism "predicted upcoming solar and lunar eclipses with date and hour accuracy", and in its abstract that the eclipses on the dial "are calculated by pure mechanical processing" and "are not documented observed events": on its account the maker set the pointers, turned the mechanism on, and engraved a glyph and "the events' predicted hour" wherever the pointers showed an eclipse. For the hours it refers to a third paper of theirs ("Reconstructing the Antikythera Mechanism lost eclipse events applying the Draconic gearing", 2023, pages 22 to 25), which neither of us has read. On that account the hours are the mechanism's own predictions and not times taken from the sky. What is shown here is only that the engraved hours are no nearer the real full and new moons after December 178 BC than after a month taken at random; whether they agree with the authors' account of how the hours were made is not tested.
  • Slips in the sealed files, left as they are (added after the reading; the reader found them). hours.py is sealed, so it is not edited: a line it prints takes August 190 BC as the year −189.4 where −188.4 is meant (a printed comparison only; no score uses it), and its opening comment offers a mode real that does not exist (the real run is hours_run.py). On the sealed sheet, 2.53 h should be 2.52, and its 10.7° for the Sun in a Saros is for a year of 365.25 days; the Sun's anomaly moves 10.5°, as hours_after.out prints.
