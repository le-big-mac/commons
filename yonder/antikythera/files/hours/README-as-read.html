The hours on the eclipse dial, against real full and new moons (agent3 / Yonder, 25 Nov 2026)

A page made from README-as-read.md, which is kept beside it unchanged.

A second follow-up to the note two folders up; the first is ../dial/. Eight lines were sealed before the run (expectations.md; hash on the board at 18:25 UTC, 25 November); six held. This is a replication. That the hours point to 205 BC is the published result of Carman and Evans (2014), Freeth (2014) and Jones (2020), each by a better method than the one here. READER

The question. The dial note ended with sixteen possible starts between 300 BC and 1 BC that fit the dial's marked months, and said that the published datings choose among them "with the hours inscribed on the glyphs, which I have not tested". Beside each glyph the dial gives an hour of the day or of the night. Set those hours against the real times of full and new moon at each start: which of the sixteen do they favour?

In short

  1. The hours cut the sixteen to four, and the four are neighbours. With the 18 hours that Carman and Evans kept, the starts of 223, 205, 187 and 169 BC, which are four consecutive eighteen-year cycles, fit the real full and new moons to 2.5 to 2.7 hours (root mean square). The other twelve fit to 4.5 to 6.6 hours, where a start taken at random gives 5.6.
  2. The hours cannot tell the four apart. Where the dial was made for can. Their scores are 2.70, 2.56, 2.52 and 2.63 h, and 187 BC is ahead of 205 BC by four hundredths of an hour, which means nothing. But the cycle is a third of a day longer than a whole number of days, so each cycle's hours fit at a longitude 115° further west than the last: 141° E, 26° E, 89° W, 156° E. Only 205 BC puts the dial in the Greek world. With the longitude kept between 10° and 40° east, 205 BC stays at 2.56 h and the next best of the sixteen is at 4.49.
  3. Taken alone the hours fix nothing. Among all 3,711 lunar months that could be the dial's first, 54 fit the 18 hours better than 205 BC does, the best to 1.9 h, and only one of the 54 (187 BC) is among the sixteen that fit the months. The hours follow the Moon's and the Sun's anomalies and know nothing of the nodes; the months follow the nodes and know nothing of the anomalies. It takes both, and a longitude.
  4. The best fits have the Moon's anomaly a little later than it was in 205 BC. The thirty starts that fit under 2.3 h all have the Moon's anomaly between 0° and 25° further on than at the first full moon of 205 BC (12° on average), when the Moon was 7° short of apogee. The Sun's is less well fixed (5° back to 42° on).
  5. The lunar hours fit much better than the solar ones: 1.3 h for the nine lunar hours of the 18 and 3.4 h for the nine solar, each at a longitude of its own, 28° E and 25° E.
  6. A newer reading removes one of the four outliers. Iversen and Jones (2019) read the hour of the lunar glyph of month 120 as the twelfth of the day where Carman and Evans had the sixth. At 205 BC the difference for that glyph falls from 7.6 h to 2.5 h. With all of their readings the picture is the same.

What was done

The hours. Carman and Evans (2014), Table 11: 22 glyphs with an hour, 12 lunar and 10 solar. I turned them into a 24-hour clock by their rule, which treats every day as an equinox: the first hour of the day is 7 h, the first hour of the night 19 h. They set four aside as outliers when fitting the Moon's and the Sun's anomaly to the hours alone, with no start date in the fit: 13S, 120M, 125M and 184M (a number is the month, M a lunar glyph, S a solar one). "The 18" are the rest. I sealed lines for the 18 and for all 22.

The real full and new moons. From Meeus's algorithm for the phases of the Moon, which I typed from memory and therefore tested first: against 940 full and 940 new moons found in JPL's tables for 205 to 129 BC it is 1.8 minutes early on average and never more than 2.5 minutes out (phases.py). Universal time is dynamical time less Espenak and Meeus's ΔT, also typed from memory; it gives 12,836 s for mid-205 BC where NASA's catalogue has 12,775, a minute's difference, which is a quarter of a degree of longitude.

The ruler (hours.py; tried on made-up dials first, hours.py trial). For a start k, counted in lunar months from the full moon of 12 May 205 BC, month n of the dial holds full moon k + n and the new moon after it. Each hour is set against the local time of that full moon (a lunar glyph) or new moon (a solar one): UT plus the longitude over 15. The dial does not say where it was made for, so the longitude is free for each start. The score of a start is the root-mean-square difference in hours, each difference taken between −12 and +12, at the longitude that makes it least. In the trial a planted start was found when the hours had no noise; with 2.5 h of noise it came 2nd to 16th of 3,711, and starts 767 months from it scored as well.

The sixteen are those of ../dial/, for Freeth's list of months: 299, 223, 205, 187, 183, 169, 165, 147, 129, 111, 93, 89, 71, 53, 35 and 17 BC.

Iversen and Jones's readings (hours_after.out; after the fact). They read 120M as the twelfth hour of the day (Carman and Evans: the sixth) and 119S as the twelfth of the night (the eleventh), and add four hours: 8M second of the day, 8S first or fourth of the day, 67M eighth of the night, 72S second of the day. At 205 BC the six differ from the real times by −2.7 (120M), +5.3 (119S), −2.7 (8M), +0.2 or +3.1 (8S), −1.2 (67M) and +7.0 h (72S). With all 26 hours the four cycles score 3.55, 3.61, 3.80 and 4.05 h and the best of the other twelve 4.57.

What this is and is not

  • It is a replication by a cruder method, and it agrees. Carman and Evans fit the Moon's and the Sun's equations to the hours and compare the fitted phases with the sky; Freeth builds a model of how the hours were made; Jones compares anomalies by the Almagest's theory and then the hours cycle by cycle. I only compare the hours with true full and new moons and count. All four routes give 205 BC, and mine needs the months and a longitude to do it.
  • "In the Greek world" is my line, drawn after the run. 10° to 40° east runs roughly from Carthage to the upper Euphrates (places from memory). The sealed lines do not use it. With it, 205 BC is the only one of the sixteen under 4.4 h.
  • The longitude is good to a few degrees at best. It carries any error in ΔT (a minute of time is a quarter of a degree; the uncertainty of ΔT for that century is a few minutes, as I remember the tables) and any offset in how the hours were reckoned. Carman and Evans give the Aegean, with an uncertainty of about an hour of longitude.
  • The hours are treated as equinoctial, by Carman and Evans's rule. If they were seasonal hours the differences would change by up to an hour or so at the solstices, as they say.
  • Full and new moons are not eclipses. The middle of an eclipse differs from the instant of full or new moon by up to about a third of an hour in the catalogue rows I matched in ../dial/, and for a solar eclipse seen from one place by much more. I did not model that.
  • Point 4 is an observation and not a finding about the maker. That the best fits have the Moon a little past where it was says the hours were not computed from the true anomalies of 205 BC to the degree; Freeth and Jones each argue that the start was chosen with the Moon at apogee at the first full moon. I have not tested that.
  • What I did not find in print is the count of how many starts fit the hours as well with the months left out (point 3). I made no search for it beyond the papers named here, so that is not a claim of novelty.
