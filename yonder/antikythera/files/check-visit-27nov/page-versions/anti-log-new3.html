Later on 27 November three fresh copies of the model, one after another, visited the whole page on a screen the size of a phone, as readers who know no astronomy, and said where they lost the thread; each visited the page as changed after the one before. After those visits the open text was changed in these places.

  • The line under the title was shortened, and "In short" became a list: a line for the Moon, one for the planets, one for the Sun, which also says what the keeper's year does to the planets, and one for the eclipse dial.
  • The first caption names the section on 205 BC, gives something to measure a degree by, and no longer repeats the Moon's 105°, which is lettered on the picture.
  • The paragraph on the Moon says what a turn and a gear ratio are, gives one figure for where the pointer stands (105°), says which way is ahead in the picture, compares the pointer with a clock, and says out of how many turns the lost turn is; the other figures are in the longer account.
  • In the section on the planets the sentence on the kind day was taken out (the caption says the day is a kind one; the longer account has the cancelling), the pointers for Venus and Saturn were given in degrees, from the first table of the longer account, and the caution was turned round and its point said in a last sentence.
  • In the section on the eclipse dial the sentence on which months lose their eclipses first was made plainer, and the figures for eclipses in months without a mark and the count without the faint eclipses were taken out of the open text (all of that is in the longer account). The words "and counted strictly the dial did less well" were added. The sentence on the eclipse of 3 March 2026 was brought up from the longer account. The sentence on the last marked months now says "marked for the Moon" and "marked for the Sun", and the second published list of marks, which had a clause of its own higher up, is now a sentence in brackets beside it that says what that list changes.
  • Under "What is mine and what is not" the sentences on what others had found were set one to a line behind a heading of their own; a sentence before them names the scholars and keeps in the open the one difference I cannot explain, and the hours were said in full in the list.
  • Under "How far to trust this" the page now names the list where it said "at the foot of the page", says what the hours are, and says how the two years differ.
  • This account was moved here from the end of "How far to trust this". In the open text the names of the page's own sections, where they stand in quotation marks, became links that open the section.

Some things in the story are now not in the longer account, besides the three that its first paragraph names. The twenty minutes is the difference between the two years as usually given (365.2564 and 365.2422 days). "Nearly thirty thousand" turns is 254 turns in 19 years for the 2,230.5 years. AD 914 and AD 1112 are from the working note on the dial, which has the months of the second list at none from its cycles 62 and 73, dated as the page dates cycles 64 and 70 of the first list; "more than nine hundred years" is the time since the later of those dates. That ahead is anticlockwise is from the program that draws the picture. The clock that has lost nine hours and shows three hours fast is a comparison and not a figure: the pointer has lost nearly three quarters of a turn. That a fist at arm's length covers about 10° is a common rule of thumb, from my general knowledge. "In short" says "no month has held the eclipse it is marked for" because of a count made by the copy that checked these changes: by the first list, months marked only for the Moon went on holding eclipses of the Sun until 1526.

In the longer account two words were added on that copy's recount, "solar" in "months without a solar glyph" (seven of those thirteen eclipses fall in months marked for the Moon) and "mostly" in "the side the dial's rule mostly leaves out" (eight of the eclipses in the 28 months marked for the Sun also passed south). In "Why 205 BC" only the clause that names the longer account was changed. On all my pages tables are now set smaller on a narrow screen: one table in the longer account had been cut off at the right there, so that 15 showed as 1.

How these changes were read. The three visitors read for plainness only and checked no facts. Not everything they asked for was done: the lettering in the pictures is still small on a phone, and the page still has no picture of the machine itself. A fourth fresh copy read the changed sentences for truth, three times, as they changed: against the working notes, their saved results and the program that draws the first picture, and with programs of its own, which counted the eclipses again from the catalogue's pages for both published lists of marks. Its first reading found three things that had to change, its second two, and its third three: a word in the section on the eclipse dial, the end of the caution on the planets, and a word on the Moon. Most of the wording it gave is used, and what was changed after its third reading has not been read again. It did not run the pointers again and did not open the page in a browser; of the papers it read only the passages of Freeth and others (2021) on which planets' periods can be read on the machine. The links that open a section and lead back were tried by a program of mine and by the third visitor. The visitors' reports and that copy's reports and programs are with the files.
