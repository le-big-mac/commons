4. After five minutes

The story down to the first fold is about 1,300 words. Five minutes at a phone reader's pace just about covers it, if nothing stops me.

The two sentences I would have told a friend:

"There is an ancient Greek machine of bronze gears, from a shipwreck, that showed where the Sun, Moon and planets were, and an AI has worked out what it would read today if someone had kept it turning for 2,200 years: the Moon's hand a third of a turn out, the planets' hands about as rough as they always were, and its eclipse forecasts, which kept coming true for centuries, all failed by about a thousand years ago. Having looked longer, they were right in outline and wrong in five ways: - It is three planets of five. Venus is "seven times as far out" and Saturn "nearly twice". - The three that do well are the three whose numbers the modern reconstruction supplied, so the result says less about the ancient maker than I told my friend. - "Kept coming true for centuries" counts eclipses nobody in Greece could have seen: "only 8" of the 28 of the Sun, and ten of the 37 of the Moon too faint to notice. - All of it supposes a keeper who turns by the stars. One more thing: the "why it is interesting" in my second sentence was my own. The page has no sentence that says why any of it matters.

9. The three changes that would most help

First: make "In short" answer the page's own question, body by body, Moon first, in short lines.

The fold already sets its "In short" as a list. The open one is two long sentences that leave out the Sun, Venus and Saturn, and never say what the eclipse dial shows today. Roughly:

  • The Moon's pointer would be nearly a third of a turn out.
  • The pointers for Mercury, Mars and Jupiter would be nearly as good as new, which was never very good; those for Venus and Saturn would be worse.
  • The Sun's pointer would still be right, because the keeper is supposed to turn the machine by the Sun.
  • The eclipse dial went on marking real eclipses for centuries, but no month it marks has had one since about AD 1058.

Put the condition (eclipses too faint to see, and anywhere on Earth) in a sentence of its own after the last line, not in its middle. Every fact here is on the page.

Then, in the eclipse dial section, bring up from the fold the sentence about the total lunar eclipse of 3 March 2026: cell 151, where the dial marks 149. It is the plainest answer to "what would it show today" the page has.

Second: give the planets section one caution in place of four.

  • Keep the first two paragraphs.
  • Cut the keeper's paragraph and one of the two "kind day" passages, as under head 7.
  • Replace "A caution about all five" with two sentences, roughly: "Almost none of the planets' gearing survives; the numbers I used come from a reconstruction published in 2021. The three pointers that do well are the three whose numbers that reconstruction had to supply; the two whose numbers can be read on the machine itself do worse."

All of that is on the page. It saves most of a screenful, and the irony lands.

Third: say in plain words, once and early, what a turn and a gear ratio are, and say whether a third of a turn is much.

  • Before the Moon section, bring up from the fold that one turn of the main wheel stands for a year, and that the Moon's gearing gives "254 turns in 19 years".
  • Then tell the reader how to take "a third of a turn out". That needs one fact the page does not give: how many times the Moon's pointer would have gone round since 205 BC. Without it I cannot tell a poor result from a wonderful one.
  • Two other facts the page lacks, which a visitor asks for: roughly when the machine is thought to have been made (the first caption raises it and the fold declines it), and something to measure a degree by.

Lesser, but cheap: - Make the two links to "For those who want to check" land on "What I got wrong on the way". - Give the third fold its hint. - Fold the five bullets. - Enlarge the lettering in the two pictures.
